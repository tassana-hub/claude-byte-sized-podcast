Starting with Japan and Asia-Pacific. Japan's parliament has passed amendments to the Financial Instruments and Exchange Act that reclassify crypto as a financial product, putting it in the same regulatory bucket as stocks and bonds rather than treating it as a payment method. The reform brings insider trading rules, mandatory disclosures for issuers and prison terms of up to ten years for unregistered operators, alongside a flat tax rate on crypto gains of around twenty percent, down from as high as fifty five percent under the old rules. It takes effect around January twenty twenty eight, and the Japan Exchange Group is already eyeing domestic spot crypto exchange traded funds as early as twenty twenty seven, a sign Tokyo wants to be seen as a serious, well regulated home for digital assets rather than a cautionary tale.

Over in India, two stories point the same way: state governments are competing hard for capital. Gujarat has unveiled a data centre policy for the next three years targeting sixty two billion, seven hundred seventy million dollars in investment and seven and a half gigawatts of capacity, while Uttar Pradesh has approved its own new startup policy alongside a matching data centre policy of its own. It is the kind of state-by-state industrial policy race that rarely makes international headlines but shapes where the next wave of Asian data infrastructure actually gets built.

And in Washington, a new analysis from CSIS's India chair argues that the long-stalled first tranche of the U.S.-India bilateral trade agreement could finally move, once two ongoing American trade investigations wrap up and a ten percent global tariff imposed by the Trump administration expires on the twenty fourth of July. The piece is candid that the relationship has more often suffered from too many overlapping dialogue channels than too few, and argues this pause is a chance to simplify them rather than simply resume business as usual.

Now, over to A.I. and tech, where the biggest release of the week comes from Thinking Machines, the lab founded by former OpenAI chief technology officer Mira Murati. It has published its first open-weight model, called Inkling, a mixture of experts system with nine hundred seventy five billion total parameters, forty one billion of them active at any one time, multimodal reasoning across text, images and audio, and a context window of a million tokens. Releasing a model of that scale as open weights, rather than keeping it behind an API, is a statement in itself about how the newer labs plan to compete with the incumbents.

OpenAI, meanwhile, has shipped its first piece of branded hardware, and it is deliberately small. Codex Micro is a two hundred thirty dollar mechanical keypad built with a company called Work Louder, designed to let developers steer coding agents with physical keys rather than a keyboard shortcut, complete with a joystick for switching between tasks like code review and debugging and a dial for adjusting how much the model reasons before it acts. It is not the consumer smart speaker that OpenAI's hardware chief, former Apple design lead Jony Ive, is reportedly building, but arriving the same week Apple sued OpenAI over trade secrets tied to that unreleased device, the timing was hard to miss.

Elsewhere, the workflow orchestration company Prefect is acquiring rival Dagster, absorbing around forty staff while promising to keep Dagster's name, pricing and product roadmap intact for now, another sign that the crowded data pipeline tooling market is starting to consolidate. And a growing theme across this week's developer newsletters is what people are calling self-improving harnesses: frameworks that let an AI agent analyse its own execution traces and rewrite the scaffolding it operates inside. One widely shared experiment ran a coding agent on itself for eight days and came away with a smaller, faster prompt and a system that had quietly out-performed a harness engineers had spent two years hand-tuning, which is either an efficiency breakthrough or a reason to pay closer attention to what these systems are doing unsupervised, depending on your mood. In a related vein, Perplexity has detailed a sandbox platform called Space, which spins up an isolated, disposable environment for every task an AI agent handles and destroys it afterwards, isolating credentials and encrypting storage along the way, the kind of unglamorous infrastructure that becomes essential the moment agents are trusted with anything sensitive.

Now, over to startups and venture, where European robotics had a genuinely big day. Aramco Ventures, the venture arm of the Saudi oil and gas giant, is in talks to back the London humanoid robotics startup Humanoid in a deal that could value the company at around one point one to one point two billion dollars on roughly two hundred million dollars raised, while Amsterdam-based Monumental, which builds robots for construction sites, closed a thirty two million dollar Series B led by Khosla Ventures. European investors have already ploughed two point seven billion euros into robotics this year against one and a half billion for the whole of last year, and founders in the space are increasingly framing robotics, rather than software, as the continent's best shot at building globally relevant technology companies.

Lenny's Newsletter flagged a compensation trend worth knowing if you are hiring or being hired at that level: so-called builder executives, the operators who can still personally ship product, are starting to get paid like professional athletes, with pay packages that reward hands-on building ability rather than pure management scope for the first time in a long while.

And on the bootstrapped end of the spectrum, Indie Hackers profiled Saeed Ezzati, who built a Chrome extension called Superpower ChatGPT in a couple of days during the first week ChatGPT launched because he found the native interface clunky. He kept it free for nine months, funded it through a companion newsletter, and now runs it solo to four hundred twenty thousand downloads, one hundred fifty thousand weekly active users and five figures in monthly recurring revenue, a useful reminder that some of the best founder stories are still just one person fixing their own problem in public.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics, where CSIS trade expert Bill Reinsch has a sharp column on what he calls the trade transparency challenge. As supply chains have become genuinely global, with components sourced from dozens of countries before final assembly somewhere else entirely, determining a product's true country of origin has become far harder just as governments are demanding far more of that information for security reasons. His conclusion is blunt: more due diligence for manufacturers and customs brokers, more contested origin disputes for lawyers to argue over, and ultimately higher prices for consumers.

And on Ukraine, CSIS's Futures Lab frames the war as a race between two competing theories of victory. Kyiv is betting on maximising drone production and cheap, high-payoff deep strikes, backed by AI and integrated with cyber and electronic warfare, aimed at Russia's oil and gas revenue. Moscow is betting on a punishment campaign against critical infrastructure and political centres like Kyiv itself, hoping to force a settlement on its own terms. The analysts argue that whichever deep strike campaign proves more sustainable over the next six months, not the static front line, is now the most likely thing to actually end the war, which is a useful corrective for anyone still picturing this conflict mainly in terms of trenches and territory.

Now, over to community, events and marketing, where the idea worth stealing is about how founders run board meetings. The newsletter The VC Corner argues that most founders waste their board's time walking through thirty slides live, when the better approach is sending a tight packet four to five days ahead of the meeting so nobody is surprised by the numbers, then spending the actual hour on the two or three decisions only the board can make. It is a small operational shift, but one that changes a board meeting from a status update people endure into a working session people actually value.

And Marketing Brew looked at how the crisps brand Lay's approached World Cup marketing this summer, leaning into bandwagon fandom rather than pretending to have deep footballing credibility. Instead of chasing authenticity it cannot claim, the brand built campaigns around the casual, join-in-for-the-spectacle fans who only pay attention once every four years, a reminder that leaning into what your audience actually is can beat pretending to be something you are not.

Finally, one interesting thing to take you into the day. The newsletter Every has a timely piece questioning one of this year's more fashionable AI habits: building ever larger libraries of custom skills, the reusable instruction packages that load automatically when an AI assistant thinks they are relevant. On parts of the internet, a sprawling skill library has become something people show off, but the piece argues that stacking in skills indiscriminately can just as easily clutter an agent's context and make its answers worse, not better. The lesson, as with most tools, is that skills only help when you reach for them deliberately, not simply because you can.
