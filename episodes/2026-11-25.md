Section one, Japan and Asia Pacific.

A quick note first: there was no Japan-specific story in today's newsletters, so this section looks across the wider region.

First, Australia moves to replace China in the Solomon Islands. Canberra is reportedly offering the Solomon Islands about six hundred and sixty-seven million dollars under a sweeping new security treaty, due to be signed today. It would replace the contentious security pact the Solomons signed with Beijing in twenty twenty-two, which alarmed Western capitals and set off the current race for influence in the Pacific. If it goes through, it is a clear strategic win for Australia, and a sign that the Pacific contest is now being fought with large cheques rather than speeches. Via International Intrigue.

Second, A I agents are now running real attacks in East Asia. Security researchers describe a China-linked group that pointed autonomous A I agents at East Asian universities for about forty-eight hours. The agents ran nearly a hundred and fifty actions against an exposed database server, tried more than thirty thousand password combinations on a G P U server, then found a flaw in an open-source medical imaging application and took full control of the machine within eleven minutes. In the same newsletter, the United States Justice Department moved to seize about one point one seven billion dollars in stablecoins linked to North Korea's Lazarus Group and October's six billion dollar Binance hack. For anyone running university or hospital infrastructure in the region, the lesson is that the time between a weakness being found and being used is collapsing. Via T L D R Information Security.

And third, Indonesia's president goes into bat for his stock market. Prabowo Subianto held direct talks, reportedly brokered by the American billionaire Ray Dalio, with the index provider M S C I, to try to head off a damaging downgrade of Indonesia's market status. A downgrade would force index-tracking funds to sell Indonesian shares. It is unusual for a head of state to negotiate with an index company personally, and it shows how much emerging Asian economies now depend on passive global capital. Via International Intrigue.

Now, over to A I and tech.

Section two, A I and tech.

First, the Nvidia story takes another turn. We covered Jensen Huang's reinstatement yesterday; the new detail is how it happened and what comes with it. President Trump says he personally lobbied Nvidia's board, which voted unanimously to bring Huang back just eleven days after he resigned, and the shares jumped more than thirty per cent, the largest single-day gain in value for any stock in history. Nvidia says it will set up a national security and export control committee and appoint a chief compliance officer, while independent directors keep investigating claims that Huang was targeted by foreign intelligence. Huang still faces a House hearing on the first of December, and the Democratic leader Hakeem Jeffries has called the decision indefensible. Benedict Evans, writing just before the reversal, put the governance problem bluntly: the board was weighing a founder the company was built around against a record of affairs, a customer linked to chip smuggling, and repeated dishonesty to the board itself. Via The Rundown A I, T L D R A I and Benedict Evans.

Second, Meta's A I chief says superintelligence is arriving. Alexandr Wang published an essay arguing that the industry needs to take, in his words, meaningfully different actions from the path it is on. He pointed to Claude's role in the Riemann hypothesis proof as evidence that A I could soon speed up almost every field beyond what humans can follow, and said society should not accept a handover to A I. That is a striking shift in tone from Meta, whose boss Mark Zuckerberg has usually brushed off calls to slow down. Benedict Evans adds the sharper question: if machines produce proofs that are correct but run to a quarter of a million pages, what is that knowledge actually for if no one can understand it? Via The Rundown A I and Benedict Evans.

Third, coding agents move from writing code to shipping it. Anthropic has launched Claude Ship, a set of agents that review pull requests, push fixes, and check changes against a team's own approval rules before anything is merged. It is available on Team and Enterprise plans for code held on GitHub. Separately, Slack has started offering its Claude-powered Slackbot at a flat price per person rather than per use, pitching it as a way to stop companies rationing A I because they fear surprise bills. Both point the same way: the business model is shifting from selling a model to selling finished work at a predictable price. Via T L D R Founders and T L D R I T.

And fourth, OpenAI is paying local people to teach small businesses. Its new pilot, OpenWorks, pays tech-savvy people up to eight thousand dollars a month to sit with small business owners in twenty American cities, mostly in the Midwest and South, and help them use A I on a problem the owner chooses. Businesses get three months of ChatGPT Business free, and venture-backed and private equity-owned firms are excluded. It is a clever bit of distribution: a paid route into A I consulting for the helpers, and a pipeline of new business customers for OpenAI. Via The Rundown A I.

Section three, startups and venture.

First, the security industry consolidates in a very big way. CrowdStrike has agreed to buy Zscaler in an all-stock deal worth about forty-four billion dollars, a premium of roughly a third. Combining endpoint protection with Zscaler's zero-trust networking gives a business with more than ten billion dollars of annual recurring revenue, and CrowdStrike is pitching it as the control layer for securing A I agents. It comes as security firms report being so overwhelmed by A I-driven attacks that some are turning away new clients. Via T L D R I T and T L D R Information Security.

Second, Amazon buys an A I efficiency team for seven and a half billion dollars. Amazon has acquired the technology behind Decart's optimisation software, along with its founders and research team. The software helps A I models run efficiently on Nvidia, Google and Amazon's own chips, and the deal lands as Amazon prepares to sell its Trainium chips more widely. Cheaper compute means cheaper training and faster answers, and that is exactly where Nvidia's rivals need to close the gap. Via T L D R A I.

And third, Europe's fintech funding is splitting in two. Sifted data shows Series B and C fintech deals fell forty-eight per cent between twenty twenty-five and this year, while the average round size rose by almost half: fewer companies, bigger cheques. Spend management firm Payhawk raised two hundred and eighty million dollars despite the downturn, and London's Model M L, which builds A I for financial services, raised a two hundred and fifty million dollar Series B led by General Atlantic and Q E D. With Revolut not expected to list until twenty twenty-eight, early stage is where the energy is, and deals for A I-native fintechs jumped nearly three hundred per cent in the first half. Via Sifted.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

Section four, policy and geopolitics.

First, Washington goes after remote access to A I chips. C S I S analysts unpack the Commerce Department's new rule, which formally replaces the Biden-era A I diffusion framework. Instead of global country-by-country quotas, it requires licences for a group of high-risk countries, plus a worldwide licence whenever companies headquartered in China or other arms-embargoed countries rent serious computing power from data centres anywhere. The catch is that exporters must now gather enough information from foreign data centres to prove they have checked who their customers are, without being told what enough means. Expect over-compliance, and nervousness among cloud and data centre operators across Asia. Via C S I S.

Second, the real question behind the Nvidia saga: who sets American tech policy? International Intrigue argues the episode is a proxy fight between Washington's growth-at-all-costs faction and its national security hawks. On the same day Huang returned, the government finally blacklisted Megaspeed, the firm accused of channelling Nvidia computing power to China, and Senator Tom Cotton is now promising both an investigation and a tougher chip export bill. With Democrats taking control of Congress in January, anyone dealing with American export controls should expect more volatility, not less. Via International Intrigue.

And third, two quieter moves worth knowing. The United States Trade Representative has extended all one hundred and seventy-eight existing exclusions from tariffs on Chinese imports until the ninth of January, citing ongoing talks with Beijing. And the White House A I task force has recommended limiting regulatory liability for companies hit by A I-driven cyberattacks, with at least twelve months' grace for firms that report problems in good faith. Both are signs of a government trying to manage disruption rather than add to it. Via C S I S.

Now, over to community, events and marketing.

Section five, community, events and marketing.

One idea worth stealing: build communities of commitment. David Spinks argues that the most valuable groups are not the ones that answer questions or throw good parties, but small circles where you say your commitments out loud and the same people ask you about them next time. He cites research showing that simply announcing a goal can backfire, because being heard gives you a false sense of having arrived. What works is coming back, week after week, to people who remember what you said. If you run a founder or community programme, design for that return visit, not the launch moment. Via David Spinks.

And a second, from events: when a neighbourhood becomes your venue, you inherit every job a convention centre usually does. The first B twenty U S A summit spread its programme across fourteen venues on Washington's waterfront, loaded in on a single day, and used so much L E D screen that it exhausted the local supply, leaving a sixty-foot ticker dark during the opening concert. Power, wifi, loading docks and security queues all became the organiser's problem. Skift's second tip is timely: with Thanksgiving travel projected to fall for the first time since two thousand and eight, mainly because of cost, watch registration pace early and be ready with value-adds for self-funded attendees. Via Skift.

Section six, one interesting thing.

Why do people love the streets of Osaka and Taipei, even though the buildings are mostly ordinary? Works in Progress revisits the Austrian architect Camillo Sitte, whose eighteen eighty-nine book argued that beloved places are small, enclosed outdoor rooms, not grand symmetrical plazas. His rules of thumb still hold: a square should be no wider than twice the height of its main building, statues belong at the edges rather than the centre, and great buildings look grander when they are tangled up with everyday housing rather than standing alone. It is a useful lens for anyone designing a space where people gather, from a city block to an event venue. Via Works in Progress.
