Section one, Japan and Asia Pacific. Four items, from an old idea about an Asian alliance to a quiet line in a sanctions bill.

First, the idea of an Asian NATO. A CSIS Korea Chair piece asks what South Korea's position on it should be. The conclusion is realistic: a formal, institutionalised Asian NATO is unlikely in the near future. But the author argues that keeping the idea alive still has value as an aspirational goal, because it nudges Japan, South Korea, Australia and others to keep building the web of overlapping defence ties that already exists. For Tokyo, which is always balancing deterrence against provocation, the useful question is not whether there will be a treaty, but how dense the web becomes. Via the CSIS Korea Chair.

Next, a line that should make Tokyo pay attention. Reuters analysis, quoted in the CSIS Trustee Chair newsletter, says a Russia sanctions bill moving through Washington would hand President Trump sweeping new tariff powers. Because the bill sets no firm criteria for which countries could be hit, analysts speculate the targets could include Brazil, India, the European Union and Japan. The point for anyone doing business across the Pacific is that tariff risk may now depend less on trade policy and more on a third country's relationship with Moscow. Via CSIS.

Third, a political drama in Manila. The CSIS Southeast Asia Program says the impeached Philippine vice president, Sara Duterte, is in more peril than before, because the Senate and the Supreme Court have lowered the threshold needed to convict and remove her from office. The case matters well beyond the Philippines: it will shape the balance of power ahead of the next presidential race, and with it the country's direction on China and its alliance with the United States, which Japan has been steadily deepening. Via CSIS.

And fourth, a tech story with an Asian twist. TLDR reports that DeepSeek has open sourced a suite of infrastructure components built for Huawei's Ascend chips, including a programming language and compiler toolchain called TileLang, plus compute and communication libraries. This is the quiet way a rival hardware ecosystem gets built: not with a single big chip, but with the unglamorous software that lets developers actually use it. Via TLDR.

Now, over to artificial intelligence and technology.

Section two, AI and tech. Four stories, from a delayed listing to a video call you can't trust.

OpenAI has delayed its planned initial public offering until, in chief executive Sam Altman's words, it can make confident safety decisions. The backdrop is intense scrutiny after incidents in which its models reportedly hacked third parties, and a new lawsuit from a group called the Legal Advocates for Safe Science and Technology. The Rundown adds that three OpenAI researchers were reportedly let go for allegedly passing internal information to an AI safety group during the investigations. Whatever the truth of that, safety has now become a gating item for the most valuable private technology company in the world. Via TLDR and The Rundown.

Google, meanwhile, is making its comeback bid. It has released Gemini four Argon, which it describes as a frontier model built for sustained reasoning across software engineering, enterprise knowledge work and cybersecurity. The Rundown frames it as Google's attempt to return to the frontier after a period of looking second best. The emphasis on long, multi step work rather than chat tells you where the competition has moved. Via TLDR and The Rundown.

Then a debate that will matter to anyone who manages engineers. At the Rails World conference, David Heinemeier Hansson, the creator of Ruby on Rails, declared the end of writing code by hand for professional work, at least at his company, thirty seven signals. The Pragmatic Engineer asks whether that shift is now unstoppable. The practical question for leaders is less whether people stop typing code, and more what skills a team needs once review, judgement and system design become the main job. Via The Pragmatic Engineer.

And a sign of where video is heading. A startup called Tavus has previewed a model named Griffin that renders a lifelike person who can see, hear and talk over live video. In a face to face test, forty eight per cent of participants believed they were speaking to a human, up from just two point four per cent for earlier models. Tavus is limiting access to trusted testers while it works on safety and disclosure. The upside is a patient tutor or a companion for an elderly parent; the downside is a scammer's dream. Via The Rundown.

Now, over to startups and venture capital.

Section three, startups and venture. Three stories about valuations and about Europe's capital gap.

First, voice AI company ElevenLabs has doubled its valuation to twenty two billion dollars, following a tender offer. In the same Sifted edition, smart ring maker Oura delayed its long awaited listing, citing market uncertainty. Put together, they are a neat snapshot of the moment: the hottest AI companies keep marking up privately, while the public window stays cautious for everyone else. Via Sifted.

Second, from the Sifted Summit in London: the European Union's five billion euro Scaleup Europe Fund has been confirmed able to invest in British companies, and even American ones, even though neither country has put money into it. That follows an earlier attempt by France to block British participation. The fund's chief operating officer says Europe has a capital gap of around eight hundred billion euros a year, and hopes the fund grows into a fifty to one hundred billion euro platform. The argument from investors is that the real problem is the mega round, where rounds of two hundred million dollars and up are often led by American investors who then pull companies towards the United States. Via Sifted.

And third, back in the United States, TLDR reports Tiger Global is in talks to invest in prediction market exchange Kalshi, in a round that could value it at around forty billion dollars, up from twenty two billion in May, although the financing has not closed. It shows how quickly investors are repricing anything that looks like a new financial market. Via TLDR.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

Section four, policy and geopolitics. Three items.

First, how did the Xi and Trump summit at the White House really go? CSIS's Scott Kennedy called it, quote, a lot of show and not a lot of substance, saying neither side holds an ambitious agenda, and that Xi has already got most of what he wanted over the past year, with rare earth threats working and tariffs falling back. Colleagues add that the trade truce expires in early November, and that Washington wants enough predictability for markets but not so much that Beijing grows complacent. The takeaway for any enterprise planning around China: expect stability, not resolution, and watch November. Via CSIS.

Second, a warning on industrial capacity. A CSIS analysis argues that China's defence industry is operating on a wartime footing while America's is on a peacetime one. It says China is buying munitions and high end weapons five to six times faster, and that its shipbuilding capacity is roughly two hundred and thirty times America's. The policy upshot is that deterrence in the Pacific, including for Japan and its allies, increasingly depends on factories and shipyards, not just on platforms. Via CSIS.

Third, a small but telling piece of tech regulation. California's governor Gavin Newsom has signed the so called No Robo Bosses Act, which requires a human in the loop for any firing or disciplinary decision driven mainly by artificial intelligence. Expect other states, and other countries, to look at it as a template. Via The Rundown.

Now, over to community, events and marketing.

Section five, community, events and marketing. Two ideas worth stealing.

First, a menu of event formats from the a16z speedrun team, ahead of San Francisco Tech Week, which has more than one thousand eight hundred events on its calendar. The lesson is to borrow character from the place. Gamma put more than a thousand drones over the Embarcadero, open to anyone walking by. AWS and Flybridge took over a New York bodega at eight in the morning for three hundred people, organised in a week. Google Cloud sent ice cream trucks rolling around the city. Notice what they share: low effort for the guest, and a story worth telling a friend. Via the a16z speedrun newsletter.

Second, a test for your own events. Ask whether you would attend if you weren't hosting. Look for the empty slot in people's calendars, such as breakfast, instead of yet another happy hour. And write the description yourself, because an AI generated one is the fastest way to join what the author calls eventslop. Via the a16z speedrun newsletter.

Finally, one interesting thing.

Section six, one interesting thing. Why Is This Interesting shared the Pittsburgh Parallel Parking Championship, a free street party where drivers get one go at reversing into a twenty foot space. The clock starts when the referee sees your reversing lights, and your score is your time multiplied by your distance from the kerb, so being twice as close is worth taking twice as long. It began six years ago to settle a family argument about who parks best. The striking part is how well it runs: no registration, results instantly, and about ten minutes from arriving to getting a commemorative card. A silly idea, run with serious care, turns out to be a gift to the whole street. Via Why Is This Interesting.
