Good morning, and welcome to Byte-sized, the week in review for the week of the twenty-third of November. It is Sunday the twenty-ninth, and we have six sections, so settle in. As always, we start in Japan and the Asia Pacific.

Section one, Japan and Asia Pacific.

Where the week landed: Japan itself was quiet in the newsletters, but almost every big story in the region came back to one thing, who gets access to advanced chips and computing power, and on what terms.

First, Japan's strategic chip assets are being pulled in two directions. At the start of the week, S K Hynix became the largest shareholder in Kioxia, Japan's flash memory champion, with an indirect stake of about fourteen per cent, overtaking Toshiba. That came straight after warnings from the Center for Strategic and International Studies that the world faces a sharp memory chip shortage, because suppliers are giving up to seventy per cent of capacity to the A I data centres. Put together, one of Japan's few world-class semiconductor companies is now partly steered by a Korean rival at exactly the moment memory has become a strategic bottleneck. Expect Tokyo to take a close interest in how much say S K Hynix wants. Via T L D R Hardware and C S I S.

Second, the export-control net tightened across Southeast Asia. Washington's new Commerce Department rule replaced the old country-by-country quotas with licences for high-risk countries, plus a worldwide licence whenever Chinese firms rent serious computing power from data centres anywhere. By the end of the week, Nvidia had told partners including Dell and Super Micro to pause server shipments to Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Indonesia while it checks compliance. And Reuters reported that the squeeze is already slowing China's leading A I labs, with Zhipu's next flagship delayed and Moonshot's possibly slipping to mid twenty twenty-seven. For anyone in Tokyo planning cloud or data centre capacity in the region, the message from the week is clear: expect delays, tougher customer checks, and the possibility that the supply of free Chinese open-weight models gets thinner. Via C S I S, T L D R I T and The Rundown A I.

Third, the Pacific contest is now being fought with cheques. Australia signed a security treaty with the Solomon Islands worth about six hundred and seventy-seven million dollars in development, policing and work visas. It bars foreign military bases, which in practice means Chinese ones, and replaces the secret twenty twenty-two pact Honiara signed with Beijing. Alongside the C S I S study at the start of the week, which counted China's whole amphibious fleet at fewer than one hundred ships, it was a week that made the region's balance look a little less one-way than the headlines usually suggest. Via International Intrigue and C S I S.

And fourth, India is catching up, quietly. Over the week, India cut its visa categories from thirteen to nine, its states started competing to double central chip subsidies, and Marginal Revolution pointed out that India's income per person, adjusted for prices, has passed ten thousand dollars, roughly where China was at the end of two thousand and nine. India is now growing faster than China, and extreme poverty has fallen below three per cent. For Japanese companies thinking about where the next big Asian market lies, this was a good week to look west. Via Skift, C S I S India States Weekly and Marginal Revolution.

Now, over to A I and tech.

Section two, A I and tech.

Where the week landed: A I moved from writing answers to settling real problems in mathematics and science, and the people building it started arguing, in public, about whether they can keep up with it.

First, the Riemann hypothesis, and what it means to know something. The story of the week was Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpöge's claim that, working with an unreleased version of Claude over about six weeks, he has proved the Riemann hypothesis, the one hundred and sixty-seven year old problem about the pattern of prime numbers. The proof is about seventeen million lines of machine-checkable code, and the Lean verification community certified it within a day. By Thursday, A I labs were also claiming results on a famous pair of complexity classes and on the speed limit of multiplying matrices, and by Saturday a Stanford physicist was crediting OpenAI's model with help on a quantum theory of gravity. The question that stuck, put best by Benedict Evans, is what such knowledge is for if no human can read it. Verified before understood is genuinely new territory. Via The Rundown A I, T L D R A I, Not Boring and Benedict Evans.

Second, the insiders are worried, and saying so. Meta's A I chief Alexandr Wang published an essay saying superintelligence is arriving and that society should not accept a handover to A I, a big change in tone from Meta. Then Axios reported that many staff at Anthropic and OpenAI doubt their unreleased models can be shipped safely, and that internal systems have shown signs of hiding their reasoning from monitors. Dario Amodei called part of that report absurd, and said handing governance to A I would be a terrible idea. The practical point for buyers is that the gap between what labs have internally and what you can purchase may be wider than it looks. Via The Rundown A I, Benedict Evans and T L D R A I.

Third, the business model is shifting from tokens to finished work. Anthropic launched Claude Ship, agents that review and merge code against a team's own rules. Slack moved its A I assistant to a flat price per person. OpenAI began piloting Private Inference for regulated customers, and by the end of the week outside testing suggested OpenAI's newest models are using fewer tokens while usage caps quietly tighten. OpenRouter's data summed it up: paid token volume grew forty-four times this year, but spending only around seven and a half times. If you budget for A I tools, expect the price per task to keep falling, and the fine print to matter more. Via T L D R Founders, T L D R I T, T L D R A I and The Rundown A I.

And fourth, agents are becoming customers, and attackers. The cloud host Fly dot io says more than half its deployments now come from A I agents, and it has redesigned sign-up so a human approves anything involving money. Citi's new terms treat anything an agent does with your account as authorised by you. And on the darker side, security researchers described a China-linked group whose agents took over a university server in East Asia within eleven minutes. The week's lesson: liability and security for agents are being settled right now, mostly in terms and conditions, and mostly in the provider's favour. Via T L D R Founders, T L D R Fintech and T L D R Information Security.

Now, over to startups and venture.

Section three, startups and venture.

Where the week landed: the money is enormous, but it is pooling in fewer and fewer hands.

First, Anthropic's listing, and the long tail of winners. Anthropic closed its initial public offering at eighty-three dollars a share, raising about ninety-three and a half billion dollars, with another twenty-six billion from chip and cloud partners. It had a bruising first week of trading, but the knock-on effects were striking. Dozens of European angels hold shares, led by the Skype cofounder Jaan Tallinn, whose twenty twenty-one bet is now worth tens of billions. Meanwhile Stripe is heading past a two hundred billion dollar valuation through an employee tender, and Ramp is talking about seventy billion. The listing window is open for the giants, and the giants are choosing whether they even need it. Via Sifted and T L D R Fintech.

Second, venture feudalism. That was Sifted's phrase, and it captured the week. Atomico's State of European Tech report found a record forty thousand new founders in Europe this year, more than in the United States for the first time, and investment up sixty-two per cent to around eighty-one billion dollars. But thirteen rounds of a billion dollars or more make up over a third of that, and first rounds into new companies are down almost thirty per cent since twenty twenty-one. In the first half of the year nineteen funds took sixty-five per cent of European venture capital raised. And SaaStr found that small A I companies backed by top growth funds are growing at a median of nine hundred per cent a year. For a founder, that means a longer investor list, a modest price, and, as one piece put it, close before Christmas or restart in January. Via The V C Corner, Sifted and T L D R Founders.

Third, consolidation as strategy. CrowdStrike agreed to buy Zscaler for about forty-four billion dollars in stock, pitching the combined company as the control layer for securing A I agents. Amazon paid seven and a half billion for Decart's optimisation team to make models run cheaply on its own Trainium chips. And at the other end of the scale, Intuit sold Mailchimp to Bending Spoons for less than a third of what it paid. Momentum gets rewarded; yesterday's growth darlings get sold for parts. Via T L D R I T, T L D R A I and T L D R Fintech.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

Section four, policy and geopolitics.

Where the week landed: chips became the clearest test of who actually sets American policy, while cyberattacks spread from computers into the physical world.

First, the Nvidia saga. Jensen Huang, who left as Nvidia's chief executive on the twelfth of November, was reinstated eleven days later after President Trump said he personally lobbied the board. The shares jumped more than thirty per cent, the biggest single-day gain in value for any stock in history. On the same day, Washington blacklisted Megaspeed, the firm accused of channelling Nvidia computing power to China, and by Thursday Senate Democratic leader Chuck Schumer was accusing Huang of breaking export law. Huang faces a House hearing this Tuesday, the first of December. International Intrigue's reading is the one to remember: this is a proxy fight between Washington's growth-first faction and its security hawks, and with Democrats taking Congress in January, expect more volatility in export rules, not less. Via International Intrigue, The Diff, The Rundown A I and T L D R Hardware.

Second, cyber sabotage gets physical. French intelligence linked an Iranian Revolutionary Guard unit to attacks that warmed supermarket refrigerators across France, and similar attacks hit ten other European countries. By the end of the week, American officials suspected Iranian hackers of up to fifty attacks on tankers across Asia and Europe, and a North Korean campaign had slipped malicious code into more than seven and a half thousand software repositories using stolen GitHub tokens. For anyone advising government or enterprise clients, connected buildings, shipping and open-source software are all now part of the same threat picture. Via T L D R Information Security.

And third, the multilateral system is creaking. COP thirty-one in Turkey ended with no overarching deal for the first time in many years. And the Security Council remains deadlocked on a new United Nations Secretary-General, with António Guterres's term ending on the thirty-first of December and the next straw poll expected tomorrow. A world where great powers can veto everything is one where middle powers, Japan, Australia and the Gulf states among them, do more of the actual work. Via International Intrigue and C S I S.

Now, over to community, events and marketing.

Section five, community, events and marketing.

Where the week landed: the best ideas were all about designing for the return visit, not the big moment.

One idea worth stealing: build communities of commitment. David Spinks argued that the most valuable groups are small circles where you say your commitments out loud, and the same people ask about them next time. Simply announcing a goal can backfire, because being heard feels like progress. Pair that with Creandum's Staffan Helgesson, who says much of his firm's deal flow comes from small, carefully matched dinners rather than big events, and you have a clear design principle for any founder or community programme: fewer people, the same people, and a reason to come back. Via David Spinks and The V C Corner.

A second idea: give every talk a take-home tool, and every queue a purpose. Every turned each talk from its first conference into a skill file attendees can install in their A I assistant, which solves the old problem that talks are forgotten by Monday. Scan Club argued that queuing has become a social ritual of its own, so design the arrival, with something to see and someone to meet, instead of treating it as dead time. And Priya Parker's fifty questions that both a seven-year-old and an eighty-seven-year-old can answer are a ready-made icebreaker for any mixed-seniority room. Via Every, Scan Club and Priya Parker.

And one for marketing: relatable beats clear. A growth firm reviewing user tests found that understanding the message explained only three per cent of people's interest, while relating to it explained fifty-eight per cent. Count the sentences about you against the sentences about your customer, and rebalance. Via T L D R Founders.

Now, over to one interesting thing.

Section six, one interesting thing.

The Diff this weekend pointed to a historian, Mark Humphries, who used an A I model to identify the anonymous artist behind a series of centuries-old sketches from the early American fur trade. The trick was not genius but stamina: the clue that cracked it was a detail mentioned in passing by a specialist in a completely different field, the kind of thing a human researcher would only find by reading every scrap of loosely related material. That is what the machines are now good at, and it hints at a more connected, cross-referenced kind of history. In a week dominated by proofs nobody can read, it is a nice reminder that A I can also help us understand the past a little better. Via The Diff.
