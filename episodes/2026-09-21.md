JAPAN AND ASIA-PACIFIC

A thin day for the region, but one genuinely interesting policy experiment out of Singapore. The government there has launched a scheme paying citizens actual cash to read: log fifty reading sessions of at least fifteen minutes each, with only one session countable per day, and you earn one Singapore dollar, worth roughly fifty nine pence. It won't make anyone rich, but behavioural scientists think the real trick isn't the money so much as the structure around it: a manageable daily goal built into an existing routine, like a commute or bedtime, with visible streaks to track progress. It's a small, very Singaporean bet that gamifying a habit can outcompete the dopamine hit of scrolling. Via Marginal Revolution.

Meanwhile in India, economist Tyler Cowen's Emergent Ventures grant programme unveiled its nineteenth Indian cohort this week, and the range of projects funded says something about where the region's next wave of builders is coming from. Grants went to a solar powered elephant deterrent already field deployed in Jharkhand, a fully electric hubless bicycle, a two hundred dollar diagnostic pen built as a cheaper alternative to a three thousand dollar Parkinson's scan, and a platform tracking real time updates from Delhi's courts. Several of the recipients are still teenagers. It's a small programme by venture standards, but a genuinely useful read on how much grassroots deep tech and civic tooling is being built across India right now. Via Marginal Revolution.

Now, over to AI and tech.

AI AND TECH

Claude keeps turning up in unexpected corners of business. A fundraising newsletter walked through using Claude Code to build what amounts to a fundraising engine: founders feed it a one page thesis describing their stage, region and target investor type, and it scores prospective investors on three dimensions, fit with the fund's actual recent deals, how recently that specific partner wrote a check, and whether there's a real way to reach them, rather than treating a scraped list as gospel. The pitch is blunt: half of any bought investor list is dead weight, wrong stage, dead funds, or contacts who left years ago, and no amount of clever subject lines fixes a list problem. Via the VC Corner.

Elsewhere, the newsletter Every used its own AI agent as a party planner, handing an upcoming Brooklyn meetup over to its in house assistant to organise end to end, while flagging a separate piece collecting thirteen beliefs about writing with AI and an early look at a strikingly capable new model. It's a small, telling example of AI moving from a tool people use to something people are willing to delegate actual logistics to, and a reminder that the most interesting AI adoption stories right now are often the mundane ones, not the frontier benchmark ones. Via Every.

And economist Tyler Cowen gave a rare unqualified rave to a product called Muse this week, praising its interface, its speed and its calendar and email integration as some of the smoothest he's seen from any piece of software recently. High praise from a writer who reviews a great many AI tools and rarely sounds this enthusiastic, and worth a look if your current note taking and calendar setup still feels like three separate apps stitched together. Via Marginal Revolution.

Now, over to startups and venture.

STARTUPS AND VENTURE

On the podcast circuit, Lenny's newsletter sat down with Peter Sellis, the first product manager at Snapchat and later head of product at Discord, for an unusually candid conversation now that he has nothing left to promote. Among the more memorable lines: his case for designing product teams the way you'd design a small, disciplined outfit rather than a large bureaucracy, and his three oxymorons of great product management, born from watching what actually separates strong product leaders from mediocre ones at two of the last decade's fastest growing consumer apps. Via Lenny's Newsletter.

On the fundraising side, the VC Corner published a fresh database of nine hundred and three seed stage venture firms across Europe, each one checked for an actual way in, whether that's two hundred and eighty seven with a direct email, three hundred and eighty six with a deck submission link, or two hundred and thirty with both. It's less a story than a resource, but a useful reminder of how much of fundraising success now comes down to basic list hygiene rather than pitch polish. Via the VC Corner.

And a nice indie hacker story out of the pandemic years. Shaun Donnelly built a simple iOS workout tracker in five days during lockdown, mostly as an excuse to leave the house afterwards. Six years on, that app, Personal Best, has passed five hundred thousand downloads, has around fifty thousand monthly active users, earned a cameo in one of Apple's developer conference keynotes, and now brings in close to five thousand dollars a month, all while running almost entirely on the device itself to keep costs near zero. He still freelances part time to cover the gap. Via Indie Hackers.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

POLICY AND GEOPOLITICS

CSIS's weekly numbers roundup was a useful snapshot of how stretched the world's supply chains and alliances currently are. Europe has filled just sixty eight percent of its gas storage against an eighty percent winter target, with prices climbing on Middle East conflict and reduced pipeline flows from Algeria and Norway. China has been quietly upgrading its military base in Djibouti, a country that sits on a chokepoint carrying an estimated sixteen percent of global maritime trade. On the AUKUS submarine pact, the United States alone reckons it needs to grow its submarine industrial workforce by one hundred and forty thousand people over the next decade, a reminder of just how much physical capacity, not just political will, that partnership still requires. And separately in the same digest, CSIS put US private sector investment in proprietary AI models at around two hundred and eighty five billion dollars, framing China's push toward competitive open weight models as a genuine challenge to that spending rather than a simple catch up story. Via CSIS.

And in economic data, Britain's statisticians quietly rewrote a decade of history this week. The Office for National Statistics now estimates UK productivity grew at one point three percent a year between two thousand and nine and twenty nineteen, almost double its earlier estimate of zero point seven percent, thanks to a new methodology for counting workers and hours. It doesn't undo the last decade's stagnation entirely, but it does make Britain look meaningfully less of an international productivity outlier than the old numbers suggested. Via Marginal Revolution and the Financial Times.

And in travel economics, airline executives used investor conferences this week to warn that renewed attacks during the Iran war have pushed jet fuel back up to around four dollars fifty a gallon, undoing this summer's brief relief. American's chief executive said the airline is slowing its growth into next year, Southwest's finance chief said there's room to pass more of that cost onto ticket prices, and United said it expects to recover the entirety of its higher fuel costs by the end of the year. It's a reminder that a shooting war in the Middle East shows up in ordinary lives less through headlines than through the price of a plane ticket. Via Skift.

Now, over to community, events and marketing. One idea worth stealing.

COMMUNITY, EVENTS AND MARKETING

From the DO Lectures: you don't need a big content drop to stay in someone's inbox. Their newsletter this week, cheerfully titled Chicken Shed Chronicles, was mostly a handful of small, human curiosities: an artist who paints in miniature using paint colour cards, a design studio's take on a less boring highlighter, a Welsh craftsperson's handmade instruments. None of it was a pitch for anything. The idea worth stealing is that a community newsletter doesn't have to justify itself with an event or an announcement every time; sometimes just being interesting and a little odd is enough to keep people opening the email. Via the DO Lectures.

Now, to close, one interesting thing.

ONE INTERESTING THING

The newsletter the Diff flagged a genuinely strange piece of history this week. Papua New Guinea's interior stayed almost entirely cut off from the outside world until nineteen thirty, a fact confirmed not just by old explorers' accounts but by modern genetic analysis showing a population that was isolated for an extraordinarily long stretch of human history. Its traditional economy ran on shell currency, and its social status system rewarded whoever could give away the biggest gifts without expecting them back in full, which amounted to an accidental, deeply progressive tax on accumulating wealth. It's a reminder of how recently parts of the world existed entirely outside the systems the rest of us take for granted. Via the Diff.
