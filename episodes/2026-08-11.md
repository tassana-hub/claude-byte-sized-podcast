Good morning, it's Tuesday, the eleventh of August. Six sections for you today.

Now, over to Japan and Asia-Pacific.

China and North Korea appear to be resetting their relationship after years of relative distance. Chinese leader Xi Jinping travelled to Pyongyang in June to meet Kim Jong-un for the first time in seven years, and it was his only trip abroad so far this year. That visit was followed by two exchanges of congratulatory letters between the two leaders in July, and separate visits by a close aide of each man to the other's capital in the middle of that month. Analysts at the China Power Project read this flurry of diplomatic activity as a genuine recalibration rather than a one-off photo opportunity, with implications for how Beijing balances its relationships with Pyongyang, Seoul and Washington. Via CSIS China Power Project.

Asia's chip sovereignty race is intensifying. SK Hynix has approved a fifty four trillion won, or roughly thirty eight billion dollar, investment to build two new memory fabrication plants, aimed squarely at the bottlenecks in artificial intelligence infrastructure. At the same time, Chinese firms are reportedly planning to lift their spending on domestically made AI accelerators from around thirty percent of their budgets to forty six percent over the next year, as Beijing pushes self-reliance in chips. Together the two stories capture a region racing in parallel to insure itself against the other side's export controls. Via TLDR Hardware.

And in Southeast Asia, Indonesia's president Prabowo Subianto is trying to walk a narrow line between Beijing and the West. A new analysis argues Indonesia has to defend its maritime claims in the Natuna Sea even as it deepens economic ties with China, a balancing act that's becoming harder as competition in the South China Sea intensifies. It's a reminder that plenty of Asia-Pacific governments are hedging rather than picking sides, whatever Washington or Beijing might prefer. Via Geopolitical Monitor.

And Japan's fighter jet partnership has picked up new interest. Canada has become the first observer nation of the Global Combat Air Programme, the next-generation fighter jet being developed jointly by the United Kingdom, Italy and Japan. Observer status gives Ottawa a look inside the programme without full membership rights, and analysts frame it as a narrow window for Canada to prove its worth before deciding whether to push for a fuller role. For Tokyo, it's another sign that its highest-profile defence-industrial project is drawing wider international interest. Via Geopolitical Monitor.

Now, over to AI and tech.

OpenAI has paused work on its unreleased Astra model after internal evaluations suggested it may be approaching what the company calls a Critical cybersecurity capability threshold, meaning it could plausibly assist with advanced, autonomous exploit development. The company has tightened controls in response, including isolated testing environments, restricted access and stronger protection of the model's underlying weights, and it's delaying wider availability until it's confident Astra can be deployed safely. It's a rare case of a lab publicly slowing itself down rather than a regulator or a leak forcing the issue. Via TLDR and the Rundown.

Anthropic is pushing further into autonomous coding. Claude Code's auto mode becomes the default for Pro, Max and Team users from the fourteenth of August, letting most actions proceed without a permission prompt, and the company has also shipped cross-session messaging, so a Claude Code session on one project can now message another session running elsewhere on the same machine. Together they point at the same direction of travel: less babysitting, more autonomous, coordinated agent work. Via TLDR and This Week in Startups.

Mark Zuckerberg has used a lengthy essay to recommit Meta to open-weight AI, pledging further releases including two new Muse models alongside a billion dollar fund aimed at communities hosting Meta's data centres. The pitch is as much political as technical: an argument for how ordinary Americans might share in AI's productivity gains, at a moment when open-weight releases from Chinese labs have been chipping away at the case that only closed frontier labs can lead. Via This Week in Startups.

And a small personal essay this week captured something a lot of vibe coders will recognise. A writer describes shipping an AI-generated feature that worked perfectly, only for a code-reviewing agent to flag afterwards that nobody, including her, had actually checked whether it was safe. It's a neat illustration of a wider pattern: as AI-assisted coding gets faster, the bottleneck is quietly shifting from writing code to reviewing it, and plenty of builders are learning that lesson the hard way. Via Every.

There's also a quieter strategic shift underway at Google. Rather than chasing outright frontier-model dominance, the company appears to be leaning into AI diffusion instead, prioritising Cloud, its own custom chips and the infrastructure that powers other companies' AI applications. The comparison being drawn is to Westinghouse over Edison: capturing more long-run value by distributing the technology broadly than by trying to win the single most expensive model race outright. Via TLDR.

Now, over to startups and venture.

Hadrian, which builds automated factories using AI and robotics to mass-produce precision parts for submarines, munitions and drones, has closed a one billion, three hundred and seventy million dollar Series D at a valuation of nearly eight billion dollars, more than quadrupling its worth since January. The round underlines how much investor appetite has shifted toward AI-powered defence manufacturing as governments look to rebuild industrial capacity outside China. Via TLDR Hardware.

European robotics is having a moment too. Sifted reports that European robotics startups raised two point six seven billion euros in equity funding in just the first six months of this year, more than the total raised across the previous two years combined, with fresh rounds for Spain's HappyRobot, backed by Andreessen Horowitz, and Zurich-based Exclaim Robotics, which builds maintenance robots for data centres. It's a sign that robotics, long Europe's quieter cousin to software, is starting to pull in serious capital. Via Sifted.

And legal AI is heating up: Harvey is reportedly in talks to raise at least five hundred million dollars at a fifteen and a half billion dollar valuation, potentially led by Lightspeed, as it works to build proprietary models and fend off rivals like Legora. Meanwhile Cursor could end up rebranded once SpaceX completes its expected sixty billion dollar acquisition of the company, a reminder that even well-known developer tools aren't immune to being folded into a bigger platform story. Elsewhere, Stanford-founded chip manufacturing startup Source Foundry raised four hundred million dollars from Situational Awareness, taking its total funding to half a billion dollars as it works to make semiconductor production faster and cheaper, and real-time video startup Decart is reportedly fielding interest from Nvidia, SpaceX, Amazon and Nebius at a valuation as high as seven billion dollars. Via This Week in Startups.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

The Trump administration has signed a Section 232 order aimed at protecting the US polysilicon industry, the raw material behind both semiconductors and solar panels. It sets minimum import prices and a fifteen percent tariff on downstream products, and creates an incentive programme for domestic polysilicon production, part of an effort to rebuild a supply chain that's shrunk from half of global capacity in 2005 to under two percent today, with China now supplying around ninety percent. The remedies take effect a hundred and twenty days after signing. Via TLDR Hardware.

Cross-strait analysts are revisiting the foundations of the status quo. A new piece argues the so-called 1992 Consensus between Beijing and Taipei was never really a lasting settlement, just a temporary bargain to postpone an argument neither side could resolve, and that bargain looks increasingly threadbare. It's a useful corrective for anyone who assumes the current uneasy calm across the Taiwan Strait rests on firmer ground than it actually does. Via Geopolitical Monitor.

And a number worth noting: Germany's trade deficit with China hit fifty nine billion dollars in the first half of this year, with German exports to China down twelve percent year on year while imports from China rose a further nine percent. Watch for whether that imbalance hardens Berlin's position on Chinese industrial overcapacity in the months ahead. Via International Intrigue.

Now, over to community, events and marketing.

One idea worth stealing: ahead of its Loop 2026 conference, community platform CMX previewed a session on turning AI into a reporting shortcut rather than a buzzword. Two Slack community marketing managers are building a practical session around using AI to translate raw community activity into the kind of business-impact story executives actually understand, complete with reusable prompt frameworks so community teams stop rebuilding the same report from scratch every quarter. The bigger idea travels well beyond community management: if a part of your job is translating messy activity data into a narrative leadership will act on, that's exactly the kind of repeatable task worth handing to an AI-assisted workflow. Via CMX.

Now, one interesting thing.

America's greatest chess tournament organiser, Bill Goichberg, has died. Over more than five decades he built much of the infrastructure that ordinary amateur chess players in the United States still rely on, running countless open tournaments and helping found the system that lets a weekend hobbyist and a grandmaster show up at the same event. Economist Tyler Cowen, who knew him, remembers him simply as someone who was always encouraging to newcomers, a quiet reminder that entire hobbies can be held together for generations by one person's persistence rather than any grand institution. Via Marginal Revolution.
