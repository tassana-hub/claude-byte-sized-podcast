Good morning, and welcome to Byte-sized, the week of the twenty-sixth of October. It is Sunday, so this is the week in review, in six sections, and we begin, as ever, in Japan and the Asia Pacific.

Section one, Japan and Asia Pacific.

Where the week landed: it was a week of breaches that came in through the back door. In Japan, the story was less about clever attackers and more about suppliers, and across the region, old border disputes refused to stay settled.

First, G M O Internet. On Monday and Tuesday the story was still unfolding, and by Wednesday the picture was clear. An attacker got into the Onamae Desktop Cloud service, which is used by financial traders, and reportedly demanded about fifty million dollars. G M O shut the service down on the twenty-first of October. Up to twenty-seven thousand servers may be affected, customers reported unauthorised activity in their trading accounts, and some alleged customer data has since appeared online. The company is verifying the leak, asking users to reset passwords and collecting customer devices to check for malware. If you sell cloud or fintech services into Japan, expect this to shape procurement questions for months. Via T L D R Information Security.

Second, Japan Airlines, which on Friday warned ninety-two thousand customers that their data had been exposed. The breach did not come through the airline itself, but through a third party that runs its reservation, ticketing and check-in systems. For most people the attackers only took names, booking codes and flight details, but for about two thousand customers they also took passport numbers, frequent flyer numbers, email addresses and phone numbers. Put the two stories side by side and the lesson for Japanese enterprise is the same: your weakest link is probably your supplier. Via T L D R Information Security.

Third, the wider region is dealing with the same pressure. In South Korea, Daishin Securities reported a breach exposing the names, email addresses and mobile numbers of more than thirty-eight thousand customers, part of what was described as a fresh wave of attacks on the financial industry. Via T L D R.

Fourth, the security backdrop on the Korean peninsula. Seoul's Joint Chiefs of Staff said Pyongyang fired more than ten short-range ballistic missiles towards the East Sea on Thursday, so soon after Kim Jong-un's border trip to China that the diplomacy and the military signalling look to be running side by side. In the same week, a United Nations General Assembly committee adopted Japan's annual resolution calling for the abolition of nuclear weapons, which keeps Tokyo's long-standing position on the record in a year of loud nuclear signalling elsewhere. Via the C S I S Nuclear Policy News.

Fifth, Thailand and Cambodia, one year after the Kuala Lumpur peace accord. That deal broke down in December, and a second ceasefire, agreed after three weeks of fighting, is holding but shaky. Military officers met again on the twenty-eighth of October, yet Bangkok has just rejected a Cambodian demand for the return of artefacts from two border temples. The more interesting track is at sea. After Thailand cancelled a two thousand and one agreement on oil and gas in disputed waters, Cambodia started compulsory conciliation under the United Nations law of the sea, only the second time that process has ever been used. The latest round, in The Hague, produced a promise to tone down the rhetoric and a hotline between the two navies. Via C S I S, The Latest on Southeast Asia.

Now, over to artificial intelligence and tech.

Section two, A I and tech.

Where the week landed: the models kept leapfrogging each other, and the security consequences arrived faster than anyone planned for.

First, Google. Its new frontier model, Gemini 4 Carbon, arrived only about a month after Gemini 4 Argon, and it takes the top spot on the Artificial Analysis intelligence index with a score of fifty-nine, nine points up on Argon, and ahead of Claude Opus five point five. It is aimed at cybersecurity and long-running coding work, and its introductory price is half that of Opus five point five, though that doubles after the discount. The catch is that almost nobody can use it yet. Access starts with select cyber defenders, and Google has given no date for wider release. With OpenAI and Anthropic shipping at a similar pace, the crown may not last long. Via The Rundown A I and T L D R.

Second, the security story that dominated the engineering newsletters. Gergely Orosz, in The Pragmatic Engineer, called it a month of breaches in a single day. Two critical flaws in P H P, the language behind roughly seventy per cent of websites, were found by researchers working with an A I model, and Amazon saw exploit attempts four days before public disclosure, so the details leaked from somewhere. Once the patch was out, Cloudflare saw millions of attempts within hours. Meanwhile a certificate authority called eMudhra turned out to have issued certificates whose private keys were tiny numbers, one of them literally the number four, and it lost about forty per cent of its market value in two days. And the managed database provider PingCAP disclosed that an attacker had roamed its systems for a month. His argument is that A I has lowered the skill needed to attack, so patching must become automatic, monitoring must be continuous, and defenders will need to use A I too. Via The Pragmatic Engineer.

Third, a small hiring story with big implications. An engineering manager at Anthropic, Felix Rieseberg, wrote that he has dropped the coding interview from his team's hiring, because Claude now outperforms people on the short tests it relied on. He also argues that hunger beats tenure, and that junior engineers armed with A I can now overtake veterans, citing two newer employees who prototyped a desktop app in a single weekend. The Pragmatic Engineer reported the same shift across the wider industry. Via The Rundown A I and The Pragmatic Engineer.

Fourth, OpenAI said it will stop training its models on the live internet, citing significant risks after models accidentally attacked unauthorised sites during tests. It plans to build a cached and indexed copy of the public web for training instead. Via T L D R Information Security.

Fifth, two smaller items from the engineering side that will matter to anyone running A I agents in production. Cloudflare disclosed that a very long prompt sent to its GLM five point three model on Workers A I could return other customers' data, in a hundred and fifty-five responses, because of how a serving framework handled over-length requests. It found no evidence of deliberate exploitation. And a security firm called Tracebit tested decoy tricks against A I attackers in more than seven hundred controlled cloud assessments, and found that combined deception cut the average number of completed attacker objectives from about two and a half to under a quarter. The takeaway is that decoys can slow down and misdirect automated attackers before they reach anything real. Via T L D R Information Security.

Now, over to startups and venture.

Section three, startups and venture.

Where the week landed: the money is enormous, and it is concentrating, while the argument about what investors are actually for got louder.

First, the numbers. Bloomberg figures reported by The V C Corner put global venture funding at roughly seven hundred and fifty billion dollars through the third quarter, already above the previous record from twenty twenty-one, with a quarter still to go. A I took fifty-four per cent of the total, and eighty-eight rounds of a billion dollars or more made up over sixty per cent of disclosed deal value. Separately, A I companies raised five hundred and nineteen billion dollars through mid-September, two point three times the whole of last year, and seventy-six per cent of it went to American companies, with China on fifty-nine billion and Europe on forty. If your fundraising story is not about A I, you are competing for the other half of the market. Via The V C Corner.

Second, the Asia angle. Korean chip start-up FuriosaAI raised six hundred and twenty million dollars in a Series D to scale production of its A I inference chips, one of the biggest rounds of the week. Alternatives to the dominant chip suppliers continue to find backers. Via The V C Corner.

Third, Lovable, the Swedish vibe coding start-up. Sifted reported that it is offering employees an eighty million dollar cash-out at a valuation of thirteen point three billion dollars. Employee liquidity at that scale is becoming a retention tool as much as a reward. Via Sifted.

Fourth, the argument of the week. Revolut chief executive Nik Storonsky said he had gradually removed venture investors from his board, warning that investors without operating experience can be dangerous to a company's growth, and recalling one who wanted him to fire an employee for being too hardcore, who went on to found Fuse Energy. Sifted asked founders and investors whether he has a point. The honest answer is that it is easier to say when you run one of Europe's most valuable scale-ups. Via Sifted.

Fifth, a note from Europe, because it will matter if you raise money there. Sifted reported that the Bank of England ordered a review of Revolut's internal controls over new product launches, only months after the company won its long-awaited United Kingdom banking licence. Growth and compliance are colliding, even for the most successful scale-ups. Via Sifted.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

Section four, policy and geopolitics.

Where the week landed: technology policy and security policy are now the same conversation.

First, the United States government as a buyer. Anthropic widened its government purchasing deal with a forty per cent discount on Claude, saying civilian agency use is up about twenty times in a year, after a judge blocked an order to drop it. Whatever one thinks of the politics, it shows that public sector adoption is moving faster than the argument about it. Via The Rundown A I.

Second, an idea from the policy end of the A I debate. An essay summarised in The V C Corner argues that a United States and China slowdown in frontier A I could be made enforceable by verifying compute at the chip level, because computing power is energy intensive, comes from a few suppliers, and is easier to watch than software. Satellite imagery, customs data, inspections and tamper-resistant chips would each lower the payoff from cheating. It is a thought experiment today, but it is the kind of thing that could turn up in a conversation with government. Via The V C Corner.

Third, the election. OpenAI is pulling hundreds of staff into a war room for next week's United States midterm elections, after flagging Russian and Iranian influence campaigns on its models this month. Expect the same questions to arrive in Japan and Europe at their next votes. Via The Rundown A I.

Fourth, a defensive tool that policy people may find useful as a talking point. GitHub put a package firewall into public preview for its enterprise cloud customers. It sits between coding agents, build systems and the public software registries, and lets administrators set cooldown periods, check advisories and block risky packages. In a week where supplier compromise was the theme in Japan, it is a concrete example of how large organisations are responding. Via T L D R Information Security.

Now, over to community, events and marketing.

Section five, community, events and marketing. One idea worth stealing.

The idea this week comes from the Event Design Collective, and it is a single question to bring to your next planning meeting: what future is this event assuming? Every event is built on quiet assumptions, that people will care about certain issues, that particular skills will matter, that stakeholders will behave in familiar ways, that today's formats will keep working. The authors' method, which they call future literacy, is simply to make those assumptions visible, and then to ask what happens if the future does not turn up. It costs nothing, takes ten minutes in a meeting, and it is exactly the kind of habit that separates a trusted adviser from an order taker. Via the Event Design Collective.

And a second, smaller one. Marketing Brew reported this week that brand interest in run clubs is not slowing down. If you run a community, the lesson is that a regular, low-barrier, physical meetup still beats most digital formats for building loyalty, and brands are willing to pay to be part of it. Via Marketing Brew.

Now, one interesting thing to close.

Section six, one interesting thing.

From Experimental History, a finding about peer review. A researcher inserted one hundred errors into ten psychology papers and ran them through several A I models. No single model did especially well, but together they caught ninety-three per cent. A second study found that both human and A I judges preferred A I reviews to those written by paid Harvard experts, on every dimension tested. The author's point is subtle: this is not machines replacing human labour, because humans were never doing this kind of quality checking in the first place. Does the code run, do the analyses match the plan, does something in the appendix undo the main result? Nobody was checking, and now something can. Via Experimental History.
