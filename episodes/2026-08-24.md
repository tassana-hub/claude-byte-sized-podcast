Good morning, it's Monday the twenty-fourth of August, and this covers the news from Friday through the weekend. Six sections today, running from Tokyo's currency diplomacy to a founder who lost eight million dollars overnight and built it straight back up. Let's get into it.

Now, over to Japan and Asia-Pacific.

Tokyo Steps Into the Currency Markets. On the latest Trade Guys podcast, CSIS's Bill Reinsch and Scott Miller broke down a joint United States-Japan intervention in currency markets, alongside trade provisions tucked into the Senate's farm bill and fresh Trump administration enforcement action against goods linked to forced labour. The currency move is a reminder of how central the Tokyo-Washington relationship remains to managing global trade friction, even while attention drifts toward tariffs elsewhere. Via CSIS.

Japan's Boardrooms Get Faster, Not Necessarily Wiser. A Japanese-language dispatch from Uzabase's Speeda AI News desk looked at how generative AI is reshaping mid-term corporate planning in Japan, the three-year "public promise" companies traditionally rewrote only occasionally. Plans can now be updated annually, or even continuously, as AI compresses research and analysis that used to take weeks. Yet in a new management-planning survey, only thirteen percent of respondents said they would trust AI's output for a decision more or less as it stands. Speed has outpaced trust, and the final call still sits with a person. Via Speeda AI News, Uzabase.

Tokyo's Missile Test Rattles Pyongyang. CSIS's nuclear policy briefing flagged a sharp read from the Washington Examiner: Japan's test-firing of US-made Tomahawk cruise missiles, paired with Tokyo's push for its own long-range strike capability, appears to have shifted Kim Jong un's nuclear calculus more than anything coming out of Washington. The item landed alongside news that President Trump plans to meet Kim again later this year, and wildly differing estimates of the North's arsenal, from Trump's own count up to South Korea's estimate of one hundred and twenty warheads, more than double what Trump himself cited just a day earlier. It's a reminder that on this question, allies watching Pyongyang closely still can't agree on the basic facts. Via CSIS's Project on Nuclear Issues.

Now, over to AI and tech.

The Hospital System That Built Its Own Claude. Every profiled Headway, a nine-hundred-person mental healthcare company that couldn't find an off-the-shelf AI tool meeting its security and compliance bar, so it built one. The result, called Eddy, runs on Anthropic's Claude Code SDK inside Headway's own cloud environment, with every conversation sandboxed in a disposable container and tightly scoped access to company systems. Six months after starting, the whole company now uses it, and the chief technology officer says relying on outside vendors "felt like we were missing the train." Via Every.

Slack Wants to Own the Room Where Coding Happens. Salesforce launched Slack Code, which drops coding agents, including Claude Code, Devin, GitHub Copilot and Vercel, straight into shared Slack channels, so engineers and non-technical teammates alike can watch, steer and approve a build as it happens. Deployment stays gated behind human sign-off, and finished projects leave behind a searchable, archived record. It's a bet that the winning AI coding platform won't be the smartest agent, but the venue where humans and agents already meet. Via TLDR AI and TLDR IT.

Your Meetings Now Do Your Agents' Homework. Anthropic is testing a Mac-first feature called Project Parka that listens in on meetings, produces speaker-attributed transcripts, and turns them directly into runnable prompts for Claude's agents. It's not yet clear whether those agents act automatically or wait for a nod first, but the direction is unmistakable: the meeting itself becomes the input, and follow-up work can start before anyone's typed a word. Via TLDR AI.

A Chinese Model Jumps Sharply on Security Benchmarks. Z.ai released GLM-5.3, and on two closely watched security benchmarks the jump was steep: its score on CyberGym rose from seventy-seven point two percent to eighty-four point five percent, and on ExploitBench it more than doubled, from twenty-four point four to fifty-four point four. In tests with security teams in China, the model surfaced over two thousand vulnerabilities across two hundred and sixty-nine projects. It's a useful marker of how fast frontier coding models are also becoming frontier offensive-security tools, for better and worse. Via TLDR Information Security.

Now, over to startups and venture.

Italy's Answer to Big Language Models Tops a Billion. Domyn, the Italian company building its own large language models, has raised one point one billion dollars in debt and equity over two years, and founder Uljan Sharka wants to push that to two billion by year end. It's a rare case of a European company chasing frontier-model scale rather than ceding the category entirely to the United States and China, and it lands in the same week Sifted's data team reported that European robotics startups alone raised more in the first half of this year than in the previous two years combined. Via Sifted.

Revolut's Founder Bets Big on an AI Stock-Picker. QuantumLight, the venture firm co-founded by Revolut's Nik Storonsky, closed its second fund at five hundred million dollars, oversubscribed and hitting a hard cap just fifteen months after its first two-hundred-and-fifty-million-dollar fund. The firm leans on an AI system called Aleph that reportedly sifts ten billion data points to hunt for deals, and claims it can move from product demo to term sheet within twenty-four hours. Its portfolio already includes the vibe-coding platform Lovable, Revolut alumnus Alan Chang's Fuse Energy, and the San Francisco-based Applied Compute. Via Sifted.

The Midas List Has a Rival, and the Rankings Don't Agree. Stanford's Ilya Strebulaev built a data-driven alternative to Forbes' Midas List, scoring nearly thirteen thousand venture investors on value actually captured after dilution, cost, and a three-year decay factor, rather than headline deals. Sequoia comes out on top, but the more striking finding is how little the two lists overlap: when Strebulaev tried to reverse-engineer the Midas List from its own published method, forty-nine of his best-fit top one hundred still didn't appear on the real thing. Counting unicorns barely helps either, since one firm with over a hundred unicorns still ranks below a firm with fewer than fifty, because concentrated, capital-efficient bets score higher than a scattershot of small checks. Via The VC Corner.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

Cybercrime's Twenty-Billion-Dollar Bill. CSIS's weekly numbers round-up put a figure on America's cybercrime problem: over twenty billion dollars lost annually, serious enough to be named a national security threat in the twenty twenty-three US National Cyber Strategy. The same briefing flagged that the US has now struck more than thirteen thousand Iranian military and industrial targets in Operation Fury, a reminder that air strikes alone haven't ended that conflict and land power still matters. On a gentler note, the World Food Programme's AI tool, called Scout, saved around six point two million dollars over eighteen months by timing food purchases and storage decisions more precisely. Via CSIS.

Washington Woos Windhoek Over Rare Earths. CSIS's Critical Minerals Security Program spent the week on Africa, hosting a dinner in Windhoek on US-Namibia cooperation in mining and energy, attended by the US ambassador and Namibia's own mining and capital-markets leadership, and publishing an interview on whether critical minerals could reset the US-South Africa relationship. Both point to the same strategic problem: breaking China's grip on rare earth processing takes more than new mines, it takes new demand-side commitments too, since supply-side policy alone hasn't been enough. Via CSIS.

Now, over to community, events and marketing.

One idea worth stealing: go and work inside your customer's business. Founder Ruslan Leteyski watched Shopify quietly lock down the checkout access his eight-million-dollar business depended on, killing it overnight. His next venture, an AI sales agent called Zipchat, is nearing two million dollars in annual revenue instead. The sharper lesson came from a separate founder in the same newsletter: Jonathan Weinberg took a part-time job inside one of his own customers' businesses when money ran short, and discovered his software was solving the wrong problem entirely. Repositioning around what he actually saw on the job took his company to eighty thousand dollars a month. Via Indie Hackers.

One idea worth stealing: shout quietly. This month's the DO Lectures newsletter is built around unsung effort, the theme they call "shout quietly." It points to Sylvia Bloom, a legal secretary who quietly built a nine-million-dollar fortune over sixty-seven years and left most of it to send disadvantaged young people to college, and to Chuck Feeney, who gave away billions and insisted, for years, that his gifts stay anonymous. The takeaway for any community builder: recognition and impact don't have to travel together. Via The DO Lectures.

And now, to close, one interesting thing.

Economist Tyler Cowen points to a Wall Street Journal piece on founders who now feel obliged to stay awake whenever their AI agents might need a nudge, some running from half past seven in the morning to two the next. An agent stuck for eight hours feels like an unbearable waste. One founder describes it as a kind of fear of missing out, where every idle minute means a week's worth of unclaimed agent output slipping away. Cowen's dry response: we will get an AI leisure dividend eventually, just not yet. Via Marginal Revolution.
