Good morning, and welcome to Byte-sized. It is Wednesday the eleventh of November, and today there are six sections, covering everything since Saturday, beginning, as ever, in Japan and the Asia Pacific.

Section one, Japan and Asia Pacific.

First, Fukuoka, where immigration has become a local election issue. The city's mayoral candidates are setting out sharply different visions for foreign residents, ranging from calls for tighter restrictions to explicit pledges against discrimination. It is a useful signal that the national debate on foreign workers and residents is now playing out at city level, in one of Japan's most internationally minded startup cities. In the same update, Fukuoka Airport posted record domestic and international traffic, Kyushu posted record September exports, Kitakyushu Airport is adding extra flights to Taipei and Seoul, and Fukuoka plans to try electronic voting at next spring's assembly election. Via Fukuoka Now.

Second, the regional chip story that dominated the week. Nvidia's chief executive, Jensen Huang, has apologised for an affair with a former Singapore-based executive whom American intelligence says was working for China's Ministry of State Security. She worked for Megaspeed, an obscure Singaporean firm that went on a four point eight billion dollar spree for advanced chips, often through affiliates across Southeast Asia, and which United States officials now describe as a front for getting chips into China. Nvidia's board has launched an independent review, and Huang and the company now face a shareholder class action. The wider point for Asia is that Washington is likely to tighten oversight of chip sales routed through Southeast Asia, and may start treating key chip firms more like defence contractors. Via International Intrigue, and The Rundown A I.

Third, a quieter story worth knowing. Witness accounts suggest Vietnamese intelligence may have abducted Australian, Norwegian and Vietnamese pro-democracy activists from neighbouring Cambodia in September; they now face terrorism charges in Vietnam. International Intrigue's read is that Hanoi is calculating that neighbours and Western partners, keen to keep Vietnam close as they move supply chains out of China, will not push back. Via International Intrigue.

Now, over to A I and tech.

Section two, A I and tech.

The big launch: Anthropic has released Claude Fable five point two and Mythos five point two. They are about twenty per cent cheaper and up to thirty per cent faster than the previous version, and Fable has gone straight to the top of the Artificial Analysis intelligence leaderboard, just ahead of Google's still unreleased Gemini four Carbon. Mythos is limited to vetted security and life sciences groups, while Fable is the same model with stronger guardrails. Anthropic describes its approach as pacing, not pausing, deliberately holding back some capabilities that would speed up A I development itself. Via The Rundown A I, and T L D R A I.

Meta is going after the budget end. Its new small model, Muse Ember, is built for the high-volume routine steps inside A I agents, such as tool calls, coding and document parsing. Meta says it matches Anthropic's Haiku model on quality at roughly a third of the cost per task, mainly by using fewer tokens per job, and it is reportedly building an enterprise sales push through the big consulting firms. For businesses running agents at scale, cost per task is becoming the number that matters. Via The Rundown A I.

Google, meanwhile, announced two new specialised A I chips for next year: one that keeps data encrypted even while a model is working on it, aimed at governments and regulated firms, and one built for very long context work. Google now sells its chips to nearly a hundred outside customers, including Anthropic and Apple, and every available chip is spoken for. The timing, the day after Nvidia's troubles, was not lost on anyone. Via The Rundown A I.

And a sign of how physical A I has become: Anthropic has reportedly bought one point seven gigawatts of Caterpillar gas generators for three point four billion dollars, to get around power shortages and long grid connection queues for its data centres. The Wall Street Journal also reports Anthropic is preparing to press ahead with its stock market listing despite market jitters. Via T L D R A I, and The Rundown A I.

Now, over to startups and venture.

Section three, startups and venture.

OpenAI has reportedly raised eleven billion dollars at a one point two five trillion dollar valuation, with the round possibly reaching twenty billion before it closes. That is well short of the thirty billion it was previously reported to be seeking, which is being read as a sign of growing investor caution about A I valuations. Via T L D R A I.

Lenny Rachitsky's latest tech job market report has some surprises. Open product manager roles are at a near four-year high, while engineering openings have stalled and design roles are at a record low. The hottest titles are forward deployed engineers, up nearly fivefold in a year, and security engineers. The words L L M and Claude are now among the most requested skills in product manager job descriptions, fifteen per cent of all open tech roles are in the San Francisco Bay Area, and only fourteen per cent offer remote work, the lowest on record. Via Lenny's Newsletter.

In Europe, two deals worth noting. Amsterdam-based Onodrim raised three hundred million dollars to buy up existing defence, space and industrial manufacturing startups, a sign that defence roll-ups are heating up. And British digital bank Monzo is reportedly in talks to raise up to five hundred million pounds at around a seven billion pound valuation, weeks after takeover talks apparently collapsed. Via Sifted.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

Section four, policy and geopolitics.

Christine Lagarde is leaving the European Central Bank in February, months early. The reasoning, according to International Intrigue, is to lock in a steady, technocratic successor before a wave of European elections in twenty twenty-seven, including in France, where Marine Le Pen is now favourite and has already called on the bank to buy French debt. The race is between two near-identical centrist central bankers, Spain's Pablo Hernández de Cos and the Netherlands' Klaas Knot, with leaders hoping to decide by Christmas. Via International Intrigue.

On Iran, President Trump says the military part of the war is effectively over, while Secretary of State Rubio insists the two sides are nowhere near a breakthrough. Markets chose to believe the optimistic version, with Brent crude down to around ninety-one dollars. Separately, American, Russian and Ukrainian delegates resumed talks in Abu Dhabi, with Washington reportedly asking whether Moscow would accept a NATO-style Western guarantee for Ukraine. Via International Intrigue.

And in Washington after last week's midterm losses, the President has replaced his chief of staff, Susie Wiles, with the immigration hardliner Stephen Miller. The read: no appetite for compromise ahead of two years of hostile House oversight, and possibly a tilt in priorities towards security at home and in the Americas rather than big diplomatic prizes further afield. Via International Intrigue.

Now, over to community, events and marketing.

Section five, community, events and marketing.

One idea worth stealing, from Skift Meetings. Event platform RainFocus has launched a connector that gives Claude access to event data across seventeen areas of event management and, with permission, the ability to change it. That opens the door to adjusting an event in real time rather than reading a spreadsheet after it is over, but it forces a question every organiser will soon need an answer to: how much attendee data are you willing to hand to an A I agent? The same issue has a practical scheduling tip: if much of your audience is in their mid-forties to early sixties, many of them are juggling children and ageing parents, so ending a day early or offering a day pass may do more for attendance than another big-name speaker. Via Skift.

And from Priya Parker, a reminder for the holiday season: when a family or group changes, its traditions often stop working, and forcing them to stay the same can make things worse. Her advice is to ask what a tradition is actually for, then keep, edit or cut parts of it, starting small rather than overhauling everything. It applies just as well to a team offsite or a community's annual gathering. Via Priya Parker.

A small local format worth borrowing: Fukuoka Now runs a Kyushu Roundtable for the international community, each time on one theme with one guest commentator. The next, on the fourth of December, is about cycling and urban mobility, with the head of public policy at a bike-share company, followed by a year-end bonenkai party. A single theme, one expert and a social afterwards is a simple recipe for a recurring community event. Via Fukuoka Now.

Now, over to one interesting thing.

Section six, one interesting thing.

Why Is This Interesting makes the case for the solo diner. The New York restaurateur Keith McNally insists that people eating alone at his restaurants are looked after and sent a glass of something sparkling, and Will Guidara made going above and beyond for solo guests a rule at Eleven Madison Park, because someone dining alone is saying the restaurant is their main event. The deeper point is that treating the smallest booking as the most important one is the essence of hospitality, and that applies to any host, not just restaurants. Via Why Is This Interesting.
