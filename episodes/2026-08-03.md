# Byte-sized — Monday, 3 August

Hello, and welcome to Byte-sized for Monday, the third of August. Today's episode runs across six sections, from Asia-Pacific news through to a closing idea. Let's get started.

Japan and Asia-Pacific.

First, to Taiwan, where the Center for Strategic and International Studies reports that China now attempts an astonishing two point six million cyber intrusions each day against the island's critical infrastructure. CSIS analyst Andrew Faulhaber argues Taiwan urgently needs what he calls an AI cyber shield to keep pace, warning that traditional, human staffed defences can no longer match the sheer scale of automated, AI powered probing. The piece frames this squarely as an invasion risk issue, not just a routine security concern, given how much of Taiwan's government and infrastructure now runs digitally. Via CSIS.

Taiwan's economy, meanwhile, is red hot. New figures highlighted by economist Tyler Cowen show the island posted annualised gross domestic product growth of fourteen per cent in the first half of twenty twenty six, a startling number for a mature, developed economy. The growth is driven largely by explosive global demand for the advanced chips and AI hardware that Taiwanese firms manufacture, underlining just how central the island has become to the entire artificial intelligence build out. Via Marginal Revolution.

That AI hardware boom is reshaping trade well beyond Asia too. The Financial Times reports that Mexico has quietly become a cornerstone of the AI server supply chain, with Taiwanese manufacturers rapidly expanding factories there to gain tariff free, geographically close access to American data centres. Taiwan remains the largest exporter of servers to the United States this year, at fifty three and a half billion dollars, but Mexico is closing in fast at forty six point nine billion, and Taiwan has jumped from Mexico's eighth largest trading partner back in twenty twenty two to its third largest today. Jesse Rogers, head of Latin America economics at Moody's Analytics, called it a new frontier of collaboration, and even dependency, between the two economies. Via Marginal Revolution.

And in China, the commercial space sector keeps swelling. CSIS researchers Kari Bingen, Aidan Powers-Riggs and Chantal Li note that since Beijing opened parts of its space industry to private investment back in twenty fourteen, the country has gone from fewer than a dozen private space firms to roughly six hundred today, a sign of how deliberately Beijing is building out a parallel, state backed commercial space ecosystem to rival the likes of SpaceX. Via CSIS.

Now, over to AI and tech.

Every's newsletter this week argues that the most effective way to work with artificial intelligence isn't to treat it as one generalist assistant, but as a team of specialists, each suited to a different kind of task, echoing how a well run company delegates rather than expecting one person to do everything. The same issue points to OpenAI's push to remake software development itself, alongside a growing library of voice driven workflows that let builders dictate code and prompts rather than type them. Every also flagged its own Builder Pack, a bundle of AI tools for subscribers now worth more than nine thousand dollars in credits, including a new design canvas called Paper Pro that turns visual work directly into code. Via Every.

On the product side, Lenny's Newsletter sat down with Tom Verrilli, chief product officer at the live shopping platform Whatnot, now the fastest growing marketplace business in American history with over eight billion dollars in gross merchandise value. Verrilli says Whatnot was built on the premise that the company regrets product management needing to exist at all, and describes how artificial intelligence is pushing teams toward fewer, more senior individual contributors doing the real work themselves, rather than layers of process. He also talks through what he calls his play the accordion mental model for knowing when a team should expand and when it should contract, drawn from his earlier years as chief product officer at Twitch. Via Lenny's Newsletter.

And The Rundown, the AI newsletter read by over two million people, has launched a Community AI Workflow Hub after readers said they wanted to see how ordinary people, not executives or influencers, are actually using artificial intelligence day to day. The hub lets readers browse real prompts and workflows by the problem they solve, copy the steps, and put them to work the same day, with no maze of channels or features to get lost in. The best submission each day gets featured in front of the newsletter's full readership, alongside a link to the contributor's LinkedIn profile. Via The Rundown.

Now, over to startups and venture.

The VC Corner flags a piece of research warning of what it calls the AI layoff trap. Individual companies save money by automating jobs away, but as consumer spending shrinks across the economy, profits eventually weaken for everyone, including the firms doing the automating. The researchers argue this cannot be fixed through redistribution alone, because any single company still benefits from replacing one more worker, even as the collective effect turns corrosive. Via The VC Corner.

The same newsletter also points to a shifting venture landscape, from a growing market for VC secondaries, which let early investors cash out stakes before a company exits, to fresh thinking on seed stage investing. First Round Capital's Liz Wessel is running a session on how her firm evaluates founders before there's any real traction or product market fit to point to, a reminder that early conviction is still mostly about people, not metrics. Via The VC Corner.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

CSIS has a sobering set of numbers on the state of American air defences. Following the resumption of combat operations in what CSIS terms Operation Epic Fury, Patriot interceptor inventories have fallen to somewhere between seven hundred and fifty nine and eight hundred and twenty seven missiles, with stocks now under one thousand, and THAAD interceptor inventories down to around two hundred and fifty. Analysts Mark Cancian and Chris Park warn that a renewed war with Iran would test these already diminished stockpiles severely, since both systems have already been drawn down heavily during the current fighting. Via CSIS.

Further south, CSIS notes that Latin American and Caribbean governments are moving to shore up their own defences against weaponised drones. In a report titled Autonomy and Affordable Mass in Latin America and the Caribbean, analyst Henry Ziemer details how Brazil's army has submitted a plan to invest four hundred and fifty six billion reais, around eighty eight point seven billion dollars, between now and twenty forty, to strengthen its air defences and counter the growing threat posed by armed drones in the region. Via CSIS.

Now, over to community, events and marketing.

One idea worth stealing this week comes from Priya Parker, author and facilitator, who is teaching a new workshop concept from her forthcoming book The Art of Fighting, which she calls healthy heat. The idea is that the groups and communities that last aren't the ones that avoid conflict, but the ones that know how to hold tense, honest conversations with enough care that they become clarifying rather than destructive. Parker's observation is that most groups oscillate between two failure modes, avoiding disagreement altogether or letting it explode, and that the groups which actually grow and last are the ones that learn to sit in the middle. If you run any kind of community, the lesson is to build in structured space for disagreement, rather than leaving it to chance. Via Priya Parker's newsletter.

And a sharp growth tactic from the newsletter Growth In Reverse, whose author also used this issue to announce she's cancelling her podcast after eighty three episodes to focus on building tools for other newsletter owners instead, a useful reminder that knowing when to stop a project is its own skill. The tactic itself comes from a creator called ADHD Weasel, who launched a Substack newsletter early last year and, within eighteen months, built thirty six thousand free subscribers and over eight hundred paying ones, plus a hundred thousand dollars in subscription revenue. A big driver was a simple quiz used as a lead magnet, which alone brought in two thousand eight hundred new subscribers in twenty three days, lifting their daily sign up rate more than sevenfold. The takeaway for anyone building an audience, a well designed quiz can outperform almost any other opt in. Via Growth In Reverse.

And finally, one interesting thing.

The Do Lectures' newsletter this week is themed around an old idea called festina lente, or hurry slowly, the personal motto of the Roman emperor Augustus. The Venetian printer Aldus Manutius illustrated the same idea with a dolphin wrapped around an anchor, speed bound to stability. Warren Buffett made a similar point in his own way, describing how the great baseball hitter Ted Williams only swung at pitches inside his happy zone, and how Buffett used that same discipline to sit out the dot com bubble entirely. The common thread, moving fast matters far less than knowing when not to move at all. Via The Do Lectures.

That's Byte-sized for Monday the third of August. Thanks for listening, and I'll be back tomorrow.
