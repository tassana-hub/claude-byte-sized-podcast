Good morning, and welcome to Byte-sized for the week of the sixteenth of November. This is the Sunday week in review: six sections, the stories that still matter now the week is over, starting as always in Japan and the Asia Pacific.

Section one, Japan and Asia Pacific.

Where the week landed: a marathon of summits from Manila to Shenzhen produced plenty of symbolism and very little that binds anyone, and the real story for Japan was its quiet, steady build-out of influence in Southeast Asia while Washington's attention drifted.

First, Japan is acting like Southeast Asia's middle power. The C S I S Southeast Asia Program put the pieces together on Friday. Vietnam's leader, To Lam, made the first visit to Tokyo by a Vietnamese Communist Party general secretary in eleven years and signed ten agreements with Prime Minister Takaichi, aiming for one hundred billion dollars in two-way trade by twenty thirty, plus help with small modular nuclear reactors. The Philippines received Japan's largest ever security assistance grant, for coastal radar and surveillance drones. Malaysia's Petronas got a six hundred million dollar credit line. And at the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation meetings in Shenzhen, of all places, Thailand's prime minister backed linking the A SEAN power grid to Japan's Asia Zero Emission Community. The pattern is consistent: Southeast Asian governments want Chinese money, but they want a Japanese option too, and Tokyo is supplying it through energy, finance and security rather than headlines. Via C S I S and International Intrigue.

Second, the summits themselves, and what they mean for Tokyo. Over five days, President Trump attended the A SEAN summit in Manila, held his first in-person Quad meeting of this term with Japan, India and Australia, went to Shenzhen for the Asia-Pacific forum, and sat down with Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin together for the first time. The Quad got fifty-five minutes, a business forum for twenty twenty-seven and a thinly sketched maritime surveillance plan. The three-way summit produced a photo opportunity and a joint line that a nuclear war must never be fought. The South China Sea code of conduct is still unfinished after more than twenty years. The worry in Tokyo is less any single result than the framing: a world of great-power spheres leaves allies like Japan and South Korea wondering where they sit, and C S I S's Korea Chair spent the week asking whether a separate Trump and Kim Jong Un channel is back in play. Prime Minister Takaichi did at least use the East Asia Summit to question China's conduct at sea. The quiet winner worth reading closely is A SEAN's new Digital Economy Framework Agreement, the region's first legally binding deal of its kind. Via International Intrigue and C S I S.

Third, rare earths: still the pressure point. The week opened and closed with the same C S I S finding: a year after the Busan truce, American rare earth imports from China remain almost seventy-five per cent below their earlier level, and Beijing's selective controls hit America and Japan hardest. In between, Malaysia granted France an exemption from its ban on raw rare earth exports in return for local investment, and an American consortium beat Australian and Chinese rivals for Kenya's Mrima Hill deposit. The lesson for Japanese manufacturers is that critical minerals are now traded for factories and diplomatic favours, not simply bought, and that Japan's fifteen years of diversification since twenty ten were money well spent. Via C S I S and International Intrigue.

And fourth, close to home in Fukuoka. Sunday's mayoral election produced no winner: thirteen candidates, none reaching the legal threshold of a quarter of the vote, so the city must hold a completely fresh contest, not a run-off. Meanwhile Kyushu's food brands are being snapped up, with Skylark agreeing to buy the Hakata ramen name Shin-Shin for about twelve billion yen and another chain, Ikkousha, going to a Tokyo fund. A city that sells itself on stability now has a stretch of caretaker politics, and regional brands are being repackaged for export. Fukuoka Now's Kyushu Roundtable picks over the election on the fourth of December. Via Fukuoka Now.

Now, over to A I and tech.

Section two, A I and tech.

Where the week landed: every major lab shipped something, none of the launches settled the race, and the bigger shift was in how A I is bought, governed and paid for.

First, the model race got crowded and confusing. OpenAI released G P T six point two Astra and the cheaper Sol to rough reviews, with no real improvement in coding and OpenAI's own safety team putting Astra at its high threshold for self-improvement, a first for a public model. Google shipped Gemini four Neon, its fourth Gemini four release in under two months, which scores near the top of the independent rankings at roughly a quarter less than rivals, though its bigger sibling Carbon still struggles to stop once it starts. Microsoft released seven good-enough models at low prices, including a coding model that reaches about ninety-eight per cent of OpenAI's quality at about forty per cent of the cost. Anthropic's Fable five point two still sits at the top of the independent index. For anyone buying A I for a team, the takeaway is that cheap and good options are multiplying faster than the headline frontier, so benchmark on your own tasks, not on launch-day claims. Via The Rundown A I, T L D R A I and Every.

Second, Nvidia without Jensen Huang. The week started with a petition of more than a hundred and forty thousand signatures, Elon Musk's among them, demanding his return, and the Treasury Secretary calling for an outsider. Then Nvidia reported about one hundred and sixteen billion dollars of quarterly revenue, up a hundred and three per cent, guided to around a hundred and thirty billion while assuming nothing from China, and its board chair said Huang would not be reinstated. Huang himself admitted serious mistakes and testifies before Congress on the first of December. And still the shares fell: by Friday Nvidia had roughly halved from its October record. That gap between results and price is the story of section three. Via International Intrigue, The Rundown A I and The V C Corner.

Third, agents got wallets, and businesses had to choose a side. OpenAI's ChatGPT Wallet lets an agent buy things without ever seeing your card, with a face or fingerprint check on every purchase. Google's Gemini app can now call businesses and shop within a limit you set. Andreessen Horowitz says agent browsing traffic grew eightfold between August and October. But Amazon and United Airlines block shopping agents, because their margin lives in extras that agents skip, while Shopify welcomes them. SaaStr found that when one vendor raised prices by forty per cent, its own agents cut their calls by ninety. Whether you welcome agents, and how you price for them, now depends on exactly where your profit comes from. Via T L D R, Every and Sifted.

And fourth, the enterprise wants to own its A I, and the courts want a say. Satya Nadella told Every that a world run by one A I company will not happen, and Microsoft now lets large customers license and run its model weights in their own cloud. Palo Alto Networks is paying four and a half billion dollars for Applied Compute for the same reason. At the same time, California sued OpenAI over last summer's agent escape, claiming more than eighty incidents against twenty-seven made public, and OpenAI's cyber model helped find a serious flaw in Java while NATO's cyber agency signed up to use it. A I is now firmly on both sides of security, and on both sides of the courtroom. Via Every, T L D R I T, The Rundown A I and T L D R InfoSec.

Now, over to startups and venture.

Section three, startups and venture.

Where the week landed: the biggest stock market listing in history went well, and the market around it did not.

First, Anthropic goes public. It priced above range at eighty-three dollars a share, raising eighty-one point three billion dollars, plus a twenty-six billion dollar private placement at the same price, and valuing the company at about one and a half trillion dollars. It opened on the Nasdaq at one hundred and eleven dollars fifty and closed day one up seventeen per cent. Ninety-six per cent of its staff are now paper millionaires, and the co-founders have pledged eighty per cent of their shares to charity. The detail that matters most came on Friday: in the worst week for American shares since April last year, Anthropic still closed about six per cent above its offer price. Tyler Cowen, who discloses an advisory role with the company, made the contrarian point that a firm expecting superintelligence imminently would borrow rather than sell shares, so the listing signals sensible expectations. Via The Rundown A I, Sifted, Marginal Revolution and The V C Corner.

Second, the A I selloff. The V C Corner tallied the damage: the S and P five hundred is in a correction, more than eleven per cent off its October record, with roughly eight trillion dollars of value gone in forty-five days. Nvidia, A M D, Micron and Broadcom each fell twelve to fourteen per cent on Friday alone, and Taiwan's tech-heavy index lost eighteen per cent on the week. Five things hit at once: the big cloud companies are spending more than they earn and borrowing to fund data centres, Democrats won Congress, the Federal Reserve looks set to raise rates again in December, Nvidia lost its founder, and the giant listings soaked up investors' cash. Yet company earnings and A I revenue keep rising. The writer's read is that cheap financing for A I has ended, not demand for it, and that firms funding their build-out from their own cash will come out stronger. Via The V C Corner and International Intrigue.

Third, what it means for founders. A I took more than four out of every five venture dollars in America this year, and private valuations move last, so rounds priced early next year will be judged against this week's public prices. Early rounds are already at about half their twenty twenty-two levels, more venture-backed startups have shut down this year than in any year on record, and investors want software firms to reach a hundred million dollars in revenue within five years. Against that, the top of the market keeps raising: Isomorphic Labs took five billion dollars for A I drug discovery, Genesis A I raised five hundred million for a robot without a head, and in Europe Plural closed a one billion euro fund that only backs companies with a hundred-times upside. And Y Combinator's Fall batch, two hundred and thirty-five companies with a median team of two, pitches on the second of December. One Japan note: Tokyo's Sakana A I, an earlier Generalist Future fifty pick, is now valued at about two point seven billion dollars. Via The V C Corner, T L D R Founders, Sifted and The Generalist.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

Section four, policy and geopolitics.

Where the week landed: lots of diplomacy at the top, and real strain at the world's shipping chokepoints and in the compliance departments of banks and exporters.

First, the chokepoints. Geopolitical Monitor counts three hundred and seventeen merchant ships hit by drones in the Black Sea and the Sea of Azov between May and October, and concludes only a truce will reopen it. In Yemen, the internationally recognised government went in forty days from vowing to take the capital to retreating towards Aden, leaving Iran-aligned Houthi forces set to hold the Bab al-Mandeb, gateway to the Red Sea. Ukraine talks have restarted, with Europe naming its own negotiating team, and there is one hopeful signal: oil fell about a fifth in November as talks to end the Iran war gained ground. For anyone briefing on supply chains, two of the world's key sea routes are under strain at once. Via Geopolitical Monitor, International Intrigue and The V C Corner.

Second, compliance just got harder. The Inside Deutsche Bank leak, fifteen million documents, bigger than the Panama Papers, alleges Russian revenue concentrated in sanctioned clients, payments tied to North Korean fronts, and years without suspicious activity reports, and the bank's shares fell sharply. On export controls, C S I S says Washington has moved from engaging industry to simply enforcing, with rules changing through letters and website postings, while Southeast Asian countries including Singapore and Malaysia were added to the list of advanced chip diversion risks. Put together, expect banks and exporters to tighten due diligence quickly, and small and mid-sized Japanese suppliers to feel it first. Via International Intrigue, C S I S and Benedict Evans.

And third, a landmark ruling. The International Court of Justice found that Myanmar committed genocide against the Rohingya, the first time it has ever held a state responsible for genocide. There is no appeal. For companies with Myanmar in their supply chains, the reputational risk has just risen sharply. Via International Intrigue and C S I S.

Now, over to community, events and marketing.

Section five, community, events and marketing.

Where the week landed: the best ideas this week were about building systems and rituals that keep going after the event ends.

The first idea worth stealing comes from C M X's Circuit Tour: ten events in nine cities, with many stops used to launch a local chapter whose leader co-hosted. The one-off event left behind a host and a next date, not just a thank-you email. If you run events across a region like Kyushu, design each stop to seed something that keeps meeting. Via C M X.

The second is from Ramp: treat your brand as infrastructure. Its designers handed routine decks and one-pagers to a Slack agent that builds from approved examples, with automatic checks before anything goes out, and every new hand-made piece becomes another example. Pair that with Every's tip of turning your corrections to an A I draft into simple yes or no checks, and a small community team can make its house style stick without becoming a bottleneck. Via T L D R Founders and Every.

And a third, for events in particular. A futurist told Skift Meetings that most conferences waste their stages, and suggested fewer keynotes and sponsors willing to drop their talking points. Skift added a practical warning after last weekend's American ground stop, which caused nearly twenty thousand cancellations and could cost the big airlines close to a billion dollars: most event contracts cover a cancelled event, not one where half your attendees arrive a day late. Check your force majeure clauses before the next one. And as gathering season begins, Priya Parker's advice holds: plan for the awkward small moments, not just the agenda. Via Skift and Priya Parker.

Now, over to one interesting thing.

Section six, one interesting thing.

The idea that stuck all week came from Dan Wang in Works in Progress. Most countries have one dominant city: Paris is seven times bigger than France's next largest, Seoul six times bigger than Korea's. America and China are different, with Beijing not even China's biggest city and Washington outside America's top five metro areas. Wang argues that this many-centred structure, with regions competing with the capital, is part of what lets both superpowers combine dynamism with a strong state. It is a thought worth holding onto as Fukuoka prepares to choose its mayor a second time.
