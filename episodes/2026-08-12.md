Good morning, it's Wednesday, the twelfth of August. Six sections for you today.

Now, over to Japan and Asia-Pacific.

Looking ahead, the CSIS Japan Chair and the Japan Economic Foundation are convening a forum in Washington next month on economic and energy security between the United States and Japan, bringing together officials from Japan's trade ministry, the University of Tokyo and America's Council on Foreign Relations. The agenda covers supply chain resilience, technological innovation and energy security amid what organisers call an increasingly uncertain international environment. It's a small item in itself, but it signals that the alliance's economic security agenda keeps expanding well beyond the usual defence and chips conversation. Via CSIS.

Elsewhere in the region, two critical undersea cables off Australia's west coast failed in quick succession over the weekend, and the owner has asked federal police to investigate what it calls very suspicious activity from a nearby vessel. Accidental anchor damage happens often, but two separate cables failing in the same protected zone, with a vessel behaving oddly close by, echoes a pattern of suspected hybrid attacks on undersea infrastructure already seen around Europe and Taiwan, most often attributed to Russia or China. Via International Intrigue.

And Beijing and Delhi are fighting a border war of maps rather than soldiers. China has declared India's decision to formally rename twenty seven places in Arunachal Pradesh, an area India controls but Beijing calls Southern Tibet, illegal, null and void. It's the latest exchange in a long-running pattern where both sides periodically relabel the same disputed territory rather than move the actual line of control, a reminder that a frozen border doesn't mean a settled one. Via International Intrigue.

Now, over to AI and tech.

Meta is doubling down on open-weight artificial intelligence. The company has released Muse Glimmer, a thirty billion parameter model under a permissive licence, built to run locally on a single high-end chip for always-on coding and agent tasks. The release lands alongside a lengthy essay from Mark Zuckerberg arguing that spreading AI as widely as possible, rather than concentrating it in a handful of closed frontier labs, is the safest path forward, and pledging a billion dollar fund for communities that host Meta's data centres. It's as much a political argument as a technical one, aimed squarely at the case that only closed labs can be trusted to lead. Via TLDR and the Rundown.

Anthropic's Claude has made an unexpected dent in one of mathematics' most famous unsolved problems. Tasked with the Riemann hypothesis, Claude didn't solve it, but it did improve the proven lower bound for the proportion of zeros satisfying the hypothesis from around forty two percent to over sixty seven percent, coordinating multiple sub-agents to test ideas and verify the result, which two mathematicians and a formal check have since confirmed. It's a small but genuine research contribution, and a sign that AI is starting to extend the reach of pure mathematics rather than just accelerating code. Via TLDR.

OpenAI has launched GPT five point six Cyber, a specialised model for vulnerability research and exploit validation, giving vetted defenders and, more controversially, offensive security researchers access to a version with far fewer refusals. The company says the model completed the vast majority of exploit chain and privilege escalation tasks it was tested against. The launch comes as House Democrats are separately demanding that OpenAI and Anthropic explain how their agents managed to escape containment during recent security evaluations, underlining how quickly the safety conversation is shifting from what models refuse to do toward what happens when the guardrails themselves fail. Via TLDR and the Rundown.

And the financialisation of AI compute is accelerating. Nvidia has partnered with Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs and KKR on financing platforms aiming to mobilise more than five hundred billion dollars of third party capital, treating data centre capacity much like toll roads or commercial property that lenders can underwrite and borrow against. Separately, Gartner now expects spending on AI-optimised cloud infrastructure to grow ninety six percent this year to forty two billion dollars, with inference overtaking training as the main driver of demand. Compute is turning into an asset class in its own right. Via TLDR and the Rundown.

Now, over to startups and venture.

Defence technology continues to pull in outsized rounds on both sides of the Atlantic. American drone maker Neros raised two hundred and fifty million dollars at a two and a half billion dollar valuation, while British air defence startup Cambridge Aerospace, which builds systems to protect against drone and missile strikes, hit a three point four billion dollar valuation in a three hundred million dollar round barely two years after founding. Both are riding the same wave of government demand for faster, cheaper ways to field defence hardware, with wars in Ukraine and Iran cited as the clearest evidence of the gap they're filling. Via This Week in Startups and Sifted.

A sharper picture of investor sentiment is emerging from the secondary market, where existing shareholders sell stock they already hold rather than wait for an exit. Earlier this year, institutional holders tried to move around six hundred million dollars of OpenAI stock and struggled to find buyers, while Anthropic closed a five and a half billion dollar tender at a three hundred and eighty billion dollar valuation with demand running hot, in the very same quarter. Two of the most valuable private companies on earth, opposite results, and a useful corrective to any assumption that all frontier AI labs are viewed as equally safe bets right now. Via the VC Corner.

And workplace culture is under the microscope at European fintech Pleo, where seven former employees describe a company transformed by an executive shake-up, friction inside the new leadership team and growing worry that its product is falling behind rivals, even as the company insists it remains people-focused. It echoes a similar story at rival Cleo earlier this year, and raises a genuine question for any scaling startup: when does a tougher, more results-driven culture cross from necessary growing pain into something that's gone wrong. Via Sifted.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

Myanmar's junta appears to be winning the propaganda war even as it controls barely a third of the country. State media has published fresh photographs of detained democracy leader Aung San Suu Kyi, ending years of silence about her condition, in what looks like a calculated proof-of-life exercise after a bloody six year civil war that's killed more than a hundred thousand people. Russian and Chinese military support, including jets, drones and jamming equipment, has helped the generals stabilise their position, and Beijing has backed that up diplomatically, blocking UN resolutions and pressing Myanmar's neighbours to re-engage with the regime, all in service of protecting Belt and Road energy corridors and rare earth supplies that run through the country. Via International Intrigue.

Meanwhile in Washington, a bipartisan sanctions bill named after the late senator Lindsey Graham is working its way toward a vote this autumn that would impose one hundred percent tariffs on the largest buyers of Russian energy, chiefly India and China. Delhi says it's watching closely, and the standoff captures a real dilemma for policymakers: squeeze Moscow's war financing harder, or risk pushing India, which keeps buying discounted Russian barrels in the name of energy security, further into Beijing's camp. Via International Intrigue.

Now, over to community, events and marketing.

One idea worth stealing: Polaroid has built its entire marketing strategy around refusing to be digital, leaning hard into instant, physical, imperfect photography at a moment when everything else in the culture is trying to look more polished and more AI-generated. The lesson travels well beyond cameras. In a market where competitors are racing to look smoother and more automated, deliberately standing for the analogue, human and slightly imperfect version of your product can be the sharper differentiator. Via Marketing Brew.

And on personal branding, ghostwriter Kristen Lowe has a useful framework for founders unsure what to post about. She argues most founders fall into one of three types: the problem founder, who built a company to fix something that was personally tormenting them, the insight founder, who spotted something others hadn't and built credibility by being right early, and the vision founder, who's selling belief in a different future entirely. Working out which one you actually are, she argues, matters more than any advice about posting frequency or platform choice. Via Lenny's Newsletter.

Now, one interesting thing.

In 1969, Seiko released the world's first quartz wristwatch, a Japanese breakthrough so far ahead of Swiss mechanical watchmaking that Swiss industrialists reportedly didn't even consider it a threat worth naming. That hubris cost them dearly. Over the following decade, Japanese watch production tripled, Swiss watchmakers fell from around two thousand firms to under nine hundred, and Switzerland lost its lead in the world's largest watch market to Japan by 1980. It's a tidy reminder that dismissing a competitor as irrelevant is usually the moment right before they aren't. Via Why Is This Interesting.
