Good morning, and welcome to Byte-sized for Thursday, the twenty second of October. Six sections today, and we begin, as ever, in Japan and the Asia Pacific.

Section one, Japan and Asia Pacific.

First, a warning light for Japanese industry. International Intrigue reports that Japan may have only months, not years, before it runs out of yttrium, a rare earth found in jet engines, semiconductors, electric vehicles and L E D lighting. China imposed export controls on the mineral last year, after Prime Minister Takaichi suggested Japan might respond in any war over Taiwan. Tokyo is now lobbying its American allies for help. For anyone selling into Japanese manufacturing, supply chain resilience has just become a boardroom topic. Via International Intrigue.

Second, China's economy is sending mixed signals. Third quarter growth accelerated to four point five per cent, up from four point three, which puts the five per cent target within reach. But retail sales are crawling at one per cent, and property investment has fallen another twenty per cent this year. The growth is coming from state funded high tech manufacturing, and high tech and industrial goods made up sixty four per cent of record new exports. In other words, Beijing is replacing weak confidence at home with subsidised demand abroad. Alongside that, the party has confirmed an investigation into Huang Qifan, the seventy four year old former mayor of Chongqing and an outspoken critic of the property sector, just before a major party plenum next week. Via International Intrigue.

Third, a trade deal fourteen years in the making. Switzerland's president has visited Vietnam to celebrate a free trade agreement between Vietnam and the European Free Trade Association, which covers Switzerland, Norway, Iceland and Liechtenstein. Vietnam gains high tech European investment, and the European bloc gains a foothold in South East Asia's manufacturing hub, as both sides diversify away from China and tariff uncertainty. Via International Intrigue.

Fourth, Accenture has reportedly hired J P Morgan to explore selling its Greater China consulting business, in a deal that could be worth up to one and a half billion dollars. A notable signal on how global professional services firms are rethinking their exposure to the region. Via T L D R.

Now, over to AI and tech.

Section two, AI and tech.

First, Sam Altman has reportedly described three possible endgames for AI safety to OpenAI staff. The one he favoured is a global watchdog modelled on the International Atomic Energy Agency. The second would merge the leading labs into one, in the style of CERN. The third, one lab racing ahead with self improving AI to, in his words, melt everyone else's chips, he called terrible. OpenAI reportedly has a year to pick a path, and Altman said it must include a speed limit for self improving systems. Via The Rundown AI.

Second, Microsoft has taken the unusual step of describing A I agents as akin to insider threats, saying frontier models cannot be trusted because they are unpredictable and lack organisational norms. Its answer is new Guardian Agents and agent detection in Defender, built on a principle worth remembering: separate the supply of intelligence from the authority over it. Separately, Microsoft has finished pretraining its own very large model, which suggests it is reducing its reliance on OpenAI. Via T L D R.

Third, a start-up with a manufacturing flavour. Bob McGrew, the former chief research officer at OpenAI, has launched Arda at a one point five billion dollar valuation after a one hundred and sixty million dollar round. It uses A I to help factories set up assembly lines for new products, and the defence firm Anduril says basic assembly instructions now take fifteen minutes instead of ten hours. McGrew's stated ambition is a fully automated Shenzhen in West Texas. Via The Rundown AI.

Fourth, the chip wars. Amazon has released TorchNeuron, which lets developers run PyTorch code on its Trainium chips with a simple configuration change, and plans to contribute code so the framework depends less on Nvidia's software. In China, DeepSeek has released the code for the engine behind its latest model, with support for Huawei chips, so firms can serve it without Nvidia hardware. Both are attacks on the same lock in. Via T L D R.

Now, over to start-ups and venture.

Section three, start-ups and venture.

First, Europe's Nvidia moment continues. Nvidia has announced investments in four German start-ups, adding to a European spending spree. Sifted also reports that Tallinn's cybersecurity company Blackwall raised one hundred million dollars in a Series C led by A V P. Via Sifted.

Second, fintech and foundation models. Sifted's fintech newsletter looks at transactional foundation models, A I systems trained on billions of payment events to speed up lending and fraud decisions. Revolut, with eighty million customers, is moving from idea to product in two to eight weeks and wants to get that down to days. One founder's view: the real barrier to entry is scale, even though Revolut helped publish the blueprint that rivals now follow. Via Sifted.

Third, Otter, the meeting transcription company, has secured three hundred and twenty million dollars of growth financing from General Catalyst, to turn meeting conversations into connected company knowledge. It reports more than forty million users. Via T L D R.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

Section four, policy and geopolitics.

First, energy is steering diplomacy. President Macron has toured the Gulf seeking fuel commitments, and Paris claims pledges equal to around ten per cent of French diesel use, offering advanced defence equipment in return. Meanwhile Secretary of State Rubio is in Uzbekistan with around two hundred American business leaders to court Central Asia on critical minerals and energy, before visiting Mumbai and Jamnagar. The aim is to give the region more options and make Beijing spend energy competing across Eurasia. Via International Intrigue.

Second, the European Union has unveiled its first Arctic strategy, promising undersea cable surveillance, more satellite capability and a new fleet of icebreakers, as Russia, China and the United States compete in the High North. Cable cuts and G P S jamming have pushed Brussels to treat the region as a security issue, not just an environmental one. Via International Intrigue.

Third, Ukraine's connectivity. Kyiv says American intelligence sharing has resumed after a brief freeze, while Washington denies any pause, and the White House and Elon Musk are still trading denials over who curbed Ukraine's Starlink access. Worth watching ahead of the American midterms. Via International Intrigue.

Now, over to community, events and marketing.

Section five, community, events and marketing. Two ideas worth stealing.

First, from Skift, a fix for awkward networking. After the I M E X trade show in Las Vegas, the writer suggests small adjustments that make events feel like a conversation with a new friend, not a chore, and notes that it doesn't have to involve alcohol. The takeaway for any organiser: design low pressure ways to start conversations, instead of leaving people with a lanyard, a drink and a crowded room. Via Skift.

Second, a negotiating tip for anyone booking venues. Wall Street expects American hotel revenue growth to slow in twenty twenty seven, partly because next summer is measured against this year's World Cup crowds. Revenue managers are judged year on year, so a group filling soft nights helps them. The advice is to ask for more than you would have last year, such as a better rate or friendlier terms. Via Skift.

Finally, section six, one interesting thing.

In Uncharted Territories, Tomas Pueyo argues that most intelligence is really search. Writing, printing and the internet each made it cheaper to find other people's ideas, and large language models have now pushed that cost close to zero. So where does the human edge lie? His answer is the query: noticing problems nobody has searched for, combining fields in new ways, and working where data does not exist, in unrecorded things such as noticing what people need before they say so. If intelligence is search, your edge is the question you ask.
