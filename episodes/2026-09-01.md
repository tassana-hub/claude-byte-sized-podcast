JAPAN AND ASIA-PACIFIC

In Hirosaki, in northern Japan, hundreds of schoolchildren and thousands of adult volunteers spent the weekend hauling on thirty-metre ropes to shift a four hundred tonne castle. It's part of a decade-long restoration using a centuries-old technique called hikiya, where whole wooden buildings are rolled back into position rather than dismantled. The castle moved twenty-seven centimetres by hand, with machines set to cover the remaining seventy-three metres from September. More than eight thousand adults applied for just eighteen hundred volunteer slots across the month, which says something about the pull of a shared civic ritual. Via International Intrigue.

The Center for Strategic and International Studies released a new episode of its industrial base series looking at Australia's weapons sustainment ecosystem, as the United States and its Indo-Pacific partners try to rebuild the capacity to keep militaries supplied through a prolonged crisis. It's a reminder that the region's defence conversation is increasingly about logistics and repair capacity, not just hardware purchases, and that Australia is emerging as a testing ground for how allied supply chains might actually hold up under pressure. Via CSIS.

The annual Pacific Islands Forum summit in Palau was thrown into disarray after last-minute leadership withdrawals from Fiji, Vanuatu, Samoa, Kiribati and the Solomon Islands, several sending junior officials instead. New Zealand's foreign minister openly floated Chinese interference as a factor, given the summit's Taiwan-aligned host and the Beijing-aligned leanings of some no-shows. Whatever the cause, the disruption undercuts the forum's biggest asset, which is regional unity, right as members were meant to be tackling energy security and deep-sea mining. Via International Intrigue.

And in North Korea, Kim Jong Un reshuffled his military leadership, naming a new defence minister and moving the outgoing one to head the munitions department instead. It reads less like a demotion than a doubling down on arms production, which has been propping up North Korea's economy through sales to Russia. Via International Intrigue.

Now, over to AI and tech.

AI AND TECH

OpenAI is cutting its models out of the coding tool Cursor by the twelfth of November, following Cursor's acquisition by SpaceX. OpenAI cited Elon Musk's history of walking away from contracts as the reason, pointing to a scrapped advertising deal after he bought Twitter. Musk's response was that he couldn't care less, while Cursor's chief executive said OpenAI's models make up only a small slice of its overall usage. It's a sharp reminder that the neutral, multi-model coding tools of the last couple of years are getting pulled into the same rivalries as the labs themselves. Via TLDR and The Rundown.

Anthropic published early research on self-improving artificial intelligence, showing that its automated researchers were able to make other models safer with very little human involvement. It's a small but notable glimpse of AI systems taking on a growing share of their own research and development, rather than simply being tools that humans direct. Via TLDR.

Anthropic also confirmed that Claude Code's standard weekly usage limits will rise by twenty five percent from the fourteenth of September for Pro, Max, Team and seat-based Enterprise plans. The catch is that a temporary fifty percent boost expires the day before, so for anyone currently enjoying that boost, real usage will actually drop by around seventeen percent once the new baseline kicks in. Via The Rundown.

And a federal judge ruled that the Pentagon acted unlawfully when it blacklisted Anthropic as a supply chain risk back in June, a designation normally reserved for foreign adversaries that forced its Mythos and Fable models offline for weeks. The judge found the label was retaliatory, punishing Anthropic for pushing back on military use of its models rather than responding to any genuine security threat. The label survives for now pending appeal, and the Pentagon says its own wind-down of Claude usage will finish by the end of September regardless. Via The Rundown.

Now, over to startups and venture.

STARTUPS AND VENTURE

Andreessen Horowitz raised one point one billion dollars for a new fund called Machine Age, a notable pivot for a firm best known for backing software. The money is aimed squarely at AI's physical infrastructure: chips, memory, interconnects, data centres, cooling and power-efficient edge devices, on the theory that the AI buildout is now bottlenecked by hardware and energy rather than by algorithms. Via TLDR.

Stripe and Advent International have walked away from their more than fifty billion dollar bid for PayPal after PayPal's board decided the offer was too low, sending PayPal shares down as much as sixteen percent. Meanwhile identity and fraud company Socure announced a growth investment at a five point two billion dollar valuation and acquired a fraud automation startup called Fravity, reporting three hundred and sixty four million dollars in annual recurring revenue and sixty three percent year-on-year growth. Two very different signals about where investors think the money is safest right now: less appetite for a distressed giant, more for infrastructure that's already compounding. Via TLDR.

And in Germany, just three companies, defence firms Helsing and Quantum Systems plus robotics scale-up Neura Robotics, have raised three point eight three billion euros between them this year, which is forty four percent of all the venture funding raised by every German startup combined. It's the second month in a row that German VC funding has topped two billion euros, a threshold the country had only crossed five times before this summer. The concentration is stark: since Sifted started tracking the data in January of last year, just ten companies have captured more than a third of all German venture funding. Via Sifted.

POLICY AND GEOPOLITICS

The United States and Canada's trade negotiations collapsed again last week, with Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney pulling out of talks and announcing retaliatory tariffs effective the eighth of September. The dispute centres on steel, aluminium and auto tariffs, where the US offer would have simply restored Canada to the zero-tariff status it held for forty years under free trade agreements, while asking Canada to accept permanent US tariffs in return. Trade veterans at CSIS argue this is corroding one of the world's most durable cross-border relationships and could effectively kill the wider North American trade agreement, with real costs for growth and inflation across all three economies. Via CSIS.

And Washington and Caracas announced what President Trump called the biggest oil deal in history: a joint venture giving the US a claimed fifty five percent stake in seventeen major Venezuelan oil fields, covering a fifth of the country's reserves, for a term either side describes very differently, a century according to Trump, twenty five years according to Venezuela. The deal's actual leader isn't a government body but a well-connected private oil producer, and it's drawn criticism from both pro-Chavista and opposition figures in Venezuela who say it hands unelected authorities a very long-term claim on the country's resources. It comes as the US carried out its first strike on Iranian territory in over a month, targeting preparations for mining a Gulf shipping lane, with Iran claiming retaliatory strikes on US bases that local authorities deny were hit. Via International Intrigue.

COMMUNITY, EVENTS AND MARKETING

One idea worth stealing this week: stop treating marketing as something you switch on later. A widely shared essay on The VC Corner argued that most founders are already marketing without realising it, and the mistake is trying to make it look official with an agency or a content calendar before you've worked out what's actually landing. Its sharpest tip is about positioning: write down who you are deliberately not for, because a description that fits everyone persuades no one. And its most practical tip is almost embarrassingly simple, add one optional text box to your signup flow asking how someone heard about you, because it consistently outperforms expensive attribution software at telling you which channel actually created the interest, rather than just capturing it at the last click. Via The VC Corner.

And finally, one interesting thing.

ONE INTERESTING THING

A serving police officer made an unusually candid case for Flock, the automatic license-plate camera company that's become the most reviled piece of street furniture in America this summer, with cameras sawn down, smashed and rammed by protesters in more than one hundred and fifty towns. His argument is that the outrage rests on things the technology doesn't actually do: it can't identify who's driving, it can't track phones, and every search requires a logged case number that shows up in monthly audits. What it replaces, he says, is decades of vague description-based policing, where an entire fleet of silver cars might get pulled over because that's all officers had to go on. The uncomfortable twist is that scrapping police contracts doesn't remove the cameras at all, since homeowners associations and private firms are buying up the exact same technology, unregulated by the constitutional protections that only apply to government.
