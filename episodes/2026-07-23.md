Washington's tariff fight with Canada has escalated again. The Trump administration has announced fifty percent tariffs on roughly twenty billion dollars of Canadian imports, covering alcohol, dairy and other goods, using a provision of the nineteen thirty Tariff Act that had sat dormant since the nineteen forties. C S I S's Philip Luck argues this is really coercion dressed up as leverage, and that it comes with a cost economists tend to underweight, the country doing the coercing pays a price too, through retaliation and trade diversion that hits both sides. He also points out that some of the sharpest pushback isn't coming from Ottawa at all, but from ordinary Canadian consumers boycotting American goods, a form of pressure that state to state tariff strategy can't easily control. Now, over to community, events and marketing.

One idea worth stealing this week comes from Priya Parker's Group Life community. Her team ran a session called the Hot Takes Party, where members practiced arguing about deliberately low stakes opinions, think the best sandwich or whether wired headphones beat AirPods, purely to build the muscle of disagreeing well. The logic is simple. Most groups avoid friction until it explodes, at a family dinner, in a tense meeting after a launch fails, over a wedding guest list nobody agrees on. By then everyone's practicing the skill of healthy disagreement for the first time, under the highest possible stakes. Running a low stakes version regularly, before you need it, is the takeaway here for anyone building a team or a community. Via Priya Parker.

And a smaller, quieter one from community writer Rosie Sherry, who's been experimenting with what she calls micro holidays. Rather than saving rest for one big trip a year, she's blocking out half a day or so midweek, when everywhere is quieter, to do something that simply feels like a holiday, a walk on a beach, an afternoon in a park, then heading back to work refreshed. It's a small, practical reminder for anyone running on empty that rest doesn't have to be a big production to actually work. Via Rosie Sherry.

Now, over to one interesting thing.

This one comes from Works in Progress, on the surprisingly high birth rate among Hasidic Jewish communities. A Yiddish speaking woman in the United States today can expect to have six point six children on average, compared with just one point six for the country as a whole. The piece argues this isn't down to any one factor, but to an entire culture built around facilitating large families, early marriage in the early twenties, community involvement in matchmaking, and strong discouragement of contraception. Nobody's suggesting the rest of the world adopt this lifestyle wholesale, but as a case study in just how starkly fertility can diverge even within the same wealthy, urban environment, it's a striking one.
