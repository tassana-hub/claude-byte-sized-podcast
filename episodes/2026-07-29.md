First, Japan and Asia Pacific, and there's a good spread today. The CSIS Japan Chair's latest roundup highlights a new survey from Kristi Govella and Victor Cha examining how strategic elites in Japan and Korea now feel about acquiring nuclear weapons themselves, given the wars in the Middle East and Ukraine, shifting American policy, and intensifying threats from China, Russia and North Korea. The same roundup covers fresh analysis on evolving China to Russia military coordination and what it means for the U.S. to Japan alliance, on Japan's new defence export policy and whether industry will actually seize the opportunity, and notes that Japan Chair director Kristi Govella was recently cited in the Japan Times on how China's mineral squeeze is testing Japan's military build up. Via CSIS Japan Chair.

Staying in Northeast Asia, CSIS's Korea Chair previewed this week's Capital Cable, where experts including former U.S. ambassador to Korea Mark Lippert and Georgetown's Victor Cha will dig into South Korea's twelve and a half percent tariffs imposed by Washington over forced labour concerns, President Lee Jae Myung's visit to the AI summit in San Francisco, and the nine hundred and fifty billion dollars in AI and infrastructure deals that Samsung and S K have just announced with American companies. It's a striking number, and a reminder of how central Korea has become to the AI supply chain. Via CSIS Korea Chair.

Energy security ties the region together too. CSIS's energy programme argues that the ongoing Hormuz Strait crisis, which has squeezed the global supply of liquefied natural gas and pushed prices up, is now generating a real tailwind for nuclear energy across Northeast Asia. Economies like Japan, Korea and Taiwan lean heavily on imported LNG, and analyst Jane Nakano says the crisis is sharpening the case for nuclear as a hedge against exactly this kind of shock. Via CSIS Energy Security and Climate Change Program.

And further south, a CSIS commentary marks the tenth anniversary of the South China Sea tribunal ruling against China's expansive maritime claims, but focuses on an angle that gets less attention: the environmental findings. The tribunal found China had failed to protect the marine environment there, yet Beijing has simultaneously bid to host the secretariat of the new High Seas Treaty in the coastal city of Xiamen, even while still rejecting the original ruling as null and void. The piece asks whether that contradiction should shape how the world judges China's fitness to lead in ocean governance. Via CSIS.

The infrastructure arms race isn't slowing down either. OpenAI is close to leasing a five hundred billion dollar data centre in southern Ohio, with Nvidia in talks to provide a two hundred and fifty billion dollar financial backstop for the project. The deal still needs sign off from Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick, and the U.S. government is reportedly still talking to other potential tenants for the site, underlining just how much capital is now chasing AI compute. Separately, Nvidia has also made a substantial investment into Safe Superintelligence, the secretive lab founded by former OpenAI chief scientist Ilya Sutskever, giving it access to Nvidia's flagship chips after previously relying mainly on Google's. Via TLDR.

And in a smaller but eyebrow raising move, Midjourney, the AI lab best known for its image and video generators, has acquired the social astrology app Co-Star, whose roughly four million monthly users generate and share birth charts using a mix of AI and human readers. Co-Star's two dozen staff have joined Midjourney, which is quietly building out a consumer portfolio well beyond image generation, from wellness ventures to now astrology. Deal terms weren't disclosed, but it's the clearest sign yet that Midjourney sees its future well beyond pure image generation. Via TLDR Design.

It cements Revolut's place as Europe's most valuable startup and signals strong investor confidence in its global banking push. Via TLDR Fintech.

Elsewhere in fintech, insurance startup Corgi has reportedly raised again at a four billion dollar valuation, its third financing round in roughly eight weeks and nearly triple where it stood back in May. That pace of fundraising reflects genuine confidence in its growth, but also just how capital intensive and risky the insurance model can be, especially as Corgi expands into data room software and, oddly, physical coffee shops. It's another sign that investors are still willing to write very large cheques for companies sitting at the intersection of quantum computing and artificial intelligence, even as some in the same newsletter were busy asking whether the broader AI funding boom is due a correction. Now, over to policy and geopolitics, where Geopolitical Monitor's latest briefing zeroes in on a genuinely unsettling trend: as militaries adopt AI to speed up decision making, they're compressing the one resource that careful, deliberate choices have always depended on, which is time. The piece frames this specifically around nuclear decision making, warning that the faster AI makes analysis and response possible, the less room there is for the kind of caution that has kept the nuclear peace for decades. Via Geopolitical Monitor.

And a dispatch from Uncharted Territories tracks a tightening pattern of online speech regulation across the UK, the European Union and Canada. It points to rising arrest and prosecution numbers for online speech in England and Wales, the renewal of the EU's so called Chat Control surveillance powers after a contested vote in a thinly attended July session, and Canada's new Combatting Hate Act, which took effect this month and removes a long standing legal defence for expressing opinions on religious subjects. The piece also flags a separate UK government proposal that would require social platforms to give more prominence to public service broadcasters over other creators. Whatever one makes of the underlying policy debate, it's a trend worth watching for anyone doing business with platforms or users across all three jurisdictions. Via Uncharted Territories.

Now, over to community, events and marketing, where the theme today is finding attention in cheaper, more human ways. One idea worth stealing: Target, SoulCycle and American Eagle are increasingly working with micro creators, ordinary people with a few hundred followers, paying them in gift cards, discounts and free products rather than cash. It's a lower cost, higher trust alternative to traditional influencer deals, and brands are finding the smaller audiences convert just as well, if not better. Via TLDR Marketing.

And from Indie Hackers, a case study and a tip in one. Songwriter turned founder Jacob Seeger spent two and a half years failing at six different no code apps before his seventh, a tool that automatically posts Reddit stories over gameplay footage, hit a million dollars in annual recurring revenue and two and a half million users within ten months. He got there by reinvesting a single successful Twitter thread promotion again and again until short form video clicked. The newsletter's growth tip of the day fits neatly alongside it: sponsor a conference before your product actually works, since early visibility can be worth more than a finished feature set. Via Indie Hackers.

And now, to close, one interesting thing. Marginal Revolution flagged a new paper on what economists call the Confucian fertility paradox: East Asia's birth rates have fallen to levels with no historical precedent, even though Confucian tradition prized large families and continuing the family line above almost everything else. The authors argue the tradition actually had two strands, one favouring many children for lineage and old age security, which faded naturally as pensions and rising incomes took over that role, and a second favouring status won through educational achievement, rooted in the old imperial examination system. That second strand survived modernisation intact, but flipped its effect entirely: where it once rewarded having many sons so that one might succeed, it now rewards pouring everything into one or two intensively educated children, turning a once pro natal tradition into one of the strongest forces driving fertility down.
