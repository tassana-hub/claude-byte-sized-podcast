Good morning. It's Wednesday, the twenty sixth of August, and this episode carries six sections of news gathered since Monday. Let's get into it.

Now, over to Japan and Asia Pacific.

Letara Raises Sixteen Million Dollars to Build Bigger Rockets. A Japanese startup building rocket propulsion systems, Letara, has raised sixteen million dollars in a round co-led by Headline Asia, J I C Venture Growth Investment, and Incubate Fund. The company started out building thrusters for satellites, but this new money is earmarked for larger hybrid rocket systems aimed at the space, defence, and security markets. It's a reminder that Japan's small but growing space technology scene keeps punching above its weight. Via This Week in Startups.

A Stolen Blueprint Behind China's Biggest Memory Chip Maker. Testimony in a South Korean criminal trial has laid out, in striking detail, how China's now largest maker of memory chips got its start. Prosecutors say a Samsung engineer of twenty eight years' standing left for China's C X M T around 2016 carrying a six hundred step process manual covering an entire generation of Samsung's memory chip technology, from deposition through to annealing. He was sentenced to seven years in prison this April, but the technology transfer is already done, and it's said to have saved C X M T years of independent development. It's a sharp illustration of how contested the boundary still is between Asian chipmaking rivals. Via T L D R.

Beijing's Robot Olympics Signals Who's Winning the Humanoid Race. Beijing has just wrapped its second annual World Humanoid Robot Games, and the numbers tell their own story: over two thousand robots and more than six hundred teams took part, the vast majority of them Chinese. Unlike last year, most contests now test genuine autonomous skill, wiring circuits, sorting parcels, working a restaurant floor, rather than remote controlled stunts. Chinese firms including AgiBot and Unitree are already said to account for roughly three quarters of humanoid robots shipped worldwide, helped by a nearly fully localised supply chain, and Unitree's share price has surged several fold since its Shanghai listing. Washington, meanwhile, has just moved to restrict imports of foreign advanced robotic hardware. Via International Intrigue.

A Kyushu Roundtable Tackles What's Broken in Japanese Healthcare. Closer to home, Fukuoka Now is hosting its sixth Kyushu Roundtable next month, this time on healthcare, with Doctor Ronald Schlemper, a Dutch physician who has practised in Fukuoka for decades, comparing how Japanese and European medicine handle patients differently. It's a small, English language gathering capped at forty five seats, but it's exactly the kind of grounded, practical event that makes navigating Japan's healthcare system as a foreign resident a little less daunting. Via Fukuoka Now.

Now, over to AI and tech.

Why a Fintech Company Ditched Claude Code to Build Its Own Coding Agent. The fintech company Ramp has quietly built its own AI coding agent rather than relying on Claude Code or Cursor, and the results are hard to argue with. A tool called Inspect, built by a team of just five and a half people, now produces three quarters of all the code that gets merged at the company, up from sixty percent back in January. It began life as a Chrome extension before evolving into a full remote development environment that can spin up a sandbox in under five seconds. Ramp isn't alone either, Stripe, Block, and Shopify have all built comparable in-house agents of their own. Via The Pragmatic Engineer.

OpenAI Claws Back Ground on Anthropic in the Corporate Wallet War. The competitive picture between OpenAI and Anthropic for corporate spending keeps shifting. Data from Ramp, covering more than seventy thousand companies, shows Anthropic ahead in May and July, but OpenAI clawing back share as the third quarter gets underway. Interestingly, within Anthropic's own customer base, its pricier Fable model now accounts for barely a tenth of spending, with the cheaper Opus model overtaking it within a month, a sign that businesses are becoming far more price sensitive about which model they reach for. Via T L D R.

Nvidia's New Chip Claims the Fastest Inference Yet. Nvidia has pushed its new Groq three L P X inference chip into full production, an extension of its Vera Rubin platform that the company claims responds four times faster than the nearest rival and can turn hour long agentic tasks into minutes. Nebius has already committed to deploying it. In a stranger footnote, an anonymous AI model calling itself Ox Alpha appeared for free on the OpenRouter marketplace and processed twenty six trillion tokens in just four days, drawing over three hundred thousand users before anyone quite worked out who built it, though the naming and behaviour point toward a Chinese lab. Via T L D R.

Alibaba Bets Ten Billion Dollars on the AI Build-Out. Alibaba is putting real money behind its AI ambitions, planning to raise around ten billion dollars through a share sale to expand chips, data centres, and model development, just days after reporting a seventy five percent jump in AI related capital spending. Via T L D R.

Now, over to startups and venture.

Solo Founders Need Sixty Percent Less Capital Than They Used To. Starting a company has arguably never been cheaper. The share of startups built by a single founder has grown from under a quarter in 2019 to over a third last year, helped by the running cost of AI tools falling roughly a hundredfold in two years. Solo founders are punching above their funding too, Wix's eighty million dollar acquisition of Base44, built by one person in six months, is becoming something of a template. But solo led startups, despite making up over a third of new companies, still capture only around fifteen percent of venture funding, a gap investors haven't fully closed. Via The V C Corner.

Hugging Face Explores a Sale Nearly Three Times Its Last Valuation. Hugging Face, the open source AI hub, is reportedly in talks with a bank about a sale that could value it above thirteen billion dollars, nearly three times its twenty twenty three valuation. The platform now hosts more than two point seven million models and thirteen million users, a long way from its origins as a teenage chatbot app in Paris a decade ago. Via Sifted.

Europe Weighs Chasing the AI Frontier or Playing a Different Game. There's a real debate underway about whether Europe can still compete at the frontier of AI, or whether it should play a different game entirely. Italian model maker Domyn says it has raised over a billion dollars so far, mostly in debt, and expects to add another billion in equity this year as it leads the European Union's Europa consortium on frontier models. Meanwhile France has pointedly ruled out OpenAI for sensitive public sector cybersecurity work in favour of the homegrown Mistral AI, and a fresh crop of nineteen European startups are betting not on chasing frontier scale but on making AI radically more efficient, led by Multiverse Computing, which just raised five hundred million euros at a two billion valuation for its model compression technology. Via Sifted.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

Eighteen Months In, Countries Have Learned to Read Trump's Tariff Bluffs. Countries seem to have found the playbook for handling the current wave of tariff threats. CSIS trade analysts point out that despite the fifty percent tariff Washington has threatened against Canada, it actually touches less than five percent of Canadian imports, and the average real US tariff rate sits around eleven percent, far below the headline numbers. Countries are increasingly responding with vague, nonbinding framework agreements that let Washington claim a win while quietly preserving the status quo. The moments that actually shifted US behaviour were the forceful ones: Iran closing the Strait of Hormuz, and China tightening its grip on critical minerals exports. Via CSIS.

Washington Declares Economic Warfare on Iran as Canada Talks Collapse. The Treasury Department has launched what it's calling an economic D Day against Iran, threatening secondary sanctions across digital assets, gold, technology, aviation, and shipping, and naming sixty entities so far, with a major financial institution reportedly next. Notably absent from the list is China, which still buys the vast majority of Iran's oil through a shadow fleet of tankers, a gap that lines up conveniently with President Xi's planned visit to Washington next month. Separately, trade talks between the US and Canada have collapsed altogether, with Washington doubling car tariffs to fifty percent from the new year and Canada promising dollar for dollar retaliation on twenty billion dollars of American goods. Via International Intrigue.

Now, over to community, events, and marketing.

Puzzle Nights Are the New Dating Apps. Here's one worth stealing. Young people are trading dating apps and doom scrolling for what's being called soft socialising, low pressure, activity based meetups where the point is the puzzle or the trivia night, not the small talk. Puzzle events are up a hundred and fifty one percent year on year, mahjong meetups up ninety four percent. If you're building community, the lesson is to give people something to do together, not just a room to stand around in. Via T L D R.

Community Teams Still Can't Prove Their Own Value. CMX's latest community industry report has a sobering headline of its own: only seventeen percent of community professionals feel confident they can prove their work's business value, down from a quarter last year, even though ninety three percent of them are now using AI tools and in-person events are on the rise again. If you run a community programme, that gap between doing good work and proving it in numbers the C-suite understands might be the thing worth fixing first. Via CMX.

Finally, one interesting thing to take you into the rest of the week.

The writer Rebecca Solnit, by way of Dense Discovery, has a thought worth sitting with. She argues that we increasingly crave tidy, airtight categories, total success or total failure, in ways that flatten anything genuinely complicated, and that once something gets filed into a category, that's often where thinking about it stops rather than starts. Her point isn't that nuance is nice to have, it's that treating the status quo or the middle ground as automatically neutral is its own kind of bias, dressed up as balance. Real understanding, she argues, means being able to hold two true but contradictory things at once. Via Dense Discovery.
