# Byte-sized — Tuesday, 4 August

Hello, and welcome to Byte-sized for Tuesday, the fourth of August. Today's episode runs across six sections, from Asia-Pacific news through to a closing idea. Let's get started.

Japan and Asia-Pacific.

We start in Washington, where the Center for Strategic and International Studies hosted a special live episode of its Impossible State podcast on the sharpening trade tension between the United States and South Korea. Victor Cha was joined by Bloomberg Economics' Adam Farrar, alongside journalists from the Chosun Ilbo and the Hankyoreh, to unpack a recent US House Judiciary Committee report on discriminatory trade practices, the expiry of so called Section one twenty two tariffs, and the fate of recent Korean investment deals into the United States. The tone from all three panellists was that Seoul and Washington are drifting further apart on trade than either government would like to admit. Via CSIS.

In China, Alibaba has unveiled Qwen three point eight Max, a two point four trillion parameter model with a context window stretching to a million tokens, which the company says can code autonomously for weeks at a stretch. It is the clearest sign yet of Beijing's push to close the gap with American frontier labs, and it lands in the same week that DeepSeek quietly released the production version of its V4 Flash model, which independent benchmarking found averages just three cents per test, making it more than a hundred times cheaper to run than Anthropic's Fable five for broadly comparable reasoning and knowledge performance. Via This Week in Startups and TLDR.

And that AI rivalry has spilled into hardware politics too. The American Federal Communications Commission this week banned new foreign made humanoid robots, quadrupeds, robot vacuums and sidewalk delivery robots from the US market, citing privacy risks from devices that map homes and public spaces, as well as supply chain security concerns. The rule does not name China outright, but China controls roughly eighty five per cent of the global humanoid robot market, and Beijing has already threatened retaliation, calling the move a threat to economic and trade stability. Notably, the ban does not curb data collection by robots already in American homes, and it risks hurting US robotics firms that themselves depend on Chinese components. Via TLDR.

Now, over to AI and tech.

OpenAI has revealed that Astra, an unreleased internal version of its next model family, has solved ten long open problems across mathematics, quantum complexity and theoretical computer science, including a symmetry structure mathematicians had hunted for since nineteen ninety nine, and three items from Paul Erdos's list that had not moved in a decade. Every proof was formally verified using the Lean theorem prover, and the whole run cost roughly two thousand dollars in tokens. Within twenty four hours, an Anthropic researcher said he had reproduced five of the ten proofs using Fable running on a generic prompt with no internet access, which has sparked a lively debate about whether a machine's proof can meaningfully be called Fields Medal worthy work. Via The Rundown.

That breakthrough arrives amid a full blown frontier pricing war. OpenAI cut the price of its Luna model by eighty per cent just three weeks after launch and trimmed its Terra model by a further twenty per cent, Anthropic released Opus five at half the price of Fable five, and Google is now pricing Gemini three point six Flash below Alibaba's Kimi K three on a per task basis. Commentators note that model pricing can now go stale before a startup even ships, which is pushing labs to move before most enterprise customers have even finished switching. Via TLDR Founders.

And on the practical side of things, Lenny's Newsletter sat down with Nick Baumann of OpenAI, who demonstrated how he now runs huge parts of his work and life through ChatGPT's Codex Voice interface. He triggers it with a hotkey, and it can read his screen, then spin up separate parallel threads to book a flight, file an expense report and check his calendar all at once, forking new conversation threads on its own when a task gets complicated. He also uses a custom plugin that takes fifty or sixty raw video clips, transcribes them, picks the strongest takes and assembles a finished video overnight while he sleeps. The bigger theme, as his co host puts it, is that talking to AI rather than typing tends to give it more context, and more context tends to produce better results. Via Lenny's Newsletter.

Now, over to startups and venture.

The British chip startup Olix has raised three hundred and twelve million dollars in a Series B round at a three point three billion dollar valuation, with Arm and Hudson River Trading among the participants, to expand its specialised AI inference silicon platform. It is one of several large European raises this week: Index Ventures closed a fresh two billion dollar fund to double down on the next generation of AI startups, even as Sifted's reporting flags a broader awkward question hanging over so called neolabs, ambitious outfits like Recursive Superintelligence and Inherent that have raised eye watering sums chasing self improving AI and world models without ever quite explaining how they intend to make money. Via Sifted.

Meanwhile in travel, Expedia has acquired the AI planning startup Layla to speed up its own AI strategy, hoping to marry its scale and supply relationships with Layla's personalisation features, while the corporate travel platform Engine has acquired Options Travel to build a fully AI native online booking tool for travel management companies. Industry watchers at PhocusWire argue that in travel, agentic AI succeeds or fails less on the model underneath and more on whether the everyday conversational interface actually gets adopted by agents and travellers. Via PhocusWire.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

A review of more than eighty Chinese academic papers and patents has found that military and security linked researchers in China are using a technique called model distillation to train smaller domestic AI systems on the outputs of American models from OpenAI and Anthropic, extracting frontier level capability without needing the vast GPU clusters that training from scratch would require. It is a workaround that quietly undercuts the entire logic of American chip export controls, since restricting access to advanced silicon matters far less if capability can simply be distilled down onto whatever hardware is already sitting in a domestic data centre. Via TLDR.

And writer Byrne Hobart has a sharp piece this week on why some of the wealthiest people in tech are pouring money into aggressive, seemingly irrational AI bets. His argument is that for someone whose entire net worth rests on a business that AI could suddenly make obsolete, an investment that pays off specifically if AI accelerates further is not really a bet for returns at all, it is a hedge, and hedges are allowed to look financially strange because their job is to protect against a very particular kind of ruin rather than to maximise profit. Via The Diff.

Now, over to community, events and marketing, and here is one idea worth stealing.

Marketing Brew reports that executives at Netflix, Tubi, Adobe and Reddit are increasingly building strategy directly around fandom rather than around generic audience segments, after a twenty twenty five Deloitte study found that seventy five per cent of American adults now consider themselves part of at least one fandom. Netflix's chief marketing officer described deliberately designing around fan obsession, in her words the good kind rather than the stalker kind, treating superfans as strategic partners worth investing in directly rather than just another slice of the audience to advertise at. Via Marketing Brew.

And community builder Rosie Sherry published a candid reflection this week on falling out of love with indie hacking, the loosely organised movement of solo founders building small software businesses in public. Her complaint was not with building businesses at all, but with the transactional churn around it, the spammy requests for upvotes and feedback that substitute for genuine community. Her one idea worth stealing is really a warning: the founders she still respects are the ones who tuned out the noise and the hustle theatre entirely, and simply kept building something they believed was good. Via Rosie Sherry.

And now, one interesting thing.

Economist Tyler Cowen flagged a genuinely elegant piece of philosophy this week. Utilitarian ethicists have long been stuck on a puzzle: if the universe is infinite, then there is infinite positive value and infinite negative value in it, which makes it mathematically impossible to even describe how good or bad the current state of things is, let alone whether any action improves it. Oxford philosopher Toby Ord's proposed fix is to use so called hyperreal numbers, a mathematical system in which infinities can still be compared in size, so that infinitely many twos genuinely add up to twice as much as infinitely many ones. It does not resolve everything, since some of the underlying choices in the system remain unavoidably arbitrary, but for a problem that has quietly bothered moral philosophy for decades, it counts as real progress.
