# Byte-sized — Thursday, 10 September

Good morning. It's Thursday the tenth of September, and today's episode has six sections for you.

Let's start in Japan and Asia-Pacific.

First, China. Chinese regulators are tightening the approval process for humanoid robotics companies seeking to go public, after a volatile trading debut from sector leader Unitree Robotics. The China Securities Regulatory Commission wants to cool a sector it sees as sizzling but full of copycats with little real technological breakthrough behind them, and the tighter posture is expected to catch other hopefuls too, including the firms AGIBOT and Galbot. Via TLDR.

Next, security in North East Asia. North Korea has commissioned its second nuclear-capable destroyer, more than a year after the first was damaged in a botched launch attempt. It's a reminder that the security picture across the region keeps sharpening, and it lands just as analysts debate whether the alliance between South Korea and the United States needs a clearer, more coherent strategy going forward. Via the CSIS Project on Nuclear Issues.

And a policy story you won't have seen elsewhere: India's state governments have been on a reform tear. Uttar Pradesh drafted a new data centre policy offering incentives for renewable-powered facilities, Haryana passed a Right to Business bill promising newly registered firms a three-year moratorium on inspections, and half a dozen other states pushed through similar ease-of-business measures in the same week. It's a reminder that some of the most consequential regulatory shifts for investors in Asia are happening at the state level, not the national one. Via CSIS's States Weekly.

And a reminder of how much of the advanced chip supply chain still runs through a handful of hands: reporting this week found that China's homegrown lithography machines, the tools used to print the tiniest features onto computer chips, still rely on lenses from the German company Zeiss. Huawei has become the de facto coordinator of China's push to build a fully domestic alternative, but for now, even Beijing's chip independence drive has a European bottleneck built into it. Via TLDR.

Now, over to AI and tech.

The big claim of the week: OpenAI says an internal model, more capable than its public system, used ten thousand parallel agents working for eighty eight hours to produce a proposed solution to the Navier-Stokes existence and smoothness problem, one of mathematics' seven Millennium Prize problems, unsolved for roughly ninety years. The system reportedly showed that smooth three-dimensional fluid flow can develop a finite-time singularity, and backed the claim with both a written proof and a machine-checked formal version. Via TLDR and This Week in Startups.

Meanwhile, Meta launched Muse, which it's billing as the first personal AI agent built for everyone. It runs in its own secure cloud computer, can handle tasks like booking travel or sending emails on your behalf, and is rolling out on iOS, Android and the web in the United States, with an encrypted tier coming soon. It landed the same week OpenAI pushed out an upgrade to its image generator, with sharper detail, more reliable editing, and faster generation times. Via TLDR.

On the engineering side, OpenAI's Tibo Sottiaux talked through how Codex, the company's coding agent, actually got built. It was written in the programming language Rust from day one, for scale, even though the underlying models were better at other languages at the time. It was released open source, partly so it could plug into rival companies' models rather than lock users into OpenAI's own. And it's now embedded so deeply into OpenAI's internal tools that new hires are simply told: if you want to know who's working on something, ask Codex. Sottiaux argues that maintenance and re-architecting work that used to take years can now take days. Via the Pragmatic Engineer podcast.

And a sharper note: an Anthropic researcher, Jacob Coxon, said this week he's quitting the company over fears the industry is racing toward self-improving superintelligence without adequate safeguards, naming both Anthropic and OpenAI directly. It's gone semi-viral, and it's a reminder that the safety debate inside the top labs is far from settled, even as ChatGPT itself quietly broke its own usage record for the fourth month running, reaching one point zero six billion monthly active users in August. Via TLDR and Sifted.

Now, over to startups and venture.

AI coding startup Cognition closed a two-billion-dollar funding round at a forty-eight-billion-dollar valuation, up from twenty-six billion in May, led by Andreessen Horowitz, Accel, Founders Fund, General Catalyst and Avenir. It's a sign investors still think there's room for several winners in AI coding, even as the startup's cash burn could reach eight hundred million dollars this year. Via TLDR and This Week in Startups.

In Europe, French AI group Mistral confirmed a three-billion-euro round at a twenty-one-billion-euro valuation, backed by Samsung, the European Union's Scale-up Fund, and the American growth investor PSG Equity. Almost in the same breath, Mistral's own robotics chief is now out raising two hundred million euros for his own robotics-model startup, a sign of how quickly talent is recycling itself inside Europe's AI boom. Sifted's own new leaderboard of the hundred fastest-growing startups across Central and Eastern Europe and the German-speaking DACH countries found Germany claiming the biggest share of names, but the very top spot going to a company in Prague, with strong showings from Latvia and Georgia too. Founders there flagged a challenge unique to the region: several of these countries border Russia, and building a company now means building against a backdrop of an escalating geopolitical standoff with the West. Via Sifted.

And in legal tech, Harvey closed a five-hundred-and-fifty-million-dollar round at a fifteen-point-six-billion-dollar valuation, beating even the fifteen-point-five-billion target it was said to be chasing a month earlier. Its Swedish rival, Legora, is expanding just as fast off the back of its own five-hundred-and-fifty-million-dollar raise, underlining just how hot enterprise legal AI has become. Via This Week in Startups and Sifted.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

The veteran trade voice Bill Reinsch used his CSIS column this week to tally up what he calls three Trump-era own goals. First, the war with Iran, which closed the Strait of Hormuz, stranded cargo ships and depleted American weapons stocks, seemingly heading toward an outcome that will look much like the deal Trump abandoned in his first term. Second, the trade war with Canada, where escalating tariffs have been met with strong Canadian public support for retaliation, despite Canada's economic dependence on the United States. And third, the tariff fight with China, which he argues has only accelerated Beijing's push for high-tech self-sufficiency. His conclusion: all three reflect a failure to think through consequences before acting. Via CSIS.

And an old dispute is flaring again: Argentina's president, Javier Milei, is once more talking up reclaiming the Falkland Islands from Britain, this time threatening criminal charges against an Israeli energy firm planning to drill for oil near the islands. The timing lines up with sliding approval ratings at home, a Trump administration reviewing its neutrality on the issue, and elections roughly a year away. Britain's response has been to simply repeat that ninety-nine-point-eight percent of Falklanders voted to stay British back in twenty thirteen. Via International Intrigue.

And in the Middle East, new satellite imagery analysis shared with CNN by CSIS points to a clear surge in construction activity at a suspected Iranian nuclear site known as Pickaxe Mountain, near the Natanz facility. Analysts describe a site that has shifted over the past few years from active excavation toward internal construction and continued reinforcement, just as Washington weighs options for striking deep underground facilities. Via CSIS's Project on Nuclear Issues.

Now, over to community, events and marketing.

Marketing Brew highlighted how the tequila brand eight-eighteen has been bringing an influencer-style production experience to ordinary couples' weddings, essentially treating someone's wedding day like a brand activation, with the kind of polish usually reserved for a product launch. One idea worth stealing: borrow influencer-marketing production values for real-life milestone moments, not just campaigns. Via Marketing Brew.

And Sifted spoke to the chief operating officer of the German AI company Voize about running a great team offsite as the company tripled in size: presentations on culture, candle-making, line-dancing, and beer by a lake outside Berlin. The idea worth stealing here: treat the offsite as a serious retention tool rather than a throwaway perk, and deliberately scale the format as the team grows instead of repeating the same playbook every year. Via Sifted.

And finally, one interesting thing.

Every's Laura Entis spoke to five professional writers about how they actually use AI day to day. One, facing a mountain of research, turned each day's material into a podcast she listened to while making breakfast, then had ChatGPT interview her on a walk whenever she got stuck, saying her head felt clear again within forty minutes. Another had an AI research agent that, while hunting for a source's contact details, found a security flaw that let it pull an email address straight out of someone's GitHub account. The common thread across all five: they draft the bones of a piece themselves, and only bring AI in afterwards to restructure, tighten and fill gaps. Via Every.

That's your six sections for today. Thanks for listening to Byte-sized, and I'll see you tomorrow.
