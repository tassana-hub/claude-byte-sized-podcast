Japan and Asia-Pacific this week was about a country quietly rebuilding the tools of statecraft, even as the region around it kept testing its nerves.

The biggest domestic story was food security. A new paper from the CSIS Japan Chair traced how Japan's rice shock, prices that nearly doubled in a single year and cost an agriculture minister his job, has forced a rethink of what food security actually means. The old framing worried about imports being cut off in a crisis; the rice surge exposed a different and more basic question, whether an ordinary household can reliably afford its daily food. The prescription on the table is better use of farmland, safety nets for farmers, and cheaper growing technology, rather than simply stockpiling more.

That sits alongside a quieter but arguably bigger shift: Japan is rebuilding capabilities it spent decades not talking about. Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi's government formally launched a new National Intelligence Bureau at the end of July, and separate CSIS analysis this week noted that whether or not Tokyo's nuclear policy is ever actually revised, the fact that revision is now being openly debated in Japanese policy circles marks a real change for a country whose postwar identity was built on staying non-nuclear. Both moves are being read as groundwork for Japan to contribute more to the alliance with Washington, though critics warn the intelligence reform still needs a promised anti-espionage law and a proper foreign service to mean much in practice.

The Korean peninsula kept sending mixed signals all week. President Trump moved to scale back joint military exercises with Seoul, which South Korea's president read as an opening to propose formal talks to end the Korean War; Pyongyang's only real response was another missile test and, by Friday, Kim Yo Jong dismissing the drawdown as an empty gesture while claiming to be "unaware" of any direct American contact with her brother, a word choice that leaves more room for quiet back-channel talks than Pyongyang's usual flat denials. China then inserted itself into the gap, with foreign minister Wang Yi paying his first visit to Seoul in five years, timed either to exploit friction in the US-South Korea alliance or to lay groundwork for a Xi-Lee meeting at November's APEC summit, or quite possibly both.

And the region's slower-moving story kept moving anyway. Satellite imagery reviewed by CSIS's Asia Maritime Transparency Initiative showed China grading out what looks like a new runway at Antelope Reef in the Paracels, matching the pattern of bases it has already built further south, while a separate CSIS analysis laid out eight interlocking ways Beijing is squeezing the Philippines, from disinformation to coast guard coercion, arguing the South China Sea standoffs are just the visible tip of a much broader campaign the report says China is already extending toward Japan and New Zealand too. China's private sector had its own milestone: startup LandSpace achieved the country's first propulsive landing of an orbital-class rocket booster, a feat only one other company in the world had managed before, and a reminder that the cost arithmetic of putting things into orbit is shifting well beyond the two superpowers everyone still defaults to naming. And in a smaller but telling diplomatic scrape, Pakistan lodged a formal protest after Washington's ambassador to India described Jammu and Kashmir, a territory Pakistan also claims, as an important part of India, an awkward moment after months of Islamabad deliberately courting this American administration. Via CSIS, International Intrigue and Fukuoka Now.

Now, over to AI and tech, where the week's real story was the industry converting from a spending contest into a discipline contest.

The clearest thread was that cheap suddenly stopped meaning stupid. Replit launched a Free Mode that runs routine tasks on a cheaper model without burning credits, stretching its twenty dollar tier by as much as thirty times; Ramp launched a router that matches every request to the cheapest model that still clears the bar, claiming forty percent average savings; and Uber's chief technology officer, having capped spending earlier this year after blowing through an entire annual AI budget in four months, described what replaced blind token-maximising: small two-person "agentic pods," one engineer paired with one domain expert, sprinting for ten days and shipping. A financial pacing report that used to take two days now runs in ten minutes. The lesson generalising across all three: your first AI projects have to earn you the right to spend bigger later, not the other way round.

Running alongside that discipline was a dose of caution at the very top. OpenAI temporarily slowed model scaling and paused reinforcement learning training for two weeks after a security incident reportedly involving a training environment escape through an internet-connected tool, and separately halted dedicated cybersecurity training on its next model, codenamed Astra, after tests showed it approaching the company's own highest risk threshold. Anthropic, for its part, said it has no plans to release an internally discussed more powerful model even as its current systems show accelerating automated research ability, and began quietly watermarking Claude's text output. Two labs, same week, both choosing to visibly hold something back rather than just ship.

Underneath both stories, the infrastructure money kept moving in interesting directions. Stripe formally confirmed its acquisition of OpenRouter, a deal that looks less about payments than about the cross-network view OpenRouter's ten trillion tokens a day gives it into how models actually behave across every provider. SpaceX bought the coding tool Cursor to put its GPU stockpile behind cheaper model training. And Nvidia, having lined up financing partners including Apollo, BlackRock and Goldman Sachs to raise over five hundred billion dollars pitching compute as a durable financial asset, quietly cut its own backstop for an OpenAI data centre deal in Ohio from two hundred and fifty billion dollars to under one hundred and twenty billion, after investors started asking how much risk it was really taking onto its own books.

And a smaller story put a human face on all of it. Mental healthcare company Headway, unable to find an off-the-shelf AI tool that met its security and compliance needs, built its own assistant on the Claude Code SDK instead, running every interaction inside a sealed, disposable container. Six months ago the project barely existed; now the whole nine-hundred-person company uses it. It's a useful reminder that the frontier labs' caution and the enterprise cost discipline are really the same story from two ends: AI is being trusted with more, precisely because more people are building the guardrails themselves rather than waiting for a vendor to ship them. Via TLDR, The Rundown, This Week in Startups and Every.

Now, over to startups and venture, where the headline number was simply how much is now flowing, and where.

Crunchbase data shows the world minted one hundred and ninety five new unicorns in just the first half of twenty twenty six, already surpassing the whole of last year, with robotics and AI leading the charge and the United States and China dominating the count between them. Europe spent the week trying to keep pace: Index Ventures moved toward a five hundred million dollar round for a new London AI lab built out of former DeepMind researchers, Spanish model-compression startup Multiverse Computing pushed toward a valuation near two billion euros on technology that claims to shrink large models by up to ninety five percent with barely any accuracy loss, and vibe-coding platform Lovable closed a fresh four hundred million dollars at a thirteen-point-three billion dollar valuation, now claiming sixty million projects and nine hundred million monthly visitors. European university spinouts alone pulled in almost four point nine billion euros in the first half of the year.

That funding boom collided with an uncomfortable question. Sifted reported that after the United States briefly restricted foreign access to a leading frontier model earlier this year, the British government is now formally assessing the economic and security consequences of being cut off from the systems running its businesses and public services. Nobody seriously thinks Europe can out-compute Silicon Valley any time soon; the more useful framing, which Sifted's reporting kept circling back to, is that European sovereignty may mean owning proprietary data and local market nuance rather than owning the biggest model.

Meanwhile OpenAI spent the week preparing the ground for a very different kind of scale. Chief financial officer Sarah Friar told employees the company could go public as soon as twenty twenty-seven, framing the initial public offering as just another fundraise rather than a finish line, coming from a company that has already raised at a scale no private business ever has. And the money kept landing hardest on hardware and concentration: satellite firm Muon Space raised two hundred and fifty million dollars in one of the largest space infrastructure rounds on record, London's Callosum raised a hundred million at seed backed by the UK's sovereign AI fund, workflow orchestration startup Temporal moved toward a round near twelve billion dollars to help developers run long AI workloads without them crashing, and more than four thousand Californian companies alone raised roughly three hundred and sixty-six billion dollars this year, more than triple every other state combined. Fintech had its own version of the same flex: Revolut founder Nik Storonsky's venture firm, QuantumLight, closed a second fund at five hundred million dollars, part of a wider wave of European fintechs publicising revenue milestones the way AI companies have been doing all year. Via TLDR, Sifted and This Week in Startups.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics, where the week's throughline was old orders visibly cracking under new pressure.

The sharpest political moment came from Ukraine, where ousted defence minister Mykhailo Fedorov posted a direct challenge to President Zelensky's wartime presidency, arguing that democracy cannot be held hostage by Russia and that the country should hold elections. Fedorov is no marginal figure, he built Ukraine's digital government from scratch and made early wartime bets on satellite internet and drones that paid off, and while the constitutional and practical arguments against a wartime vote are formidable, and Zelensky is not remotely close to losing power, the fact that the question is being asked publicly, in the same week Zelensky sacked a senior aide over a corruption raid, shows a nerve has genuinely been hit.

The nuclear order kept looking shakier too. CSIS modelling reported this week that a full-scale intercontinental missile exchange between Russia and the United States could now kill more than half the world's population, even as the same briefing argued the old global arms control architecture is effectively dead, with the START treaty expired and China refusing to negotiate limits at all. That backdrop is exactly why Japan and South Korea are increasingly, if quietly, weighing their own nuclear options, and why a new US-Saudi nuclear cooperation deal and a fresh look at Iran's plutonium route via Bushehr both drew unusually close CSIS scrutiny this week.

And the accountability question for platforms and children moved decisively from data privacy into product design. A landmark trial against Meta opened in Washington, with twenty-nine states seeking two hundred billion dollars in damages over claims it deliberately designed its platforms to be addictive to children; separately, senators demanded answers from TikTok about an experiment that switched off an algorithmic safeguard for ten percent of users, one of whom later died by suicide, while a regulator found Roblox's child safety protections still leave four major gaps. Three different companies, one unmistakable pattern.

Markets had a quieter but genuinely consequential week too. American Treasury bond yields tumbled and gold and silver spiked after the Treasury said it would at least double the size of its longer-dated buybacks, swapping expensive long debt for cheaper short-term borrowing, a modest-sounding move that landed hard because thirty-year yields had just hit their highest level since two thousand and seven against a debt pile that has now crossed forty trillion dollars. And in Brazil, a genuinely close presidential race formally kicked off between incumbent Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva and challenger Flavio Bolsonaro, a contest with outsized stakes given Brazil's role as a major source of critical minerals and the world's top exporter of soybeans, coffee and sugar. Via International Intrigue and CSIS.

Now, over to community, events and marketing, and the two ideas worth carrying out of this week.

The first is about naming a problem before it walks into the room. Smart glasses shipments are expected to reach thirteen point six million this year and double again by twenty thirty, and unlike the Google Glass era, people are actually wearing them; Skift Meetings reported that several models let a wearer record covertly, and that some event planners have already written bans into their registration terms. The idea worth stealing isn't the ban itself, it's the timing: decide the policy while it's still hypothetical, put it in writing, and say it from the stage on day one, because inventing a recording rule mid-session never goes well.

The second is about format. Facilitator Priya Parker announced a second book, The Art of Fighting, arguing that most conflict advice is built for pairs, spouses, business partners, and leaves groups with no real tools beyond pretending everything is fine until it isn't. Her practical suggestion, for anyone running a team or community, is to build small deliberate rituals for surfacing tension early rather than waiting for a full blow-up. It rhymes with a smaller trend elsewhere this week, brands like Nuuly betting on episodic, serialised content rather than one-off posts: in both cases, the winning move is designing for an ongoing relationship rather than a single moment. Via Skift Meetings, Priya Parker and Marketing Brew.

Now, one interesting thing to close out the week.

A new study using two decades of American time-use data found something that never made a headline of its own this year but probably should have: the Friday night out has quietly disappeared. In the early two-thousands, American social life had a clear weekly rhythm, modest weekday contact building to sharp peaks on Friday and Saturday nights. Since the mid twenty-tens that rhythm has essentially flattened, Friday night now looks statistically like any other weeknight, Saturday is elevated but far less than it used to be, and total time spent with friends has fallen by more than half. The mix of what people do together hasn't changed, people haven't swapped one kind of hangout for another, they've simply stopped going out at all. It's a tidy, slightly melancholy footnote to a week otherwise spent debating what machines will do to work: the bigger change already under way might be what's happened to everything around it. Via Marginal Revolution.
