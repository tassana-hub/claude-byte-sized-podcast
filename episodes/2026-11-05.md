Section one, Japan and Asia Pacific.

First, Fukuoka goes to the polls on the fifteenth of November. After sixteen years under Mayor Soichiro Takashima, the city is about to get new leadership, and the Kyushu Roundtable held on the thirtieth of October asked what growth should actually deliver. Forty-six people from seventeen nationalities argued over whether the priority is better jobs, more affordable housing, better transport or public spaces that work for everyone. If you are building anything in Fukuoka, the thing to watch is whether the next mayor treats the startup and foreign-resident story as a headline or as a housing and transport problem. Via Fukuoka Now.

Second, Toyota and Waymo are deepening their partnership. They plan to test autonomous Toyota bZ4X vehicles in Japan by twenty twenty-seven, with the United Kingdom and other markets to follow. For Japan, which has a driver shortage and an ageing population, this is one of the first credible routes to driverless service on its own roads. Via T L D R Hardware.

Third, the US midterms rattled Asian markets. As the results came in, Japan's Nikkei fell about four per cent, as investors repriced technology bets. Elsewhere in the region, Indonesia's President Prabowo has sacked and arrested his chief of staff over alleged wiretapping of senior officials, which advisers are describing as possible treason. And in South Korea, a foreign ministry employee accidentally uploaded the passport details of President Lee and dozens of senior officials to a public malware-scanning site. Via International Intrigue.

Fourth, memory chips. Samsung confirmed two-nanometre chip orders from major cloud companies, while Samsung and SK Hynix both say their high-bandwidth memory for AI servers is effectively sold out for years, to at least twenty twenty-eight. Meanwhile, Chinese robotics startups raised about thirteen billion dollars in the first half of the year, five times the same period last year, as the money shifts from robot bodies to robot brains. Via Benedict's Newsletter, and T L D R Hardware.

Now, over to A I and tech.

Section two, A I and tech.

First, Apple has opened its rebuilt Siri to all developers, in the iOS twenty-seven point three beta. Apps can add their own actions and teach Siri multi-step jobs, but nothing reaches the App Store until spring, and it is not yet available in the European Union. It is a real crack in Apple's walled garden, though most of the work now falls to developers. Via The Rundown A I.

Second, Anthropic has published its S-one filing for a stock market listing. Benedict Evans notes the relief of finally seeing real quarterly revenue and a real profit and loss account, rather than leaked annualised figures. The filing also shows close to a trillion dollars of future purchase commitments, plus a shareholder structure that locks in the founders' control. His wider point is that the numbers are a snapshot with little predictive value, in a week when markets were already nervous about an A I correction, with huge capital spending, OpenAI trimming a fundraising round, and Microsoft offering buyouts to sixty thousand staff. Via Benedict's Newsletter.

Third, a usage milestone. OpenAI says ChatGPT now reaches about one point two billion people each week, its search volume is around a fifth of Google's, and voice mode has two hundred and fifty million weekly users. It also plans to merge its chat and work modes into one. Via T L D R A I.

Fourth, DeepSeek reportedly plans to train its next model on Huawei's Ascend chips. Those chips have mostly been used for running models rather than training them, so this is a serious test of whether China can build a frontier model without Nvidia. Via T L D R A I.

Now, over to startups and venture.

Section three, startups and venture.

First, Menlo Ventures' yearly enterprise A I report says the frontier labs now capture sixty-nine per cent of enterprise A I deployment spending. Yet startups still take sixty-one per cent of application spending once the labs' own apps are left out, and new budgets funded more than half of the extra spend. The takeaway for founders is that the moat is outcome data that only exists when someone actually does the job, such as whether a loan defaulted. Via T L D R Founders.

Second, fal, the platform that hosts image and video models, has raised one point three billion dollars, doubling its April valuation to sixteen billion. Money is still flowing to the infrastructure layer. Via The Rundown A I.

Third, a warning sign for software. Stripe benchmarks covering about one hundred and eighty-one thousand subscription businesses show twenty-nine of forty-eight groups above one hundred thousand dollars in recurring revenue shrinking in October, up from four in September. Churn fell, so the writer suspects weaker new sales rather than customers leaving. Only companies above five million dollars were still growing. Via T L D R Founders.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

Section four, policy and geopolitics.

First, the midterms. Democrats are on track for a large House majority and appear to have taken the Senate, and flipped governorships including Texas. The tech angle matters most. Texas had paused new data centres in rural areas to calm anger over the grid and water, and markets had hoped that pause would be lifted after the election. Instead it may become permanent, which raises questions about the era of frictionless tech expansion in the United States. A Congress that cannot unwind tariffs or stop wars still controls hearings and spending. Via International Intrigue.

Second, Nvidia has reportedly told its data centre partners, verbally, that at least fifty-one per cent of their revenue must come from outside China. It targets the loophole of Chinese firms renting advanced chips through offshore data centres. Via International Intrigue.

Third, President Trump says he has agreed with Prime Minister Netanyahu to wind down four billion dollars in annual military aid to Israel by the end of the year, years ahead of schedule. Israeli officials have not yet commented. Via International Intrigue.

Now, over to community, events and marketing.

Section five, community, events and marketing. One idea worth stealing, twice.

First, the Kyushu Roundtable format. Fukuoka Now brings a few dozen people from many countries into one room, adds two local commentators with different views, then publishes a report with the questions and contrasting opinions, so people who missed it can still join the conversation. Next month it ends with a year-end bonenkai, so the serious discussion flows into socialising. It is a cheap format that builds a mixed community and a body of content at the same time. Via Fukuoka Now.

Second, David Spinks, founder of the CMX community conference, admits his creative work has felt flat since he left the industry. His practical fix is to look back at which clients felt most alive to work with, and let that pattern, rather than comparison with peers, pick the focus. If your own community or brand feels unfocused, that audit takes an afternoon. Via David Spinks.

Now, one interesting thing.

Section six, one interesting thing.

Why Is This Interesting runs a piece about trying to open a bank account in Mexico City, where the banker tore up his signature because it did not match his passport. The explanation is civil law. Countries such as Mexico, Japan, Korea and Taiwan write comprehensive codes that demand proof of identity up front. Common law countries like the United Kingdom and the United States let transactions close first, and punish lying afterwards, with private firms such as title insurers pricing the risk. One system makes paperwork painful, the other makes identity theft easy. Via Why Is This Interesting.
