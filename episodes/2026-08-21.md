First up, Japan and Asia-Pacific.

Japan's rice shock has turned into a proper rethink of what food security actually means. A new paper from the CSIS Japan Chair, by Ryosuke Inoue, points out that in early twenty twenty-four a five kilogram bag of rice cost the average household about two thousand yen, roughly thirteen dollars, and a year later that price had nearly doubled to its highest level in decades. The politics moved fast: the agriculture minister resigned in May twenty twenty-five after saying he had never bought rice with his own money, prices became an election issue, and the government eventually cut the consumption tax on food despite its fiscal worries. Inoue's argument is that Japan has spent decades framing food security around a single fear, that imports could be cut off in a crisis, when the rice surge exposed a quite different question: can an ordinary household reliably afford its daily food. His prescription is to build production capacity through better use of farmland, safety nets for farmers when prices fall, and technologies that lower the cost of growing. Via CSIS.

On the Korean peninsula, Pyongyang has given its answer to Washington's opening. Kim Yo Jong, the influential sister of the North Korean leader, dismissed Donald Trump's decision to scale back joint military exercises with Seoul as an empty gesture, and claimed to be completely unaware of any direct American contact with her brother. That phrasing is worth noticing. Pyongyang is not usually shy about flat denial, so the choice of the word unaware leaves rather more room than usual for the possibility that quiet talks are happening somewhere out of sight. Via International Intrigue.

China's private space sector has just cleared a bar that only one company in the world had cleared before. The startup LandSpace has achieved China's first propulsive land landing of an orbital class booster, on only the second flight of that vehicle, a milestone SpaceX first reached back in twenty fifteen. Eleven years is a meaningful gap, but a Chinese commercial firm now landing boosters changes the cost arithmetic of everything the country wants to put in orbit. Via International Intrigue.

And a small diplomatic scrape worth logging. Pakistan has lodged a formal protest after Washington's ambassador to India, Sergio Gor, used a visit to describe Jammu and Kashmir, which Pakistan also claims, as an important part of India. Slip or not, it lands awkwardly for Islamabad after a long stretch of deliberate courting of this American administration. Via International Intrigue.

Now, over to AI and tech.

Anthropic has published research showing its models running protein design campaigns largely on their own, which is a real step into the front end of drug discovery. Given a single expert-written prompt, internet access and tools, the models produced designs that yielded working molecules on fourteen of fifteen targets, with success rates between twenty-two and thirty-five percent against an industry norm of roughly ten to fifteen. Anthropic did none of the bench work itself; Twist Bioscience and Adaptyv Bio built the candidates and ran the measurements. In a separate test, a model opened raw instrument files with no laboratory software at all and reported a sample at ninety-six point four percent purity in nineteen minutes, where the lab's own report had taken four days. Artificial intelligence in protein design is not new. A general purpose model running the whole campaign is. Via The Rundown.

The bigger story across the week, though, is that the industry has stopped bragging about how many tokens it can burn and started asking what each one buys. Replit has launched a Free Mode on its paid plans that runs everyday chat and routine tasks on a cheaper OpenAI model without consuming credits, which the company says stretches the twenty dollar tier by as much as thirty times, and it is only viable because that model's price dropped by eighty percent. Replit's president called making it the default radical. In the same week, Ramp launched a router that matches every request to the cheapest model that still meets the performance bar, claiming average savings of forty percent, and a widely shared essay argued that enterprises should be optimising for intelligence consumed per successful outcome rather than reaching for a frontier model by reflex. When cheap stops meaning stupid, the economics of an awful lot of products change. Via The Rundown and TLDR.

Uber has now shown its working on the same problem, which is rare and useful. You may remember the company made headlines earlier this year for burning through its entire twenty twenty-six artificial intelligence budget in four months, after which its chief technology officer capped employee usage and declared the era of maximising tokens over. He has since posted what they did instead. They call them agentic pods: just two people each, one engineer who is fluent with these tools and one domain expert from finance, marketing or operations, running a ten day sprint in which the engineer shadows the expert, builds alongside them and ships. One result was a financial pacing report that used to take a senior person two full days and now runs in ten minutes. The lesson for any large organisation is that your first projects have to earn you the right to swing bigger. Via The Rundown.

And Stripe has formally confirmed its acquisition of OpenRouter, with the more interesting reading of the deal being about data rather than payments. OpenRouter routes something in the order of ten trillion tokens a day across many different model providers, giving it a cross-network view of model behaviour that no single laboratory can match, and the argument is that Stripe wants to underwrite safety across the whole ecosystem the way it already underwrites payment security. Via TLDR and The Rundown.

Now, over to startups and venture.

OpenAI is preparing the ground for a listing. Chief financial officer Sarah Friar told employees the company could go public as soon as twenty twenty-seven, or earlier if growth accelerates, and framed the initial public offering as simply another fundraise rather than a finish line. Coming from a company that has raised at a scale no private business ever has, that framing is probably the most honest thing anyone has said about modern initial public offerings all year. On the funding side, the money is going to hardware and to concentration. Satellite company Muon Space raised two hundred and fifty million dollars in a Series C led by Eclipse with Google and Salesforce Ventures joining, one of the largest space infrastructure rounds on record; it calls itself a mission foundry, handling the whole stack from design through constellation deployment and managed orbital services, with eleven satellites up across six launches and a perfect record so far. In London, Callosum raised a hundred million dollars at seed, led by Atomico with the United Kingdom's sovereign artificial intelligence fund participating. And to put all of it in perspective, more than four thousand Californian companies raised roughly three hundred and sixty-six billion dollars this year, more than triple the other forty-nine states combined. Via This Week in Startups.

Which leads neatly into Europe's uncomfortable week. Sifted reports that after the United States briefly restricted foreign access to a leading frontier model earlier this year, the British government is now formally assessing the economic and national security consequences of being cut off from the systems that increasingly run businesses, public services and critical infrastructure. Building a European alternative sounds obvious, and Europe has no shortage of talent or universities, but competing at the frontier now demands a combination of compute, energy and capital that few believe the continent currently has. The more productive version of the debate, and the one Sifted's own reporting keeps circling, is that sovereignty may mean owning proprietary data and local market nuance rather than owning the largest model. Via Sifted.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

The most striking political development this week came from a brand new channel on a video site. Ukraine's ousted defence minister, Mykhailo Fedorov, posted an address to Ukrainians making the most direct challenge yet to President Zelensky's wartime presidency, arguing that democracy cannot be held hostage by Russia and that the country should hold elections. Fedorov is not a marginal figure: he became Ukraine's youngest ever minister at twenty-eight, drove the country near the top of global rankings for digital government, and his early wartime bets on satellite internet and drones paid off handsomely, which is why he was made defence minister in the first place. He was then fired to keep the generals happy, protests followed from younger Ukrainians who saw him as the reformer, and he has declined an invitation to come back. His case is that holding a wartime ballot would neutralise the dictator smear and prove the democracy is worth defending. The counter-arguments are formidable: the constitution forbids elections under martial law, roughly a million soldiers are somewhat busy, about four million people live under occupation, and Moscow would interfere at every level. Poll after poll suggests most Ukrainians do not want wartime elections, and even one of the organisers of the pro-Fedorov protests opposes the idea. Zelensky is not about to lose power. But a nerve has been hit, and it comes in the same week he sacked a senior aide after anti-corruption investigators raided her home. Via International Intrigue.

In markets and fiscal policy, American Treasury bond yields tumbled and gold and silver spiked after the Treasury surprised everyone by saying it would at least double the size of its longer dated buybacks, covering the ten to thirty year part of the curve. The actual sums are small, moving from two billion dollars to four billion against a thirty trillion dollar market, but the timing tells you why it worked: thirty year yields had just hit their highest level since two thousand and seven, and total debt has crossed forty trillion dollars. By effectively swapping long debt for short, the Treasury secretary is reaching for the same playbook used in nineteen sixty-one and again in twenty eleven, buying cheaper long term borrowing costs without printing money. The market clearly heard the intended message. Via International Intrigue.

And child safety online is turning into the sharpest regulatory front of the year. United States senators are demanding answers from TikTok about an experiment that switched off an algorithmic safeguard for ten percent of users, among whom was a sixteen-year-old who later died by suicide. Separately, a regulator has found that Roblox's much publicised child safety protections still leave four major gaps. Set that alongside the trial already under way against Meta and the pattern is unmistakable: the accountability question for platforms has moved from data privacy to product design. Via This Week in Startups. Briefly, on trade, Washington has delayed its fifty percent tariffs on Canada by a further three days pending the finalisation of documents around a tentative deal, an extension that expires at the end of today. Via International Intrigue.

Now, over to community, events and marketing, and one idea worth stealing.

Smart glasses are quietly becoming an event policy problem. Shipments are expected to reach thirteen point six million this year and to double by twenty thirty, and unlike the Google Glass era, these are actually being worn. Skift Meetings reports that many models have workarounds allowing a wearer to record covertly, and that some meeting planners have already updated their policies to ban them outright. The idea worth stealing is not the ban itself, it is the timing. The planners writing a smart glasses clause this quarter are the ones who saw the problem before it walked into the room. Whatever your equivalent is, decide the policy while it is still hypothetical, put it in the registration terms, and say it out loud from the stage on day one, because trying to invent a recording rule in the middle of a session never goes well.

The second idea is about format. Scan Club argues we have entered an episodic, long form era in social content, and you can see brands acting on it: the clothing rental service Nuuly is betting on microdramas, short serialised story formats rather than one off posts. For anyone running a community or a brand account, the shift is from thinking in posts to thinking in series. A single good post is forgotten by Thursday. A weekly instalment that people expect gives you something a feed cannot easily take away. Via Marketing Brew and Scan Club.

Now, one interesting thing to leave you with.

We finally have a serious answer on whether unconditional cash makes people happier, and it is more sobering than either camp would like. A new study in the Quarterly Journal of Economics followed a thousand low income people given a thousand dollars a month, unconditionally, for three years, against a control group of two thousand people receiving fifty dollars a month. Recipients earned about nineteen hundred dollars a year less from other sources, participation in the labour market fell by just over four percentage points, and both recipients and their partners cut their working hours by one to two hours a week, with the largest gain in time going to leisure. There was no measurable improvement in the quality of the jobs people held, and no significant effect on completing degrees. And the finding that stays with you: reported well-being was genuinely higher in the first year, and then drifted back to match the control group entirely. Via Marginal Revolution.
