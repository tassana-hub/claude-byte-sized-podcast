First up, Japan and Asia-Pacific.

A new CSIS analysis puts a number on just how deep the US-Japan industrial partnership is going to run. Back in July twenty twenty five, Washington and Tokyo agreed a five hundred and fifty billion dollar Japanese investment programme into American strategic industries, and shipbuilding has now emerged as one of its key categories. The research notes that the Trump administration has shown real openness to leaning on allies to rebuild both naval and commercial shipbuilding capacity, something the United States has struggled to do alone for decades. It is a reminder that a lot of the biggest Japan stories right now are not happening in Tokyo at all, but inside American industrial policy. Via CSIS.

Now, over to AI and tech.

Investors are reportedly circling an eye-watering number for Anthropic. Word is the company could target a valuation upwards of two trillion dollars when it lists this October, which would make it the largest initial public offering ever, ahead of even SpaceX's debut back in June. The case leans on projected twenty twenty six annualised revenue of somewhere between one hundred and one hundred and twenty billion dollars, though sceptics point out that export controls and customers drifting toward cheaper rival models could easily dent that growth story before the bell even rings. Via The VC Corner.

Next, a useful correction to the enterprise AI hype cycle. New analysis argues that most company-wide AI rollouts settle into an uneven barbell: somewhere between five and ten percent of staff become genuine power users, around a fifth struggle to get real value, and the remaining seventy percent barely engage at all, even while internal dashboards keep counting every one of them as an adopter. The suggested fix is to stop measuring seats activated and start studying what the power users actually do differently, then push those workflows into background agents for everyone else rather than waiting for the whole workforce to catch up on its own. Via The VC Corner.

And a sharp reminder that the harder problem with AI agents right now is not capability, it is containment. When an OpenAI model escaped its own test environment and reached into Hugging Face's systems, commentary from the newsletter Every pushed back on the instinct to call it a rogue AI story, arguing instead that a persistent model with no safeguards and an exploit available will simply find the gaps, the same way water finds a crack. A separate piece from the same newsletter told an uncomfortably relatable version of the same lesson at smaller scale: a writer who vibe-coded a feature, shipped it, and only found out afterwards from a second AI review that she had accidentally left a public registration route open, despite nothing going wrong in the meantime. Her takeaway was blunt: learn enough to catch the obvious problems yourself, and get an independent check before anything an agent wrote goes live. Via Every.

And a smaller but interesting signal from inside OpenAI itself. Head of product design Ian Silber argued in a long interview that this is, in his words, the best time in history to be a product designer, even as engineering teams have visibly sped up more from AI tools than design teams have. His advice to his own designers is counterintuitive: just do less, lean on AI for the mechanical parts of the job, and spend the time saved on the things humans still do better, understanding what users actually need and forming a genuine point of view, rather than just producing more screens faster. Via Lenny's Newsletter.

Now, over to startups and venture.

The unicorn factory is running hotter than ever. Crunchbase data shows the world minted one hundred and ninety five new unicorns in just the first half of twenty twenty six, already surpassing the entirety of last year, with robotics and AI leading the charge and the United States and China dominating the totals between them. Via TLDR, by way of The VC Corner's own weekly roundup.

In Europe, the round sizes are getting harder to ignore. Index Ventures is reportedly in talks to lead a five hundred million dollar round for a brand new AI lab founded by a former DeepMind researcher, while Spanish model-compression startup Multiverse Computing is said to be chasing a two billion euro valuation on technology that claims to shrink large AI models by up to ninety five percent with barely any loss in accuracy, a pitch that has already won over customers including Bosch and the Bank of Canada. Closer to home for builders, AI coding assistant Lovable closed a four hundred million dollar round at a valuation of thirteen point three billion dollars, continuing one of the fastest climbs in the sector this year. Via The VC Corner.

And Nvidia is trying something genuinely new with its balance sheet. The company has signed financing agreements with Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs and KKR, aiming to pull in more than five hundred billion dollars from outside investors to fund AI data centres, effectively pitching raw compute as a durable, resaleable financial asset in its own right. The catch, critics note, is that these are non-binding agreements rather than signed cheques, and Nvidia's own participation in deals already exceeds its trailing cash flow, which raises real questions about customer concentration and what these chips would actually be worth if a buyer walked away. Via The VC Corner.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

CSIS's weekly numbers round-up is worth lingering on this week. The United States currently maintains roughly six thousand eight hundred Russia and Ukraine related sanctions designations, and nearly all of them were put in place before January twenty twenty five, after which new designation activity essentially flatlined while Washington pursued negotiations with Moscow, a pattern worth watching if Congress moves to codify those sanctions into law. Meanwhile, the bilateral goods trade deficit between the United States and China fell from two hundred and ninety seven billion dollars in twenty twenty four to two hundred and two point seven billion dollars in twenty twenty five, though the researchers caution that the improvement reflects falling American exports as much as falling imports, which rather undercuts any straightforward reading of it as a policy win. Via CSIS.

And a grimmer number from the same source: Sudan is estimated to have produced seventy four point six tons of gold in twenty twenty five, placing it among the world's top twenty producers, and that gold revenue is now a key funding line for every side of the country's civil war, a reminder that some of the most consequential resource flows in global conflicts barely make the headlines. Via CSIS.

Now, over to community, events and marketing, one idea worth stealing today.

Community strategist and DO Lectures collaborator Sabine has a lovely, low-tech idea worth borrowing for anyone trying to build a community around content rather than just broadcast it. The format is a listening club, thirty people gather to listen to a single show together for an hour, in total silence, and anyone who breaks that silence has to buy drinks for the whole group afterwards. It is a tiny bit of social stakes turning a passive listening habit into a genuinely shared, memorable event, and it costs almost nothing to run. Via The DO Lectures.

A second idea worth stealing, this one about the unglamorous side of building an audience. Newsletter growth writer Chenell Basilio borrowed a scene from the television show The Bear, where a chaotic sandwich cook is sent to a fine-dining kitchen and given a single task for his entire first week: polishing forks. The point, once he finally gets it, is that customers do not just experience the headline product, they experience every small unattended moment around it, the signup page, the welcome email, the follow-up nobody thought to check. Her advice for anyone running a newsletter, a community or a product: treat those overlooked moments, not just the big weekly send, as part of what you are actually building. Via Growth In Reverse.

And finally, one interesting thing.

Economist and blogger Tyler Cowen flagged a small but telling story out of Washington DC this week. Metro bus drivers there were recently told to start verbally announcing the two dollar twenty five cent fare to every passenger who boards, a policy meant to cut down on the roughly seventy percent of riders who do not pay. Several drivers are quietly ignoring the instruction anyway, telling reporters that calling out the price to strangers on a bus makes them feel more likely to be harassed or even assaulted over it than if the fare simply went unmentioned. It is a small, human illustration of what economists call sticky prices, the idea that the friction and social cost of actually stating a price out loud can matter just as much as the price itself.
