Good morning, and welcome to Byte-sized. It is Friday the thirtieth of October, and today there are six sections, beginning, as ever, in Japan and the Asia Pacific.

Section one, Japan and Asia Pacific.

There is no brand new Japan story in this morning's mail beyond yesterday's G M O breach, so here is the wider region.

First, North Korea. Seoul's Joint Chiefs of Staff say Pyongyang fired more than ten short-range ballistic missiles towards the East Sea on Thursday. A salvo of that size, so soon after Kim Jong-un's border trip to China, is a reminder that the diplomacy and the military signalling are running side by side. For anyone with people or supply chains in Japan or Korea, it is background risk worth keeping in view. Via the C S I S Nuclear Policy News, citing the Korea Herald.

Second, China's Fifth Plenum, which is meeting this week. International Intrigue's read is that the theme is the Communist Party's own discipline, not the next Five-Year Plan, reflecting Xi Jinping's frustration that officials, terrified of purges, now push every decision up the chain rather than act. Attendance is expected to be the lowest in decades, with seventy-one senior civilian and military figures investigated in the first nine months of the year, and the Central Military Commission is down to just two members, one of them Xi himself. The thing to watch is whether the commission is rebuilt, and who from the next generation of cadres gets promoted ahead of next year's Party Congress. Via International Intrigue.

Third, Hong Kong's RedotPay is reportedly preparing a confidential American listing by the end of the year, at a valuation of up to five billion dollars, with J P Morgan and Goldman Sachs advising. It says it has more than nine million customers and annualised revenue above one hundred and eighty million dollars, though it is also in a four hundred and seventy-three million dollar legal dispute with Binance. Asian stablecoin payments companies are heading for Wall Street. Via T L D R.

Fourth, a small story with a large lesson. A luxury cruise ship attempting a years-long world voyage has suspended operations and left around two hundred mostly American passengers stranded in Cambodia. Fuel costs per cabin rose from about eighteen hundred dollars a month to eight thousand after the Strait of Hormuz was squeezed. International Intrigue's point is that low-margin, fuel-dependent business models will keep failing while prices stay high. Via International Intrigue.

Now, over to artificial intelligence and tech.

Section two, A I and tech.

First, Google's Gemini 4 Carbon. It is a new frontier model aimed at cybersecurity and long-running coding work, and Google's charts show it ahead on fifteen of seventeen benchmarks. It scores fifty-nine on the Artificial Analysis intelligence index, taking the top spot from Claude Opus 5.5. The introductory price is two dollars in and ten out per million tokens, half of Opus, though it rises later. The catch is that only select cyber defenders can use it so far, so nobody outside Google can yet check the claims. Via The Rundown and T L D R.

Second, the compute scramble continues. The Information reports that OpenAI plans to buy Google's T P U chips outright, beyond renting them, as another source of capacity alongside Nvidia and its custom Broadcom chip. Meanwhile Microsoft's Azure grew forty-eight per cent to thirty-three billion dollars in the quarter, and Microsoft now reports Azure revenue in dollars. Chips and clouds are being traded between rivals more freely than at any time in this boom. Via T L D R.

Third, the Anthropic prospectus is giving the industry a lot to chew on. Analysts say it shows sixteen point three billion dollars of first-half revenue, a profitable second quarter, and nearly half of sales routed through Amazon and Google. For enterprise buyers the takeaway is vendor dependency: treat A I procurement as a resilience question, and keep data and workflows portable. Via T L D R.

Fourth, on the tools side, GitHub has launched Copilot Fusion, which automatically routes each coding task to the best model, and Anthropic has released reporting tools showing companies what their A I spending is buying. Both point the same way. The market is moving from which model is best to which model is worth the price. Via T L D R.

Now, over to startups and venture.

Section three, startups and venture.

First, the United Kingdom's budget. Sifted reports a new allowance for young entrepreneurs, capital gains tax relief for founders and changes to the skilled worker visa. Founders and investors called it welcome but overdue. The most useful detail is the advice from the departing entrepreneurship adviser: budget decisions are made about three months before they are announced, so lobby early, and bring policies that are costed. Via Sifted.

Second, French fintech Spiko raised ninety million dollars at an eight hundred million dollar valuation, even though it says it was not actively fundraising. Strong inbound demand, not a roadshow, set the price. Via Sifted.

Third, the cooler side of fintech. Global fintech funding fell twenty-nine per cent year on year in the third quarter to eleven billion dollars, the lowest in two years. Yet Guidewire agreed to buy A I underwriting platform hyperexponential for up to two point one billion dollars, and Ramp says its own coding agent now accounts for seventy-four per cent of its merged code changes. Money is scarcer, but it is concentrating on A I with real revenue. Via T L D R.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

Section four, policy and geopolitics.

First, space. A new Defense Intelligence Agency assessment says China and Russia are developing ways to disrupt or destroy American satellites, including a Russian satellite designed to carry a nuclear weapon into orbit. Anyone selling into government or defence customers should expect satellite resilience to move up the agenda. Via the C S I S Nuclear Policy News, citing Fox News.

Second, Iran. Qatar-led mediators are trying to restart talks between Washington and Tehran to head off renewed strikes after the American midterms, according to Axios. And the Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists argues that although the strikes degraded enrichment, the expertise and the political motivation remain. The election next week matters well beyond America. Via the C S I S Nuclear Policy News.

Third, sanctions in Africa. The United States has sanctioned Rwanda's defence ministry and a state-aligned bank over support for the M23 militia in eastern Congo, including financing arms shipments from China. International Intrigue calls it a big escalation, from targeting individual warlords to cutting official supply lines. Via International Intrigue.

Now, over to community, events and marketing.

Section five, community, events and marketing.

One idea worth stealing, from Marketing Brew. Brand interest in run clubs is not slowing down, and a separate piece covers Major League Baseball bringing more influencers into ballparks. The practical lesson for a community builder is that brands want to attach themselves to groups that already meet in person on a regular rhythm. A regular, low-cost, repeatable format, a weekly run, a monthly walk, a standing drinks night, is a far easier thing to sponsor than a one-off event. Via Marketing Brew.

And finally, one interesting thing.

From Why Is This Interesting: a study of fifteen elite Kenyan runners found their feet spent roughly ten per cent less time on the ground than published figures for elite runners at similar speeds, and they used almost nine per cent less oxygen. The writer's suggestion is that this form is less a technique than an accent, picked up by training in packs where the faster runner sets the pace and the slower one practises holding on. A better stride may come from spending more time beside people who already have one. Via Why Is This Interesting.
