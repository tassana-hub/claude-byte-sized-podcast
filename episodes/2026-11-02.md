Section one, Japan and Asia Pacific.

Nothing from this weekend's mail has a direct Japan angle, so let me bring you two stories from the wider region that bear on trade and travel.

First, China's trade pivot, from the weekly By the Numbers note at C S I S. China's total exports rose by five point five per cent in twenty twenty-five, even though its exports to the United States fell by about twenty per cent. Much of the displaced trade has been rerouted to the European Union. The C S I S author, Hugh Grant-Chapman, argues that cracking down on transshipment, meaning goods routed through third countries, will not fix the American trade imbalance with China, because the underlying export machine simply finds another destination. For anyone talking to government or manufacturing contacts in Japan or Southeast Asia, the point to take away is that tariffs have moved Chinese goods around the map rather than shrinking them, and Europe is now feeling the pressure. Via C S I S.

Second, travel in India. MakeMyTrip, the big Indian online travel company, reported a weaker second quarter, with declines in air ticketing and in hotel and package revenue. It blamed subdued outbound travel. It is a small signal, but a useful one if you follow regional consumer demand: Indian travellers are holding back on trips abroad, which matters for destinations, including Japan, that have been counting on strong Indian arrivals. Via PhocusWire.

Now, over to A I and tech.

Section two, A I and tech.

First, a thought from Sam Altman, picked out by Byrne Hobart in The Diff. In a conversation with John Collison, Altman noted that, so far, high-profile software vulnerabilities have not caused the chaos you might reasonably expect, and that the absence of disaster headlines is itself informative. He also suggested we may be due another scaling law, because nobody yet knows the best trade-off between the number of agents and how clever each one is, or what happens when you mix different kinds of agents together. Will agents that argue more be more effective or less? It is an open question, and a reminder that the next big gains may come from how models are organised rather than how large they are. Via The Diff.

Second, a travel technology story with wider lessons about A I agents. Hotels are starting to worry about what the industry calls re-shopping. That is where a personal A I assistant books a room, then quietly cancels and rebooks if it finds a cheaper price. Bookings made through assistants such as Meta's Muse are still at very low volumes, but hotel executives and their technology suppliers are already worried about the cost of cancellations and rate integrity. In a related move, the travel company Etraveli has added an M C P layer to its flight content, so that A I agents and developers can search its air inventory through Claude, ChatGPT or other compatible platforms. Taken together, they show where agents are heading: they are becoming real customers, with all the awkward behaviour that implies. Via PhocusWire.

Third, from Every, a newsletter item on how agents now choose what you read. The headline essay looks at readers handing their reading lists to software, and the same issue notes that Every has automated its own memes, features an Emmy-winning television writer building an A I writers' room, and asks what Anthropic's purchase of a one-person biotech company really signals. I have only the teasers, but the theme is consistent: the editor, and even the writer, is increasingly an agent. Via Every.

Now, over to startups and venture.

Section three, startups and venture.

First, what do thirty successful A I pitch decks have in common? The V C Corner read three hundred and two slides from thirty decks that sit behind about five hundred and thirty-one million dollars of funding. The median round was ten million dollars, and the largest cheques, seventy-five million each, went to Scribe and Happy Health. The pattern is useful. Strong decks put traction on slide two, not the problem. One company opened with one and a half million dollars of annual recurring revenue twelve months after launch. Only three decks showed the round size, and all three closed above what they had asked for. Pricing tends to scale with customer usage, the moat is the data the product collects, and almost nobody shows gross margin, which is an opening for anyone who does. Via The V C Corner.

Second, a venture structure to watch. This week The Diff looks at tranched rounds, where different investors in the same round pay different prices. It is an extension of what venture has always done, with bigger investors getting board seats and information rights, but it creates awkward incentives, because investors in the same round are no longer aligned on price. If you are advising founders, it is worth reading term sheets for exactly this. Via The Diff.

Third, the same newsletter makes a quietly good point about retail turnarounds, drawn from a New Yorker profile of James Daunt, who runs Barnes and Noble. The chain had drifted into being a mediocre toy shop that happened to sell books. Fixing it required a boss with the credibility to accept lower revenue for a while in order to win back foot traffic. It is a neat reminder that a real turnaround needs a mandate, not just a plan. Via The Diff.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

Section four, policy and geopolitics.

First, back to C S I S, whose weekly numbers include a striking figure on drones. Around four hundred and eighty weaponised drone attacks took place in Latin America between January twenty twenty-five and August twenty twenty-six, heavily concentrated in Mexico and Colombia but spreading across the region. C S I S's Henry Ziemer argues that the United States has a lot to learn from how Latin American forces and police have had to defend against them. It is a good example of a security threat that arrives in the Americas first, and then travels. Via C S I S.

Second, a more unusual idea. Another C S I S study argues that offshore wind farms, sitting ten to forty miles from shore, are well placed to carry sensors that strengthen maritime surveillance. Pitching energy infrastructure as a national security asset may be the way to win political support, at a time when offshore wind is under pressure. Via C S I S.

Third, a sobering statistic on funding. United States State Department spending on democracy, human rights and governance programmes fell by nearly seventy-five per cent between fiscal years twenty twenty-four and twenty twenty-five, including cuts to funds meant to protect human rights defenders. C S I S's paper looks at how business might step in. Via C S I S.

Now, over to community, events and marketing.

Section five, community, events and marketing.

Here are three ideas worth stealing.

First, from Lenny's podcast: Hector Serrano, head of content at the A I voice tool Wispr Flow, runs a roster of about fifty creators who produce five to six hundred paid user-generated ads every month. They are paid a cut of the ad spend, and the best earn more than thirteen thousand dollars a month. Revenue has grown thirty times in a year, and the company has raised at a two billion dollar valuation. His claim is that paid creator content, long used by consumer brands, is still a huge untapped lever for software. The idea for community builders: pay your most enthusiastic members to tell their own story, rather than writing the message yourself. Via Lenny's Newsletter.

Second, from Indie Hackers, a thirty-second growth tip. Put a small powered-by link in the spaces your customers' users will see. The founder of the feedback tool Upvoty did this, and some of those users became customers, helping it past twenty thousand dollars a month. Any event or community that supplies tools to others can do the same with a modest credit line. Via Indie Hackers.

Third, from the D O Lectures, a gentle idea for gatherings: the Empty Chairs project invites you to book a table in a pub or café and leave a few chairs empty, so that anyone who wants company can join. It is a low-cost format that could suit any meet-up. Via The D O Lectures.

Now, one interesting thing to finish.

Section six, one interesting thing.

From The Diff, via Works in Progress, comes a striking idea about the end of the Bronze Age. Bronze needs tin, and tin is scarce, so Bronze Age states had to be large enough to arrange imports from as far away as Cornwall and Afghanistan. Once ironworking arrived, with its shorter supply chains, the best size for a state shrank, and trade declined. So one of history's great technological advances was accompanied by less economic complexity, not more. It is an unusual thought for a Monday: sometimes progress shortens the supply chain.
