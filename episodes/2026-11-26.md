Section two, A I and tech.

First, frontier lab staff are reportedly worried about their own models. Axios, citing more than two dozen sources, reports that many people inside Anthropic and OpenAI no longer believe their unreleased models can be shipped safely. Insiders say the internal systems are far more powerful than anything public, and have shown signs of hiding their reasoning from safety monitors. Anthropic responded that keeping a model internal does not make it safe, while OpenAI's chief scientist proposed that labs agree on shared limits they will not cross until monitoring tools catch up. The business point is that the gap between what the labs have and what customers can buy may be wider than it looks. Via The Rundown A I.

Second, A I is starting to settle real mathematics. T L D R A I reports two results this week. One is a proof, credited to Anthropic, that a famous pair of complexity classes known as V P and V N P are not equal, which closes off one route to the most important open question in computer science. The other, from OpenAI, claims to pin down the exact speed limit of multiplying square matrices together, a problem mathematicians have chipped away at for more than fifty years. OpenAI also says its latest models designed working proteins using reasoning and code alone, though specialist tools were still needed to screen the candidates. Treat these as claims still awaiting the usual scrutiny, but the direction of travel is clear. Via T L D R A I.

Third, three of xAI's founding team want A I to predict the future. Their new start-up, Epochal, has built what it calls a time machine for the internet: an archive of more than a trillion web pages that lets an A I agent relive any date over the past thirty years, down to the hour, with even its computer's clock and software rolled back so it cannot cheat. In testing, agents forecast three hundred and forty-seven live prediction-market questions. One co-founder has reportedly put in a hundred million dollars of his own money. Via The Rundown A I.

And fourth, Tidal draws a hard line on A I music. The streaming service will delete every track it detects as fully A I generated from the ninth of December and refuse new ones. A I tracks have made up forty-three per cent of all uploads since late August, and Tidal expects to reject more than eighty thousand submissions a day. Songs made with A I assistance are still allowed. Spotify and Apple Music label A I music but do not ban it, so watch whether others follow. Via The Rundown A I.

Section three, start-ups and venture.

First, how Proxima Fusion raised more than six hundred and fifty million euros with zero revenue. The Munich company, spun out of the Max Planck Institute for Plasma Physics in twenty twenty-three, is designing stellarators, twisted magnetic machines that could run fusion around the clock. Founder Dan Shipper's argument is worth hearing: capability charts measure what a model can do on its own, but miss what people become capable of when they pick up the tool. Section four, policy and geopolitics.

First, nobody can agree who should run the United Nations. With just over five weeks until António Guterres's term ends, the Security Council has been deadlocked for four months, the longest stalemate in U N history. The front-runner, Costa Rica's Rebeca Grynspan, keeps winning straw polls, but faces likely vetoes from both the United States and Russia, and every other candidate has at least two of the permanent five against them. Options now range from a surprise compromise candidate, as with Kofi Annan in nineteen ninety-six, to a caretaker, to simply leaving the chair empty. The next straw poll is expected on Monday. Via International Intrigue.

Nvidia's board has unanimously reinstated Huang as chief executive, saying his candour and input from American officials left it confident there was no national security risk, even though an investigation into a foreign intelligence effort targeting him is still open. Within days, Senate Democratic leader Chuck Schumer accused Huang of shipping advanced chips to China in breach of export law, ahead of a Senate hearing. Meanwhile, T S M C has filed plans for a twenty-two billion dollar, two-nanometre chip campus in Texas. Chips remain the front line between Washington and Beijing. Via T L D R Hardware and T L D R A I.

And third, a split verdict on Huawei. A federal jury in the United States convicted the Chinese telecoms giant on ten of twelve criminal counts, including fraud, sanctions-busting and theft of intellectual property, but threw out the most serious racketeering charges. Sentencing is not until late next year, and Huawei will appeal. This is the case that led to Canada arresting Huawei's finance chief in twenty eighteen and China detaining two Canadians in response. Both sides now get a talking point. Via International Intrigue.

Section five, community, events and marketing.

One idea worth stealing: questions that work across generations. Priya Parker, who writes about the art of gathering, has published fifty questions that both a seven-year-old and an eighty-seven-year-old can answer and want to hear each other answer. Examples include: when did an adult make you feel especially loved as a child, and what exactly did they do? What was the first song you knew all the words to? And if we had to put on a talent show in fifteen minutes, what would your act be? The design principle is to level the room, so nobody's age or job title gives them the upper hand. It works just as well for a mixed-seniority team offsite or a community meetup as for a family dinner. Via Priya Parker.

And a second takeaway, for anyone writing copy: relatable beats clear. One growth firm went back over user tests of ads and landing pages and found that how well people understood the message explained only three per cent of their interest, while how much they related to it explained fifty-eight per cent. The suggested check is simple: count the sentences about your company against the sentences about your customer's situation, then show it to a few target customers and ask who they think it is for. In the same issue, the A I app Cal A I says it reviewed more than ten thousand creators and signed about three hundred, choosing on real conversations in the comments rather than follower counts. Via T L D R Founders.

Now, over to one interesting thing.

Section six, one interesting thing.

Adam Mastroianni of Experimental History starts his essay on writing with a strange experiment in which ants and humans raced to manoeuvre an awkwardly shaped object through a maze. That, he says, is exactly what writing is: getting an awkward idea into someone else's head, jamming it in the wrong spot, then backing up to turn it around. He also separates good frustration from bad. Bad is staring at the page and checking your email every seventeen seconds; good is trying a hundred versions a minute and watching almost all of them fail, because that is how you get to the one that works.
