Section one, Japan and Asia Pacific.

First, energy security, which matters enormously to Japan. CSIS's weekly numbers digest points to at least seven new or expanded pipeline projects designed to bypass the Strait of Hormuz. Goldman Sachs estimates they could add almost four million barrels a day of export capacity by the end of twenty twenty seven, and over seven million by the end of twenty twenty eight. CSIS's accompanying study warns that Gulf energy infrastructure remains a target after the conflict. Japan and its Asian neighbours buy the bulk of their crude from the Gulf, so every barrel that can leave without passing through Hormuz is a direct reduction in risk for the region. Via CSIS.

Second, a quieter signal for anyone watching Asian outsourcing and services economies. Marginal Revolution reports that global call centre headcount is now five point three percent below its December twenty twenty three peak, and has fallen year on year for eight straight quarters, a decline that started in high income countries. My own reading, not the source's: if automation is eating customer service first, the pressure will reach the service hubs of the Philippines and India, and Japanese firms that offshore support should expect that market to shift. Via Marginal Revolution.

Third, the same CSIS digest notes that digital commerce and AI enabled services are expected to make up nearly twenty percent of global output by twenty twenty eight, and cites a CSIS paper on the Texas semiconductor ecosystem, noting that a modern chip fabrication plant uses around five million gallons of water a day. That is a useful reminder for Japan's own fab ambitions in Kumamoto and Hokkaido, where water and power are as strategic as subsidies. Via CSIS.

Now, over to artificial intelligence and technology.

Section two, AI and tech.

First, a rare candid interview with Microsoft's head of Copilot, Jacob Andreou, in The Rundown. Three lessons stood out. One: Microsoft removed Copilot from several Windows apps after finding the entry points brought traffic but little value. Fewer entry points led to more use per person, which he sums up as products having to earn their right to exist. Two: no single lab's model wins at everything any more, so Copilot's Auto mode picks a model for you, because asking professionals to track which model suits which job is itself a tax. Three: his rule for agent autonomy is to sort actions into one way doors and two way doors. Anything irreversible, such as contacting someone or deleting a file, needs a human every time; reversible work can run inside clear boundaries. If your company is piloting agents, writing down your one way doors first is a very cheap exercise. Via The Rundown.

Second, Patrick Collison's notes on the economics of agents, shared by Tyler Cowen. His argument is that firms have long profited from consumers being inattentive, with insurance upsells at the end of a booking as the classic case. Personal agents that act for the consumer will erode that, making some price discrimination and subscription cross subsidies work less well, and acting as a structural subsidy for genuinely better products. Cowen adds a warning: suppliers may use agents to collude, so some prices could drift up and then drop suddenly. The takeaway for builders is that product quality, and data that proves it, becomes worth more once agents do the shopping. Via Marginal Revolution.

Third, Every launched the Every Agent, a single AI coworker in Slack shared by the whole company, built on Claude Managed Agents. Their write up covers why they handed the infrastructure to Anthropic, how they cut running costs, and a finding worth remembering: past a point, adding more instructions can make the model worse. Via Every.

Fourth, a look back from The Diff. Byrne Hobart points to Dario Amodei's twenty seventeen essay on what he called the big blob of compute, the idea that intelligence emerges from scale rather than a tidy theory. Almost a decade on, the industry has followed that playbook. Hobart finds it both impressive and a little deflating, since it suggests progress came more from brute force than from understanding. Via The Diff.

Now, over to startups and venture.

Section three, startups and venture.

First, a founder case study from Indie Hackers. Nic Mauro built Virlo, a tool that shows agencies what works across short form video on TikTok, Reels and Shorts. Revenue went from about one thousand dollars a month in March twenty twenty five to more than thirty one thousand now. Two choices stand out: he rebuilt the offer around the research workflow of agencies, who liked the product most, and he built his own data collection rather than renting it, which gave him control over quality, cost and the interface. He also swapped a hard paywall for a free trial. Via Indie Hackers.

Second, Lenny Rachitsky's interview with Josh Woodward, who runs Google Labs, the Gemini app and AI Studio. Two ideas from the outline: an almost possible framework for choosing which products to build, meaning things that nearly work today and will work when the next model lands, and a discipline of knowing when to kill an idea. His view that every company now needs a small labs team is a useful prompt for any startup deciding how to allocate experiments. Via Lenny's Newsletter.

Third, a question from a reader of The Diff: will AI agents make banking more efficient and force banks to pay higher interest? Hobart's answer is that consumer inertia is strong but agents are compounding fast, and people tend to grant agents broad permissions in big chunks. For basic accounts, below the deposit insurance limit, banks are close to identical, so agentic comparison shopping will show where margins end up in any product with low differentiation and high search costs. Via The Diff.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

Section four, policy and geopolitics.

First, California. The New Yorker's look at how a wealth tax reached the state ballot, summarised by The Diff, shows how initiatives are often tabled as bargaining chips: proponents float something popular but impractical, then withdraw it in exchange for a concession. This year one campaign dropped a proposal to limit hospital executive pay in return for the hospital association not placing hurdles in front of union political campaigns. A reminder that ballot politics in the world's fifth largest economy is partly legislation by negotiation. Via The Diff.

Second, CSIS's study on refugee self reliance. A World Bank and UN refugee agency estimate from late twenty twenty four suggests that giving refugees full economic participation would let them cover ninety percent of their own needs, cutting international assistance for refugee subsistence in the Middle East and North Africa from six point two billion dollars a year to about seven hundred million. For a donor government, that is the case for work permits over aid cheques. Via CSIS.

Third, travel regulation. A new coalition of aviation and technology bodies is lobbying the European Union to ease restrictions on voluntary biometrics in air travel, asking for stronger recognition of consent and technology neutral rules within the Digital Omnibus package. If you work with airlines or airports, this is a live policy file. Via PhocusWire.

Now, over to community, events and marketing.

Section five, community, events and marketing. Practical takeaways rather than news.

One idea worth stealing, for event organisers: the Event Design Collective is reviewing the IMEX trade show not by scoring it but by asking what it is actually designed to create, and for whom. Their questions are a handy checklist for your own events: what is the intended value, which stakeholders matter, where are the tensions, and what culture does the event create? Meetings booked is a metric, not a purpose. Via Event Design Collective.

A second idea, for product marketers, from Indie Hackers: make an educational product look like the game your students already play. Boot dev, which teaches back end programming, wrapped its courses in a fantasy role playing look with experience points and achievements, which also gave it a natural reason to market in gaming communities. Its peak revenue reached one hundred and ten thousand dollars a month. Via Indie Hackers.

And a defensive lesson, from PhocusWire. A hospitality technology firm found one point one million ads in a single month that hijacked hotel branded searches, with look alike sites persuading travellers they were booking direct. If you run a brand, search your own name in ads once a month. Via PhocusWire.

Now, for one last thing.

Section six, one interesting thing. The Diff recommends A Fabulous Debt, a history of bonds, and one fact stuck. Arguably the first leveraged buyout was also the birth of the bond: when Venice needed money for its fleet, wealthy residents were compelled to lend to the state in exchange for tradable bonds paying five percent. Once a promise could be separated from the original lender and sold on, capital became mobile, and governments could bind their successors to decisions, which is how Lincoln's railway got through Congress after the opposing legislators had left for the Confederacy. Via The Diff.
