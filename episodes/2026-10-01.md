Section one, Japan and Asia Pacific. Four items today, taking in Tokyo's cheque book, a dangerous morning on the Korean border, a rare piece of maritime law, and a long view of Japanese history.

First, Japan is putting real money behind its friendships in Central Asia. Mongolia's prime minister, Nyam-Osor Uchral, met his Japanese counterpart, Prime Minister Takaichi, in Tokyo and came away with a pledge of around one billion dollars in low interest loans to expand the international airport outside Ulaanbaatar. Tokyo paid for the original airport, so extending it is a natural step, especially with Mongolian tourism booming after the pandemic. But it also fits Mongolia's so-called third neighbour strategy, in which a landlocked country squeezed between China and Russia keeps courting friendly democracies to balance its two giant neighbours. Via International Intrigue.

Next, a mystery on the Korean peninsula. Last week an eighteen person South Korean patrol, walking on its own side of the Military Demarcation Line, was hit by two blasts. Three soldiers were wounded, and one officer lost a foot. After a week of theories, South Korea's defence minister said the most likely explanation is that North Korea planted mines on the southern side, a clear breach of the nineteen fifty three armistice, and that Seoul will take what he called corresponding measures. Pyongyang denies everything. The politics is just as tense as the geography: the conservative opposition says President Lee was slow to name the culprit because he didn't want to spoil his conciliatory message at the United Nations, while hawks and doves are again arguing about how hard to respond. For Japan, which sits squarely in range of any escalation, it's one to watch. Via International Intrigue.

Third, a quiet but significant legal process in Southeast Asia. Thailand and Cambodia have begun compulsory conciliation under the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea, to sort out overlapping maritime claims in the Gulf of Thailand. It's only the second time this mechanism has ever been used, the first being Timor-Leste against Australia in twenty sixteen, which ended in a boundary treaty. The catch is that the two sides arrived with different goals: Thailand wants a clean boundary, while Cambodia wants flexible joint development of the seabed resources in the meantime. The next round is in The Hague from the fourteenth of October. If it works, it strengthens the case for using international law against bigger powers in places like the South China Sea. If it fails, it deepens splits inside ASEAN. Via the CSIS Asia Maritime Transparency Initiative.

And fourth, something for the long view. Tomas Pueyo's Uncharted Territories newsletter published a piece arguing that almost all of Japanese history can be read from its geography. Scattered plains divided by mountains produced feudalism and the samurai, the Seto Inland Sea acted as a unifying highway, and the shogunate's obsession with unity explains why Japan sealed itself off for two and a half centuries. He then lays out four permanent Japanese priorities: unity at home, control of the island chains leading to Japan, keeping the continental powers in check, and secure resource supplies by sea. His conclusion for today is that Japan's best strategy is to stay close to America, contain North Korea, and keep China from becoming a threatening giant next door. Via Uncharted Territories.

OpenAI has launched what it calls Dots, agents powered by its GPT six Astra model that run tasks on their own cloud computer rather than inside a chat window. It's the clearest sign yet that the big labs see always on, autonomous agents as the next product category. Via TLDR.

Together with an eighty per cent price cut on the smaller Luna model, it feeds a broader story of price competition: one analysis this week suggested that both OpenAI and Anthropic are pushing towards a hundred billion dollars in annual revenue by the end of the year, helped by smarter customer segmentation and pricing. Via TLDR.

In hardware, AMD has agreed to acquire World Labs, the startup founded around spatial intelligence, meaning AI models that understand the three dimensional physical world. The logic is to join frontier research in physical AI with chip design, which matters for robotics and any machine that has to navigate real space. Via TLDR Hardware.

And in space, SpaceX's Starship reached orbit for the first time on its fourteenth test flight on Monday, despite one engine shutting down mid flight. It deployed twenty six next generation Starlink satellites, and each Starship can carry around sixty of them, roughly twenty times what a Falcon nine launch manages. That capacity is what makes Starlink's longer term ambitions look credible. Via TLDR Hardware.

And a founder story with a lesson. The VC Corner summarised an interview with Luca Ferrari of Bending Spoons, the company that runs Evernote, WeTransfer, Meetup and AOL. Evernote had about three hundred and fifty staff when it was acquired and is now run by around twenty. Ferrari's explanation is talent density: Bending Spoons received eight hundred thousand applications last year and hired fewer than three hundred people, scoring candidates on over a hundred small signals, including how they treat the recruiting coordinator. The company also has no job titles, and anyone who wants to add a process has to justify it. Via The VC Corner.

Section five, community, events and marketing. Here are two ideas worth stealing.

First, from Skift Meetings: cut the long gala dinner. Carnival Cruise Line's president says guests on holiday still refuse to spend ninety minutes in a dining room, so the line now offers express dining that finishes inside an hour, and a flexible option with no fixed seating. The takeaway for event organisers is that after a full day of programming, attendees would often rather have a short seated meal, a walk around format, or a free evening to have the one conversation they came for. Via Skift.

Second, also from Skift, a test for any event you run. Their chief executive wrote that the real job of an event isn't just the follow up emails, it's to change how people feel about the work they are going back to. So when you design your next programme, ask whether your attendees will leave more excited about their role in the industry, and cut whatever doesn't serve that, starting with the inspirational speakers. Via Skift.

Section six, one interesting thing. The writer behind Experimental History is publishing a book called The Strangest Idea in the World, built around a puzzling question: why did it take humans three hundred thousand years to invent the toilet? The same goes for almost everything else; the first randomised controlled trial wasn't run until nineteen forty eight. Worse, he notes the data suggest we're getting worse at making discoveries, with more money, time and people going in and fewer breakthroughs coming out. His argument is that humans didn't evolve to do science, so making breakthroughs means using the mind in ways it was never built for, and that we can get better if we're willing to get weird. Via Experimental History.
