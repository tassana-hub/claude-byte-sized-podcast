Good morning, it's Friday, the seventh of August. Six sections for you today.

Now, over to Japan and Asia-Pacific.

A Gulf state fund is reportedly weighing one of the largest single foreign infrastructure bets in Japan's computing buildout to date. An unnamed United Arab Emirates state investment fund is in talks to put six billion, three hundred million dollars into a new artificial intelligence data centre in the country, according to Bloomberg. It's another sign that Gulf capital is chasing the compute race well beyond the United States, and that Japan is increasingly seen as a serious landing spot for that money. Via This Week in Startups.

Staying in Tokyo, the final instalment of This Week in Startups' summer Tokyo series featured Bespoke founder Akemi Tsunagawa, who has built three separate businesses around the hard reality of Japan's shrinking population, from the Bebot AI travel concierge to robots that can climb scaffolding. She described taking a fake it till you make it approach to growing her brands, and said her core thesis, solving the country's labour gap, has paid off over time. Via This Week in Startups.

Hiroshima marked the eighty first anniversary of the American atomic bombing of the city this week, with the mayor criticising major powers for waging wars around the world and urging them to stop justifying the possession of nuclear weapons as a means of deterrence. The anniversary is a yearly moment when Japan's postwar identity as the only country to have suffered a nuclear attack collides with a present in which nuclear threats are, if anything, becoming more frequent. Via CSIS Project on Nuclear Issues.

And in Southeast Asia, Malaysian prime minister Anwar Ibrahim's governing coalition suffered its third major electoral blow in under a year. Pakatan Harapan lost control of Negeri Sembilan state on the first of August, following earlier losses in Johor and Sabah. The result is straining Anwar's alliance with his Chinese Malaysian coalition partner, the Democratic Action Party, which is due to hold an internal vote later this month on whether to keep serving in his government. Via CSIS Southeast Asia Program.

Now, over to AI and tech.

Google reshuffled its artificial intelligence leadership this week in the biggest shake-up of its AI brain trust in years. DeepMind chief executive Demis Hassabis is moving into a chairman role at the unit and taking on the title of chief scientist at parent company Alphabet, handing day to day control of frontier model work, including Gemini four, to chief technology officer Koray Kavukcuoglu. Meanwhile Jeff Dean, a twenty seven year Google veteran, is departing on friendly terms to launch a new public benefit company called Discovery Loop alongside fellow Google veterans Sanjay Ghemawat, Oriol Vinyals and Quoc Le, aiming to build AI that can accelerate scientific discovery with little human help. Alphabet shares slid around four to five percent on the news, with markets reading it as a sign of strain over delays to the next generation Gemini model. Via TLDR AI and The Rundown.

On the security side, a new variant of the Shai-Hulud worm has torn back through the open source world, compromising more than one thousand two hundred and eighty node package manager packages, including the widely used Keyv library, by injecting malicious commits through hijacked GitHub Actions pipelines. The worm harvests developer and cloud credentials, including Amazon Web Services, Kubernetes and Vault access, and uses them to spread further, meaning any organisation running an affected package should assume a breach and rotate secrets immediately rather than waiting on a routine scan. Via TLDR Information Security.

Adobe has launched a plugin bringing seventy of its creative tools directly into OpenAI's chatbot, letting users edit content without ever leaving the conversation. It's the clearest sign yet that Adobe would rather meet users inside ChatGPT than try to keep them exclusively within its own apps. Via This Week in Startups.

And Anthropic confirmed it is building out its own AI chip design team, hiring chip engineers to co-design custom silicon alongside its models. The aim is to improve Claude's speed and efficiency and to reduce reliance on existing hardware partnerships, joining a growing list of AI labs deciding that owning more of the compute stack is worth the cost and complexity of doing it themselves. Via TLDR AI.

Now, over to startups and venture.

Revolut chief executive Nik Storonsky is reportedly in talks for a new share deal that would see his personal stake balloon if the company hits a five hundred billion dollar valuation. In the same week, prominent AI researcher Yann LeCun joined a new venture capital firm backed by two hundred and forty four limited partners, another sign of senior AI talent drifting from research labs into investing. Via Sifted.

Defence manufacturing startup Hadrian, which counts the Pentagon and Lockheed Martin among its customers, raised a new round valuing the company at seven billion, eight hundred and seventy million dollars, money it plans to use to expand its workforce. Via This Week in Startups.

Optical interconnect startup Lumilens raised seven hundred million dollars at a five billion, five hundred million dollar valuation, aiming to replace traditional data centre wiring with light based connections to speed up data transfer between AI servers. Via This Week in Startups.

And self-improving AI startup Mirendil signed a computing partnership worth more than one hundred million dollars with Google Cloud, expanding the infrastructure behind systems it says are designed to accelerate scientific discovery. It's another data point in a pattern this week, from Discovery Loop to Mirendil, of ambitious new startups betting specifically on AI that can improve or research things with less human involvement. Via This Week in Startups.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

North Korea launched a short range ballistic missile toward the sea this week, days before the United States and South Korea begin annual joint military drills that Pyongyang views as an invasion rehearsal. It's a reminder that tensions on the peninsula remain a live wire even while global attention stays fixed on Ukraine and the Middle East. Via CSIS Project on Nuclear Issues.

Security researchers uncovered an unusually blatant backdoor in consumer routers sold under several different brand names, with the firmware contacting cloud servers in China for instructions and enabling remote control. The finding adds to a growing pile of supply chain security concerns tied to low cost networking hardware, and is likely to feed straight into wider trade and technology policy debates over vetting hardware from Chinese suppliers. Via This Week in Startups.

And Ukraine is critically short of air defences, according to a fresh analysis from Radio Free Europe, after the country failed to intercept a single Russian missile during a large scale overnight attack this week. The piece raises pointed questions over the state of Kyiv's Patriot interceptor stockpiles just as Moscow escalates ballistic missile strikes on the capital and other regions, a reminder that the war's trajectory still hinges heavily on the pace and reliability of Western resupply. Via CSIS Project on Nuclear Issues.

Now, over to community, events and marketing.

One idea worth stealing: The Do Lectures is running a November workshop on growing a business in what it calls the zero click world, built on the observation that social platforms increasingly bury posts that include an outbound link. Its organisers point out that email open rates have barely moved in two decades, sitting at around thirty percent in 2005 and thirty four percent in 2024, making a direct, owned list one of the few durable channels left. The practical takeaway: treat your email list as the asset you actually own, not the platform you happen to be posting on this year. Via the Do Lectures.

A second one worth stealing, on community building: Community Inc's Kaustubh Katdare argues that most communities trying to grow hide too much value behind a login, giving prospective members no way to see what they'd be joining before they commit. His suggested fix is to build visible windows into the community, public snippets of the value inside, so people can see it and start to trust it before anyone ever asks them to sign up. Via Community Inc.

Now, one interesting thing.

In 1973 the racehorse Secretariat ran the Belmont Stakes so fast that no horse has matched his time since, despite fifty years and billions of dollars poured into breeding faster horses. Works in Progress argues this isn't bad luck but a paradox: breeders selecting so hard for speed have narrowed the horse's genetic diversity so much that the raw material for further improvement, or even for staying robust, has quietly been bred away. It's a tidy illustration of how optimising too hard for one trait can use up the very variation you need to keep improving at all. Via Works in Progress.
