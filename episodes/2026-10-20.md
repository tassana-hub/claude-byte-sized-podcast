Good morning, and welcome to Byte-sized for Tuesday, the twentieth of October. Six sections today, and we begin, as ever, in Japan and the Asia Pacific.

Section one, Japan and Asia Pacific.

First, Japan is going to Mars, or at least to its moons. The Japanese space agency, JAXA, is set to launch its Martian Moons eXploration mission around now, aiming to reach Phobos, collect a sample from the surface, and bring it back to Earth by twenty thirty one. The mission leans on American, French and German technology for parts of the build, which makes it a quiet showcase for Tokyo's role as a trusted partner in the deep space race. It also tests whether Phobos could serve as a low gravity staging post for future journeys to Mars. Via International Intrigue.

Second, a number that says a lot about the media business. For the first time since the Japan Press Research Institute began its survey in two thousand and eight, fewer than half of people in Japan, forty nine point eight per cent, now pay for a monthly newspaper subscription. For anyone building or investing in media, content or subscription products in Japan, the traditional habit is clearly weakening. Via International Intrigue.

Third, a follow-up on the Asian chip story we covered yesterday. China's memory maker CXMT now expects a nine month net profit of around nineteen point seven billion US dollars, against a small loss a year earlier, with revenue jumping from roughly four and a half billion dollars to nearly thirty eight billion. The company credits AI driven shortages of memory chips and expanded capacity. Two cautions: the claim that it has fifth generation memory in mass production has not been independently verified, and memory booms have a habit of ending when new capacity floods the market. Still, it explains why Japanese and Korean equipment makers are nervous. Via T L D R.

Now, over to AI and tech.

Section two, AI and tech.

First, Microsoft reportedly plans to pay Anthropic more than two billion dollars a year to use Claude models inside its Copilot assistant. The reason appears to be performance pressure from customers on tasks such as financial automation and presentations. Microsoft is also running an internal router that sends some workloads to OpenAI models, so this looks less like a switch and more like a portfolio approach to suppliers. Via T L D R.

Second, Anthropic has announced a Public Benefit Fund, a company owned entity that would receive five per cent of its equity if shareholders agree, with more added as the company's value climbs. The fund could support nonprofits or make direct payments to groups affected by AI, such as displaced workers, with no money going to lobbying or politics. Together with founder and employee pledges, the company says about a fifth of its equity is now set aside for public benefit. The Rundown notes that until money actually reaches people, it will be hard to tell this apart from good public relations. Via The Rundown AI.

Third, the same company has published research on its own workforce, and the findings are striking. By August, hours worked by employees' AI agents outnumbered the hours of the humans by six to one. Junior staff fell from twenty seven per cent of technical hires to just nine per cent, and engineers are shipping about five times as many code changes as six months ago. Agents sit idle roughly eighty per cent of the time waiting for a person to respond, so human attention has become the bottleneck. Via The Rundown AI.

Fourth, in hardware, Intel and Qualcomm are reportedly bidding up to ten billion dollars for the chip start-up Tenstorrent. Its chips cost around a thousand dollars and run a compact number format natively, where GPUs with native support list near forty thousand. Those cost figures come from vendors and are unverified, and no deal has closed, but it shows how hard the big chip makers are chasing alternatives to Nvidia. Via T L D R.

Now, over to start-ups and venture.

Section three, start-ups and venture.

First, the payments firm Ramp has raised one point eight five billion dollars at a sixty billion dollar valuation, up from forty four billion only four months ago. Annualised revenue has passed two billion dollars, and it is moving beyond expense management into payments, accounting, procurement and AI infrastructure, including cards designed for AI agents. Via T L D R.

Second, agents buying things is becoming real. Stripe's president says agent driven commerce on its platform went vertical in September, helped by agents that can now use ordinary websites without special integrations. American Express has promised to cover cardholder losses when a registered agent makes a purchase error, though the fine print matters, and Mercury now lets agents prepare payments, with a human approving the final transfer. Liability and authorisation are the open questions to watch. Via T L D R.

Third, Europe. Nvidia's Jensen Huang has been touring Paris and London, teasing around two billion dollars in new European investments, including French firms Doctolib and Pigment and five British start-ups. Nvidia says it has put eight billion dollars into European start-ups in the past year. Sifted also reports that Accel is set to lead a sixty million dollar round for Revolution Labs, founded by the former DeepMind researcher Nando de Freitas. Via Sifted.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

Section four, policy and geopolitics.

First, a possible summit with Asia at its centre. International Intrigue reports that Vladimir Putin has proposed a meeting with Donald Trump and Xi Jinping on the sidelines of the APEC summit in Shenzhen, on the eighteenth and nineteenth of November, to discuss ending the war in Ukraine, with Ukraine not invited. The newsletter's reading is that Xi may enjoy the role of mediator but is also comfortable with a distracted America. Meanwhile, Trump quietly exempted Russia from new sanctions late on Friday, saying it was to protect peace talks. Worth knowing before any conversation about Asia Pacific diplomacy. Via International Intrigue.

Second, the United Kingdom is drawing up plans for mandatory safety reporting by frontier AI companies, as the government seeks more oversight of powerful models. Alongside that, Sifted looks at Europe's enthusiasm for open weight models as a route to sovereignty, and the risk that safety guardrails can be stripped out. Supporters argue openness is an accountability guarantee, since anyone can inspect and test the models. Via Sifted.

Third, a practical point for founders: Sifted asks why governments are so hard to sell to. Procurement can leave fast-moving start-ups waiting years for contracts, and Britain's Sovereign AI Fund is trying to make the state an easier customer. Via Sifted.

Now, over to community, events and marketing.

Section five, community, events and marketing. One idea worth stealing.

The community platform company Bevy's C M X network is running a circuit tour of small in-person meetups, and nearly one hundred community professionals have joined across its first six stops this autumn. The formats are modest and easy to copy: a casual morning of coffee and a neighbourhood walk in Los Angeles, a brewery evening with shuffleboard in Boston, and pizza and practical conversation in Nashville. The lesson is to launch a chapter city by city with a relaxed, low cost format rather than a big conference. Also, nominations for its community awards close on the twentieth of November. Via C M X Newsletter.

Finally, section six, one interesting thing.

In Why Is This Interesting's Monday media diet, the hotelier Marc Blazer shares an idea from the philosopher Bernard Suits. Play, in Suits' words, is voluntarily taking on unnecessary obstacles in order to enjoy overcoming them. Blazer's point is that we spend so much effort removing friction from life that we forget many of our greatest joys come from it, which is why he restores old cars rather than buying a new one. Via Why Is This Interesting.
