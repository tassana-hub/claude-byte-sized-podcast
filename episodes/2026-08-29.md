JAPAN AND ASIA PACIFIC

We start with Japan's prime minister, Sanae Takaichi, who six months after leading her Liberal Democratic Party to a two thirds majority in the Lower House is still riding what analysts are calling an approval shield. Her public support tracks the polling patterns of Japan's most resilient postwar leaders, which has been enough to keep would-be rivals inside her own party quiet. That shield is conditional, though: as the autumn parliamentary session opens, she faces a cabinet reshuffle and has to prove her domestic agenda is actually landing with voters, all while keeping Japan's commitments abroad on track. Via CSIS.

Next, a large vote of confidence in Japan's chip industry. Memory makers Kioxia and Sandisk are planning to invest more than thirty one billion dollars in Japan through 2032, including eleven point three billion dollars for a new plant in Kitakami. It is one of the clearest signs yet that, even as Washington and Beijing fight over chip export rules and Washington now weighs tariffs of its own, Japan is positioning itself as a safe, well capitalised base for the memory hardware that AI data centres the world over are racing to build. Via TLDR.

And over in Southeast Asia, a CSIS podcast this week dug into Myanmar's junta government, its recent diplomatic outreach to ASEAN, and what its neighbours can realistically expect next. The takeaway is that the junta is playing a longer diplomatic game than outsiders often give it credit for, using engagement with the regional bloc to slowly normalise its position. Via CSIS.

Now, over to AI and tech.

The story dominating every newsletter in my inbox this week: Nvidia has reportedly agreed to buy Hugging Face, the open source hub where developers share and test AI models, for twelve point nine billion dollars, though some reports suggest the deal isn't fully signed yet. Buying Hugging Face would give Nvidia a foothold in models and developer tools to go with its dominance in chips. In the same breath, Amazon Web Services is tripling its already enormous Nvidia order, adding two million more chips, including the newest Blackwell Ultra and Rubin systems, after demand blew past its earlier commitment of a million GPUs. Via TLDR and Sifted.

Anthropic, meanwhile, is doubling down on compute and on its independence. The company has struck a six year, forty five billion dollar deal with Nscale, a UK based AI infrastructure specialist, to lease four hundred and sixty megawatts of capacity from a new West Virginia campus running Nvidia's next generation Vera Rubin chips, due online in late 2027. That came in the same week a federal judge blocked the Pentagon's attempt to blacklist Anthropic after the company refused to let the military use Claude without restrictions, a reminder that being the AI lab with the firmest safety lines can carry real commercial cost, but also real legal protection. Via TLDR and Sifted.

Not every AI story is about who is spending the most, though. A growing number of American startups are quietly switching away from OpenAI and Anthropic toward Chinese models to save money, and the price gap explains why: DeepSeek charges around fourteen cents per million tokens against OpenAI's five dollars. Chinese models are reportedly now dominating traffic on OpenRouter, the AI routing marketplace, even as US lawmakers investigate the security and data privacy risks of running so much American software on Chinese made models. Via TLDR.

And Anthropic isn't just chasing software any more. It has opened a research preview of what it calls the Model Hardware Standard, a companion to its now familiar Model Context Protocol, designed to let AI agents operate physical equipment like microscopes and robotic arms using plain language descriptions instead of weeks of custom code. In one demonstration, Claude taught itself to align a laboratory laser through trial and error, then turned what it learned into a script that did the job in a single pass. Equipment makers Tecan and Qiagen, along with AWS, Hugging Face and Raspberry Pi, are already on board. Via The Rundown.

Now, over to startups and venture.

New data from Sifted shows just how aggressively American money is chasing European startups this year. Between January and the end of August, seven hundred and thirty eight US headquartered investors wrote cheques into European companies, with familiar names like Y Combinator, Sequoia, Andreessen Horowitz and General Catalyst among the most active. Most of that money, sixty six percent, is going into early stage deals, and it's overwhelmingly chasing AI, deep tech and fintech. Sifted also flagged a striking concentration problem closer to home: just three companies, the defence tech firms Helsing and Quantum Systems and the robotics scaleup Neura Robotics, accounted for nearly forty four percent of all the money raised by German startups so far this year. Via Sifted.

Elsewhere, the Advent and Stripe consortium has walked away from its pursuit of PayPal, after reportedly offering more than fifty billion dollars for one of online payment's oldest names. It's a rare example of a mega deal being allowed to quietly die rather than dragging on for months. Via TLDR.

And a sharper look at what AI is actually doing inside engineering teams: The Pragmatic Engineer newsletter this week pushed back on a viral OpenAI case study claiming Codex helped Asana clear five years of testing migration work in two weeks. The real story, confirmed directly with Asana's engineering leadership, is more mundane but still striking: a low priority migration that would have dragged on for years got done in a couple of weeks once AI tools were pointed at it, echoing similar AI assisted migrations at Airbnb and Uber that shrank projects from years to weeks. The lesson for founders isn't the eye catching dollar figure, it's that AI is quietly making previously unthinkable technical debt projects worth doing. Via Pragmatic Engineer.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

The strangest story of the week comes from the Spanish enclave of Ceuta, where more than seventy thousand people crossed from Morocco in a single day at the end of July after a viral, and possibly coordinated, message claimed that swimming across the border meant migrants could no longer be instantly returned. Most went back, but around five thousand remain, straining the city of eighty four thousand and reigniting a decades old dispute over who Ceuta actually belongs to. Spanish authorities suspect Moroccan involvement, given Rabat's long history of using mass border crossings as leverage in territorial disputes, though a genuine viral panic that Moroccan officials simply chose not to stop remains the more likely explanation. Via Uncharted Territories.

Migration is also the theme a continent over. A CSIS podcast this week examined recent attacks on migrants in South Africa, which have reignited one of the continent's most sensitive debates just as the African Union and the African Continental Free Trade Area push for deeper labour mobility and regional integration. The gap between the economic case for freer movement of people and the political reality on the ground remains as wide as ever. Via CSIS.

And back in Washington, the Trump administration is reportedly weighing sweeping new semiconductor tariffs that could extend all the way to data centre servers and other chip powered goods, at the exact moment American tech companies are already struggling with high end chip shortages. The irony isn't lost on anyone: tariffs meant to protect America's AI ambitions could end up undercutting them instead. Via This Week in Startups.

Now, over to community, events and marketing.

One idea worth stealing this week: Marketing Brew's look at how professional pickleball has quietly turned itself into a serious sponsorship business, attracting the kind of brand deals that used to go only to established sports. The same issue noted Zoom leaning on a similar playbook, attaching itself to a cultural moment, in that case Apple's Mac Mini relaunch, to get its own product in front of an audience it wouldn't otherwise reach. The lesson for anyone building a community or a brand around a growing niche is that credibility and sponsorship dollars follow audience size, not sport size, and pickleball's organisers built the fan base first and let the sponsorship pitch write itself. Via Marketing Brew.

Now, over to one interesting thing.

Works in Progress has a sharp essay this week arguing that American public transit is bad largely because of what the author calls scope creep. Buses and trains are asked to cut emissions, create union jobs, beautify neighbourhoods and reflect endless rounds of community input, all on top of their actual job of moving people, and every one of those extra goals costs money that could otherwise buy more frequent service. The examples are startling: Miami Dade's electric buses break down roughly every six hundred miles, seven times more often than diesel ones, so that on any given day only six of the agency's seventy five electric buses are actually running, while Boston's Green Line extension added a three kilometre bike path, after just thirteen people asked for it at a community meeting, at a cost of twenty million dollars. The core argument is simple: every dollar spent satisfying a side goal is a dollar not spent making transit fast and frequent enough that people who could drive choose not to. Via Works in Progress.
