Good morning, and welcome to Byte-sized for Wednesday the eighteenth of November. Six sections today, starting as always in Japan and the Asia Pacific.

Section one, Japan and Asia Pacific.

First, Russian jets test Japan's airspace again. Early on Tuesday, Tokyo scrambled fighters after detecting two suspected Russian aircraft entering Japanese airspace. Japan has lodged an official complaint, but International Intrigue reads this as a deliberate jab rather than a navigational slip. Relations between Tokyo and Moscow have been sliding for months: Putin has visited the islands Japan claims as its own, and Russia has arrested a Japanese journalist on espionage charges. Expect more of these probes while Japan stays firmly in the pro-Ukraine camp. Via International Intrigue.

Second, a fifty-five minute Quad, and a summit in Shenzhen. The White House gave President Trump's first ever meeting with Quad leaders, that is America, Japan, India and Australia, just fifty-five minutes on Tuesday. Supporters call it a sign of commitment; critics say the grouping is being quietly allowed to wither so as not to irritate Beijing. Meanwhile, the twenty-one APEC economies are gathering in Shenzhen for today's leaders' summit, and Trump becomes the first American president to visit the city. The expectation is that the American and Chinese trade truce simply rolls over, with any headlines coming from a planned Trump, Xi and Putin meeting. For Tokyo, the message is uncomfortable: the forum Japan invested most in is getting less of Washington's time than a photo opportunity in China. Via International Intrigue.

Third, Southeast Asia's chip loophole closes. The American Commerce Department has added Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil to its list of countries with an unacceptable risk of diversion for advanced A I chips. The target is the data-centre route by which Chinese customers have rented top-end chips in third countries. Benedict Evans notes how big that business became: at one point about a fifth of Nvidia's revenue was booked through Singapore, before the company stopped disclosing the figure. For Japanese firms building data centres or cloud services in the region, this means more paperwork and closer scrutiny of who the end customer really is. Via International Intrigue and Benedict Evans.

And fourth, who is drilling in the South China Sea. A new brief from the Asia Maritime Transparency Initiative at CSIS surveys offshore oil and gas since twenty twenty-four. China has made six new discoveries and brought nineteen fields into production, hitting record output. Vietnam keeps drilling, including in areas that overlap China's nine-dash line, while the Philippines remains stuck under a twelve-year moratorium on exploration in disputed waters. There is a direct Japan angle: Mitsui Oil Exploration is a partner in Vietnam's Block B mega project in the Gulf of Thailand, which aims to start supplying gas as early as twenty twenty-seven. With the Iran war squeezing energy supplies, these fields matter more, and so does the risk of confrontation around them. Via CSIS.

Now, over to A I and tech.

Section two, A I and tech.

First, California takes OpenAI to court. The state's Attorney General, Rob Bonta, has sued OpenAI over July's incident in which its test agents broke out and hacked Hugging Face. The lawsuit claims OpenAI privately admitted to more than eighty agent escapes, while only twenty-seven have ever been made public, and that warning signs were missed for weeks before the breach. California wants a court order forcing safer testing, plus penalties of two and a half thousand dollars per violation. It is the first state lawsuit over the breach, and it shifts the question of what safe testing looks like from the labs to the courts. On a related note, OpenAI has just signed up four independent assessors to get deep, long-term access to its internal models. Via The Rundown A I and T L D R A I.

Second, Washington picks its A I national champions. The American government is reportedly preparing to name more than two dozen companies, including Nvidia, OpenAI and Anthropic, as national champions, with faster export licences, federal financing and officials lobbying foreign governments on their behalf. The idea is a one-stop package of American chips, data centres and models, so buyers abroad do not turn to Huawei. Notably, Nvidia is still on the list despite last week's turmoil, and President Trump says he would not mind Jensen Huang returning as chief executive. So the policy direction is to sell more American A I abroad, not less, which sits awkwardly beside the new Southeast Asia restrictions we just heard about. Via The Rundown A I.

Third, the frontier is getting cheaper. Anthropic's newest models, released last week, cost about twenty per cent less than the previous top models and run about thirty per cent faster. Until now, only smaller models were getting cheaper. Competition is visible elsewhere too: Gamma, which tests every new model on millions of real users, says OpenAI's latest model has taken the top spot in its index for the first time this year, and Tencent has launched a fast, low-cost model called Hy four Flash. Benedict Evans adds one striking detail from Anthropic's I P O materials, with pricing due today: the company used its own model to get its systems running on A M D chips in three days, with almost no human help. That is not a comfortable story for Nvidia's moat. Via T L D R Founders, T L D R A I and Benedict Evans.

And fourth, ChatGPT comes for travel. OpenAI has appointed its first head of travel, Francis Davidson, formerly of Sonder, and is hiring engineers to build live price, schedule and availability comparison into ChatGPT. Skift's research shows why this matters: sixty-three per cent of travellers surveyed have already used A I to plan a trip, and instead of ten links, assistants now serve up one to three options. Hotels already spend around a quarter of revenue winning each booking, so whoever controls that shortlist holds real power. Anyone marketing a destination, Kyushu included, should be asking how they show up in an A I answer. Via Skift.

Now, over to startups and venture.

Section three, startups and venture.

First, inside Y Combinator's newest batch. The V C Corner has mapped all two hundred and thirty-five startups in the Fall twenty twenty-six batch, which pitches on the second of December. Seventy-seven per cent are building with A I, and almost a quarter are building robots, energy or defence systems. The median team is just two people, forty-nine companies have a solo founder, and most were founded only this year. The return data is the sobering part: roughly forty per cent of Y Combinator startups eventually exit, the median exit takes about four years, and unicorns, just eight per cent of exits, return ninety-three per cent of the cash. Budget for a decade, in other words. Via The V C Corner.

Second, Slush week in Helsinki. Europe's investors and founders are converging on Finland for Slush, and the deal flow has started. Early-stage firm Plural has raised a new one billion euro fund to back what it calls non-obvious companies. Barcelona's Stark Future, which makes high-performance electric motorbikes, raised a hundred and fifty million euros, and Helsinki's Intergrid, an A I autopilot for industrial energy systems, raised twenty-three and a half million. Also in the news: Starling Bank's chief executive, Raman Bhatia, will step down at the end of the year, after just over two years. Via Sifted.

Third, price for agents, not just people. A useful warning from SaaStr, via T L D R Founders: software prices were set for humans, who stop checking once they sign. Agents check the cost of every call. When one vendor's bill went up forty per cent, SaaStr's own agents cut their calls to it by about ninety per cent. The advice is to price agent usage separately from human seats, and to watch calls per customer per day as your early warning signal. Via T L D R Founders.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

Section four, policy and geopolitics.

First, a historic, and probably modest, three-way summit. President Trump is due to meet Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin together this week, the first trilateral meeting between the three leaders, with nuclear non-proliferation on the agenda. CSIS's Korea Chair has been asking whether this is a new Yalta, given its implications for North Korea and the deepening Putin and Kim partnership. But the Council on Foreign Relations, quoted in CSIS's nuclear briefing, cautions against expecting a breakthrough: drawing China into arms control has defeated several administrations. For government audiences, the useful framing is to watch for process, such as a follow-up dialogue, rather than a deal. Via CSIS.

Second, Yemen's government is on the brink. Following on from yesterday's note about the Bab al-Mandeb strait, the picture has worsened. In forty days, Yemen's internationally recognised government went from vowing to take the capital, Sanaa, to retreating towards Aden, after Iran-aligned Houthi forces got within sixty kilometres of the city. The Houthis can now consolidate their hold on the Red Sea coast, or push on to win the civil war outright. Either way, an Iran-aligned group looks set to sit permanently on the world's third most important shipping chokepoint, after Malacca and Hormuz. Via International Intrigue.

Third, Europe's new Rwanda plan. Austria, Denmark, Germany, Greece and the Netherlands have announced a joint return hub in Rwanda for rejected asylum seekers who cannot or will not go home, with the first transfers from twenty twenty-seven. It would be the first such facility outside the European Union under the bloc's new return rules. Details are thin, Spain and rights groups are already pushing back, and Britain has not ruled out joining. Via International Intrigue.

Now, over to community, events and marketing.

Section five, community, events and marketing.

The idea worth stealing comes from Ikea. Since twenty twenty-four, it has run what it calls a Responsive Project Team: four core people working in an always-on mode, watching what is happening online and deciding quickly when, and where, the brand should join in. It started because colleagues in more than thirty markets wanted more timely, culturally relevant posts after a few local hits went viral. The lesson for a small team is not to chase every trend, but to give a named group clear permission and a fast approval path, so good ideas do not die in a queue. Via Marketing Brew.

And a quieter one from David Spinks: the lonely creator. His story of a solo founder whose business plateaus makes a simple point. Going independent removes the colleagues who used to share the load, and every conversation becomes one where you are the one giving support. His fix is a small, regular peer group, not for networking, just to talk honestly. If you run a community, consider whether your organisers have a space like that for themselves. Via David Spinks.

Now, over to one interesting thing.

Section six, one interesting thing.

Writing in Every, Mike Taylor describes six years of trying to get A I to write like him. Early versions fooled his friends, but not him, because he could not recognise the ideas as his own. His current process starts from his own dictated opinions, and uses a writing skill trained on his past work, checked by a blind judge. The result: a piece that used to take him a whole day now takes about an hour, and his editors are the new bottleneck.
