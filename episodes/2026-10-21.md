Good morning, and welcome to Byte-sized for Wednesday, the twenty first of October. Six sections today, and we begin, as ever, in Japan and the Asia Pacific.

Section one, Japan and Asia Pacific.

First, a warning from the supply chain. International Intrigue reports that Japan has, reportedly, only months, not years, before it runs out of yttrium, a rare earth found in jet engines, semiconductors, electric vehicles and LED lighting. China imposed export controls on the mineral last year, after Prime Minister Takaichi suggested Japan might respond in any war over Taiwan, and Tokyo is now lobbying its American allies for help. For any manufacturer with Japanese exposure, this is a concrete example of geopolitics landing on a bill of materials. Via International Intrigue.

Second, Accenture has hired J P Morgan to explore the sale of its Greater China consulting business, in a deal that could be worth up to one point five billion dollars. The review follows unsolicited interest, and comes as Beijing restricts foreign consultants from advising state owned enterprises. Accenture says it is putting its capital and attention into artificial intelligence globally. Another sign that foreign advisory firms are rethinking how much of Asia's future runs through China. Via T L D R.

Third, some good news on trade in the region. Switzerland's president has been in Vietnam, where Vietnam and the European Free Trade Association, which covers Norway, Switzerland, Iceland and Liechtenstein, have finally closed a trade deal fourteen years in the making. Vietnam gains European engineering and investment, and the European group gains a foothold in South East Asia's manufacturing hub, as both sides look to diversify away from China and shelter from tariff wars. Via International Intrigue.

Fourth, a number from Geopolitical Monitor worth remembering: China now fields one hundred and four modern destroyers and frigates, against fifty two for Japan's maritime self defence force. The piece traces how that balance has shifted since twenty twelve, and sits behind the publication's paywall, so treat this as the headline only. Via Geopolitical Monitor.

Now, over to AI and tech.

Section two, AI and tech.

First, Anthropic's co-founder Jack Clark told economists at the I M F and World Bank annual meetings that the singularity, the point where AI speeds up discovery faster than humans can follow, has now begun. He pointed to scores on the Humanity's Last Exam benchmark rising from eight per cent to nearly sixty eight per cent since last year, and warned of very disruptive effects on jobs. It is a striking thing to say to the people who run the global economy, though it comes from someone building the technology. Via The Rundown AI.

Second, a security story with a sharp lesson. The firm NorForge says a Chinese speaking hacker used AI agents to do nearly all the work of breaking into the websites of more than one hundred companies, with victims reportedly including Cummins, Deere and SharkNinja. The agents were pointed at over twelve hundred online stores, at an estimated cost of around fifty dollars per successful break in. Offence is getting cheaper faster than defence. Via The Rundown AI.

Third, speed is becoming the next battleground. Inception Labs has launched Mercury 3, a diffusion language model that drafts many words at once rather than one at a time. By its own numbers it matches or beats rivals such as Claude Haiku five point five at four to eight times the speed, over a thousand tokens a second. It will not replace frontier models for hard reasoning, but for live voice and tutoring apps, where lag shows, it is a serious option. Via The Rundown AI.

Fourth, power is the constraint. Microsoft has reportedly terminated a data centre agreement with Nscale at a planned British site, because electricity supply delays could stretch into the twenty thirties. Nscale says the project continues. Separately, it is reportedly in talks with Anthropic for another forty five billion dollar compute deal, only weeks after the last one. Via T L D R.

Now, over to start-ups and venture.

Section three, start-ups and venture.

First, AI financial advice is attracting founders. Sifted reports that the Stockholm start-up Matters has raised a pre seed round to offer AI powered savings advice for a flat monthly fee. Sifted's reporter notes that investors are cautiously optimistic, but worry that big neobanks, or frontier models themselves, could eat the category's lunch. Via Sifted.

Second, European rounds. The French AI accounting firm Dougs raised fifty million euros, led by Expedition Growth Capital, and the Munich based autonomous finance start-up Causa Prima raised twenty million dollars in a seed round led by Base Ten Partners. Via Sifted.

Third, a reminder of operational risk. Revolut suffered a widespread outage, and the Paris headquarters of the fintech Qonto was trashed and set alight during weekend riots. Neither is a funding story, but both matter to anyone relying on European fintech infrastructure. Via Sifted.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

Section four, policy and geopolitics.

First, energy diplomacy. Emmanuel Macron is touring Kuwait, the United Arab Emirates, Bahrain and Qatar seeking fuel commitments, and has claimed pledges equal to roughly ten per cent of French diesel consumption. Meanwhile Secretary of State Rubio is leading two hundred American business leaders to Uzbekistan, and then to Mumbai and Jamnagar in India, the site of the world's largest single site refinery. Everyone is chasing the same barrels, and Central Asia and India are suddenly in play. Via International Intrigue.

Second, the American Department of Homeland Security has formally banned DeepSeek from civilian agency systems, giving departments sixty days to request exceptions, and extending the ban to third party tools built on its models. Enterprise teams with government customers should check what sits underneath their tools. Via The Rundown AI.

Third, a fiscal spat. The I M F now projects American debt at one hundred and forty five per cent of G D P by twenty thirty one, and the Treasury is pushing back. Via Geopolitical Monitor.

Now, over to community, events and marketing.

Section five, community, events and marketing. One idea worth stealing.

Skift's meetings newsletter has a neat negotiation tip for event organisers. American hotels expect a strong end to this year, but growth forecasts for twenty twenty seven are far weaker, because next summer will be measured against this summer's World Cup crowds. Revenue managers are judged year on year, so a group that fills soft summer nights helps their numbers. The advice is to ask for more than you would have last year, such as a better rate, a lower food and beverage minimum, or friendlier attrition terms. Via Skift.

Finally, section six, one interesting thing.

Tomas Pueyo, in Uncharted Territories, argues that intelligence is mostly search. Most of our thoughts are existing ideas combined in a new way, and until now search was expensive. Now that language models make it nearly free, he says, your edge is the query: noticing a problem nobody has searched for, combining fields that rarely meet, or working in areas that are unmeasured or unrecorded, such as community. Via Uncharted Territories.
