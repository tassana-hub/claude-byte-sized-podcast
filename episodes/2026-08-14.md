# Byte-sized — Thursday, 14 August

Good morning. It's Friday the fourteenth of August, and this is Byte-sized, your daily digest of newsletters, in six parts. Let's get into it.

Japan and Asia-Pacific first.

Tokyo has lodged a formal protest after Vladimir Putin paid his first-ever visit to the Kuril Islands, the chain seized by the Soviets after the Second World War but still partly claimed by Japan. Russian state media confirmed Putin visited Iturup, known in Japan as Etorofu, this week. The timing is pointed: Japan's prime minister, Takaichi, recently released a new defence white paper warning about growing cooperation between Russia, China and North Korea, so a high-profile trip to the fortified islands reads as a deliberate jab at Tokyo. It also underlines a point security commentators keep making, that Japan's defence posture still rests on an uneasy contradiction: a nation devoutly opposed to nuclear weapons that continues to shelter under America's nuclear umbrella. Via CSIS and International Intrigue.

Next, Japan has released the second version of its national Artificial Intelligence Basic Plan, aiming to speed up the rollout of smart technology to tackle national challenges while tightening up regulation and cybersecurity safeguards. It lands as part of a much bigger wave of AI policy-making breaking out across the region. Australia has stood up a new Office of AI and promised to protect creative works from being scraped for training data. China has published action plans on AI cooperation and on AI ethics governance. South Korea is pushing "physical AI" strategies for robotics, ports and manufacturing. Vietnam, Malaysia, Thailand, Singapore and Pakistan have all published their own frameworks in the past month. The upshot: Indo-Pacific governments are no longer waiting on Washington or Brussels to set the rules for AI, they're writing their own. Via CSIS's Pac-Tech Pulse newsletter.

And in Southeast Asia, CSIS's Greg and Elina sat down with journalist Shibani Mahtani to unpack the evolving scam centre ecosystem in Cambodia, which has grown from a localised criminal enterprise into a regional problem with implications well beyond its borders. The conversation is a reminder that the region's digital economy has a dark underside that governments are still struggling to contain. Via CSIS Southeast Asia Radio.

Now, over to AI and tech.

SpaceX's AI division has released Grok four point six, a model tuned for long-running agents, coding and knowledge work. It now matches OpenAI's GPT five point six Sol on independent benchmarks while undercutting it on price by around sixty percent, and it's already overtaken Kimi K3 for third place on the Artificial Analysis leaderboard. It's the latest sign that frontier AI pricing is becoming as competitive a battleground as raw capability. Via TLDR and This Week in Startups.

Anthropic is reportedly in early talks to acquire the Israeli startup Decart for around six billion dollars, a deal that would bring Decart's chip optimisation technology in-house. If it goes ahead, it would be one of Anthropic's largest acquisitions to date and points to how seriously the big AI labs are taking the cost of compute. Separately, Anthropic confirmed it will begin embedding invisible watermarks in text and images generated by Claude, bringing it in line with the European Union's AI Act code of practice. The text watermark survives copying and light editing, though the company admits detection can fail on short or heavily rewritten snippets. Via TLDR Design and CTech.

And Databricks has raised five billion dollars at a valuation of one hundred and ninety billion dollars, cementing its place as one of the most valuable private companies in AI infrastructure. The raise lands the same week Databricks confirmed it's acquiring Electric, a startup whose lightweight, in-browser version of Postgres lets AI agents keep fast local databases that sync back to a central source of truth. Together the moves show Databricks racing to own the plumbing that agentic AI will run on. Via TLDR IT.

Now, over to startups and venture.

Sifted reports that Index Ventures is in talks to lead a five hundred million dollar round for a new AI lab founded by a former DeepMind researcher, focused on so-called world models, AI systems designed to simulate and reason about physical environments rather than just text. It would be one of the largest seed-stage AI bets of the year if it closes. Via Sifted.

OpenAI-backed Thrive Holdings, which buys traditional businesses and rebuilds them around AI, has raised two billion dollars at a twelve billion dollar valuation from SoftBank, D1 Capital Partners and Altimeter. It's a striking bet that the fastest way to monetise AI isn't building new software companies, but retrofitting old ones. Via This Week in Startups.

And in Europe, two rounds worth watching: the vibe-coding platform Lovable closed a four hundred million dollar Series C backed by the European Union's own Scaleup Fund, while Portuguese drone maker Uforce is said to be targeting a valuation north of four billion dollars in its next raise. Between them they capture the twin themes of the moment, AI-native software and defence-adjacent hardware, both pulling in serious European capital. Via Sifted.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

BlackRock, the world's largest asset manager, published its latest geopolitical risk dashboard this week, and two calls stand out. First, on trade: the dashboard flags the European Union's dispute with China over industrial overcapacity as the year's likely flashpoint. China's manufacturing surplus has now reached roughly two percent of global GDP, its trade surplus with the EU has overtaken its surplus with the United States for the first time in three decades, and Beijing has been retaliating selectively against European exports, from French cognac to Spanish pork, whenever Brussels moves on electric vehicle tariffs. With Washington and Brussels not exactly aligned right now, Europe may be facing this one largely alone. Second, on AI: BlackRock doesn't expect meaningful US-China cooperation on frontier AI any time soon, but notes a narrow opening has appeared. May's Trump-Xi summit produced a new bilateral dialogue on AI "best practices," with a first working round due next month, ahead of a planned reciprocal Xi visit to the US. The goal isn't friendship, it's avoiding a repeat of Cold War-style nuclear near misses, this time with AI systems instead of warheads. Via International Intrigue.

Separately, Zhu Rongji, China's former premier and the hard-charging technocrat who drove the country's entry into the World Trade Organization, has died at ninety-seven. His legacy splits sharply depending on who you ask: the state-firm workers who lost jobs under his efficiency drives, the hundreds of millions who rode China's post-WTO boom into the middle class, and the workers abroad who lost out to the China shock that followed. It's a reminder of how consequential, and how contested, that period of Chinese economic opening still is. Via International Intrigue.

And a live example of why supply chains keep showing up on the geopolitical risk radar: a cyberattack this week disrupted eight warehouses run by the logistics giant Ceva, delaying or cancelling orders for retailers across Europe, including hardware customers of the online retailer Bol and gaming customers of Valve. Attackers reportedly accessed order-processing systems containing names, addresses and purchase details. It's a small story on its own, but it's the kind of quietly cascading disruption that boardrooms increasingly have to plan around, alongside tariffs and export controls, when they think about resilience. Via TLDR.

Now, over to community, events and marketing. One idea worth stealing from each.

From the Community Inc newsletter: most community-building advice assumes you already own a platform, a forum, a Discord, an events calendar. Writer Shyaamal Tripathi makes the case for building trust in spaces you don't control instead, drawing on his experience growing communities inside other people's Discord servers, hackathons and open-source projects. The practical takeaway: show up consistently, add value without asking for credit, and let the trust you build outlast any single platform. Via Community Inc.

From SEO writer Zyppy Signal, on how Google actually judges content quality: buried in a leaked Google ranking document is a signal called "content effort," estimated by a large language model for every page it crawls. The point isn't how long something took to make, it's whether the finished page shows visible evidence of real work, original testing, first-hand opinions, unique photography, curated judgment, rather than being a repackaging of what's already on the internet. For anyone publishing anything, physical or digital, the one-line lesson is: make the effort visible, don't just make it. Via Zyppy Signal.

And now, one interesting thing to close on.

Thrift stores are having a moment again, and the internet has decided secondhand shopping is content. The Bureau of Labor Statistics published a report this year marvelling at how many millennials and Gen Z-ers now flock to thrift shops, and resale platform ThredUp reckons the worldwide secondhand clothing market will hit nearly four hundred billion dollars within four years. Writer Jason Boog traces the through-line back to a New York Sun article from nineteen-oh-two, describing the same cast of characters haunting a Brooklyn Salvation Army store: bargain hunters, canny resellers, and one gum-chewing teenager after a good pair of shoes for the dance. More than a century on, her spiritual heirs are the same age, filming "thrift with me" videos that have racked up millions of posts on TikTok. Some things, it turns out, really don't change. Via Why Is This Interesting.
