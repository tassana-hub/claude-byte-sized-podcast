Section one, Japan and Asia Pacific. Three items today, and the theme is how money, borders and geography shape the region.

First, a story with real ripples across Asia. International Intrigue reports that a wave of Chinese founders and tycoons are selling down their own companies at the same moment. The family behind the Haidilao hotpot chain sold around three hundred and fifty million dollars of shares, and the recruitment entrepreneur Peng Zhao sold about a hundred and ten million. The reason appears to be a deadline on the twenty second of October. Beijing has closed the loophole that let the ultra-rich defer tax through offshore trusts, and they now have until that date to declare and settle unpaid trust income going back to twenty twenty three, without penalty. The newsletter suggests China is also using automated data exchange with around a hundred countries to find wealth held abroad, and is tightening rules on value added tax and cross-border trading. Its reading is that the push is about plugging a fiscal hole left by the property slump, and funding a shift towards artificial intelligence and chips. For anyone doing business with Chinese founders or investors, expect some unusual share sales before late October. Via International Intrigue.

Second, a bit of context that anyone living in Japan will enjoy. The writer Tomas Pueyo, in Uncharted Territories, argues that much of Japanese history can be explained by its geography. Scattered plains divided by mountains made it hard for a central government to tax or move armies, which produced feudalism and the samurai, much as in medieval Europe. It also meant that Kyushu, closest to Korea and to foreign traders, kept producing the leaders who wanted to open up to the West. The piece then reads Japan's modern priorities the same way: keep the main islands united, guard the island chains and Korea, avoid being crushed by a continental superpower, and secure resources by sea. His conclusion is that today's Japan, allied with the United States, wary of China, and dependent on trade routes, is following a very old logic. Via Uncharted Territories.

And a quick regional roundup, again from International Intrigue. Malaysia has begun sending Myanmar nationals and Rohingya back to Myanmar, and Prime Minister Anwar Ibrahim has even invited the junta leader to help, a sharp reversal for a long time critic, apparently driven by domestic backlash. Meanwhile Bangkok is cleaning up after its worst flooding in fifteen years, and anger there has focused on a wealthy young man arrested for riding a jet ski through submerged streets. Via International Intrigue.

Now, over to artificial intelligence and technology.

Section two, AI and tech. Four stories, and they are mostly about money.

Anthropic has released Claude Sonnet five point five. According to TLDR, it is stronger at coding, long tasks and understanding images, runs more than thirty percent faster than Sonnet five, and although the price per token is unchanged, it uses fewer tokens, so the cost of a given task can fall by up to thirty percent. Every's early verdict is that it is a more capable partner than Sonnet five, provided you keep a hand on the wheel and know when to call in the larger Opus model. Via TLDR and Every.

The same week, a leaked prospectus for an Anthropic stock market listing has been picked apart. The company is reportedly aiming at a valuation of around two trillion dollars, against revenue of four point six billion last year and a net loss of forty two billion, which International Intrigue notes is largely down to accounting for financing instruments rather than cash burn. Annualised revenue is said to be approaching sixty five billion dollars this year. The document also says nearly a quarter of last year's revenue came from just two customers, and plans more than five hundred billion dollars of spending on computing and infrastructure. Via TLDR and International Intrigue.

In hardware, AMD has agreed to buy Fei-Fei Li's World Labs in an all-stock deal worth roughly eight point two billion dollars. Li will become AMD's chief scientist, and the aim is to bring spatial intelligence research inside a chip company. It is another sign that the chipmakers want to own more of the model layer rather than just sell to it. Via TLDR.

And one for engineering leaders. The Pragmatic Engineer reports that Shopify, only a year after saying it was very happy with React Native, is dropping it, and that the reason, unsurprisingly, is artificial intelligence. It is a useful reminder that tooling choices made for human developers are being reopened now that coding agents do so much of the typing. Via The Pragmatic Engineer.

Now, over to startups and venture capital.

Section three, startups and venture. A British story, a Nordic one, and a travel technology one.

Monzo is reportedly in early sale talks with Brazil's Nubank, at a valuation of between eight and ten billion pounds. Sifted says a sale would end hopes of Monzo becoming one of the biggest London listings in years, and it has reignited the familiar British argument about why the country cannot keep hold of its best technology companies. Monzo is also said to be weighing a fresh funding round above eight billion pounds to fund its expansion into mainland Europe. One adviser told Sifted that a deal would let shareholders realise value now rather than wait for an uncertain listing. Via Sifted.

Also from Sifted, a dispatch from Nordic Fintech Week in Copenhagen. Fintech has dropped from over a quarter of the Nordic top companies leaderboard to just fifteen percent, and everyone accepts it is harder than ever, particularly in small home markets. Yet the mood was upbeat, with the name on everyone's lips being FlatPay, the region's fastest growing fintech and Denmark's fastest ever unicorn. The lesson for founders is that the exceptions are still getting attention even in a slump. Via Sifted.

And PhocusWire's daily rounds up travel technology funding: the Booking Holdings backed Lola has relaunched with a membership model for AI travel, Dextr AI raised six point seven million dollars in seed funding, and HyperGuest secured twenty five million dollars. Small numbers by this week's standards, but steady. Via PhocusWire.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

Section four, policy and geopolitics. Two items that matter for the markets and the security picture.

United States ten year Treasury yields have blown past five point two percent, a nineteen year high, according to International Intrigue. Investors are pricing in a hawkish Federal Reserve, higher oil prices and heavy borrowing. It is a double squeeze on the big technology companies: higher yields weigh on their valuations and raise the cost of the artificial intelligence build-out that those valuations depend on. A rise of more than one percentage point in a single year is a big repricing for any government bond market, and worth having in mind in any conversation about investment. Via International Intrigue.

Second, a strange security story in Britain. Five men arrested near the Fairford airbase over a suspected bomb plot have all been released on bail after about a day, and forensic sweeps found no viable explosives in their vans. President Trump has voiced surprise, while Secretary of State Rubio insists a foreign actor was involved. British police can hold terror suspects for up to fourteen days, so an early release suggests the initial fears fell apart quickly. Watch for whether the Iran link gets any support. Via International Intrigue.

Now, over to community, events and marketing.

Section five, community, events and marketing. One idea worth stealing.

Skift shared an internal memo from its chief executive, Rafat Ali, after a run of five events in New York, and the point applies to anyone who runs gatherings. The real value of an event is not the follow-up emails or the session takeaways, which fade by Friday. It is whether attendees leave feeling more excited about the role they play in their industry. A companion tip from the same newsletter: Carnival Cruise Line has found that passengers on holiday will not sit through a ninety minute dinner, and now offers meals in under an hour. So, if your gala dinner has speeches between courses, try a shorter seated option, a walk-around format, or simply a free evening. Via Skift.

Now, over to one interesting thing to close things out.

Section six, one interesting thing. Adam Mastroianni, who writes Experimental History, has a new book called The Strangest Idea in the World, and it starts from a lovely question: why did it take humans three hundred thousand years to invent the toilet? The same puzzle applies to almost everything, since the first randomised controlled trial was not run until nineteen forty eight. He argues that humans did not evolve to do science, that discovery takes five unnatural steps, and that we are getting worse at it, with more money and people going in and fewer breakthroughs coming out. Oh, and the book is a choose your own adventure. Via Experimental History.
