Good morning, and welcome to a special Sunday edition of Byte-sized. This is the week in review, the week of the second of November, and as ever there are six sections, beginning in Japan and the Asia Pacific. Rather than a list of headlines, this is a look at where the week actually landed.

Section one, Japan and Asia Pacific.

The week in one line: the region spent it watching Washington's midterms, and then found its own politics just as noisy.

First, Fukuoka. The city votes on the fifteenth of November, after sixteen years under Mayor Soichiro Takashima, and the most useful thing to come out of the week is how sharply the candidates differ on foreign residents, from tighter restrictions to open calls against discrimination. The Kyushu Roundtable on the thirtieth of October put forty-six people from seventeen nationalities in one room to ask what growth should deliver, and their answers were practical: housing, transport and public spaces, not slogans. Meanwhile the economy keeps moving. A Mitsui outlet mall with about two hundred and twenty stores is due at Marinoa in April twenty twenty-seven, Kyushu posted record September exports, and Fukuoka airport reported record traffic. If you work with the Fukuoka ecosystem, the thing to watch after the fifteenth is whether the new mayor treats foreign residents and startups as a housing and transport problem or as a talking point. Via Fukuoka Now.

Second, driverless Japan. Toyota and Waymo are deepening their partnership and plan to test autonomous Toyota vehicles on Japanese roads by twenty twenty-seven. With an ageing population and a driver shortage, this is one of the first credible routes to driverless service at scale. Via T L D R Hardware.

Third, the market jitters. As the American midterm results came in, the Nikkei fell about four per cent as investors repriced technology bets. It was a reminder of how tightly Tokyo trades with the American tech cycle. Via International Intrigue.

Fourth, the chip and infrastructure story, which only got louder. Samsung and SK Hynix both say their high-bandwidth memory for A I servers is effectively sold out for years, to at least twenty twenty-eight. Google announced a fifteen billion dollar investment in Malaysia over two years for data centres. And Chinese robotics start-ups raised roughly thirteen billion dollars in the first half of the year, around five times the same period last year. Taken together, the physical layer of A I is spreading across Asia, and Japan's strengths in materials, equipment and robotics sit right in the middle of it. Via T L D R Hardware, T L D R I T and Benedict's Newsletter.

Fifth, regional politics. In Indonesia, President Prabowo arrested his chief of staff and several security chiefs over an alleged wiretapping ring, then put more than twenty thousand troops on the streets of Jakarta, though officials call it a routine rotation. The emerging theory is a factional fight with the camp of the former president, Jokowi. In South Korea, Seoul plans to extend its naval deployment in the Middle East, closer to Somalia, with extra mine clearing. International Intrigue's reading is that the Iran war has drained Western naval capacity, and a country importing nearly all its oil cannot leave its sea lanes to others. Japan has the same exposure. Next week the C S I S Korea Chair hosts a live podcast, on Tuesday the tenth, asking whether the United States, Japan and South Korea partnership is still standing three years after Camp David, with the Asia Pacific leaders' summit in China about two weeks away. Via International Intrigue and C S I S.

Now, over to A I and tech.

Section two, A I and tech.

The week in one line: the models got better faster, the buildings to run them got harder to build, and the incumbents scrambled.

First, the speed of progress itself. The evaluation group METR published an independent assessment, after weeks of access to Anthropic's internal data, concluding that Anthropic's A I research is accelerating, with model performance on well defined tasks improving at roughly twice the rate of twenty twenty-four to twenty twenty-five, largely because A I is now doing more of the research. Treat it as one assessment, but it is the first time an outsider has put a number on the feedback loop. Via T L D R A I and The Rundown A I.

Second, the product race. Apple opened its rebuilt Siri to developers in the iOS twenty-seven point three beta, though nothing ships to the App Store until spring and Europe is excluded for now. OpenAI released a cyber security model, GPT-6 Cyber, aimed at finding and validating exploitable weaknesses within limits set by researchers. Thinking Machines released a new mixture of experts model, Inkling 2 Small, with open weights promised for next week after safety testing. And Cursor now lets anyone describe a tool and get a live, deployed app with a shareable link. Via T L D R A I and The Rundown A I.

Third, the consolidation. Anthropic filed its S-one for a stock market listing, and Benedict Evans said the real value is finally seeing actual revenue and a real profit and loss account, while cautioning that the numbers are a snapshot, not a forecast. Elsewhere OpenAI said ChatGPT reaches about one point two billion people a week, Meta bought the coding start-up Conductor, and OpenAI acquired Pierre, which makes code review tools. DeepSeek is reportedly planning to train its next model on Huawei's chips, which would be a real test of whether China can reach the frontier without Nvidia. Via Benedict's Newsletter, T L D R A I and The Rundown A I.

Fourth, the backlash. In Tuesday's midterms, voters backed limits on data centres in thirty-eight of the thirty-nine local measures fully counted, and Goldman Sachs reportedly sees nearly half of planned American capacity at risk of temporary freezes. The lesson for anyone building infrastructure is that local consent is now as scarce as chips. Alongside it, CrowdStrike launched a kill switch to cut an agent's access to models, a sign that security for A I agents is becoming its own product category. Via The Rundown A I and T L D R I T.

Now, over to startups and venture.

Section three, startups and venture.

The week in one line: capital is concentrating, and the people allocating it are redrawing their maps.

First, Sequoia. It announced it is closing its London office after six years, winding down its scout programme and merging its early and growth teams. Sifted reports that the firm says it will keep investing in Europe, and that its European deal count this year, twelve through early November, is below last year's peak but above two years ago. The reaction split between gloom about Europe in managed decline and a shrug that this is normal churn. The same week, the Swedish firm Creandum raised seven hundred and fifty million dollars for a fund aimed at the United States as much as Europe. Both moves point the same way: the best-funded A I companies are in San Francisco, and everyone is following the money there. The practical advice from one Berlin investor was to think hard before raising your earliest rounds from American multi-stage funds. Via Sifted and T L D R Fintech.

Second, Revolut completed a secondary share sale at a one hundred and fifteen billion dollar valuation, up fifty-three per cent on the year, and is preparing to apply for a national bank in the United States. It also secured a banking licence in New Zealand. Via T L D R Fintech and International Intrigue.

Third, infrastructure funding stayed hot. The model hosting platform fal raised one point three billion dollars at a sixteen billion dollar valuation, double its April figure. Against that, a cautionary analysis argued that A I labs face a squeeze, with token prices falling while the chips behind them get more expensive to rent, and Ramp data suggested the biggest A I spenders trimmed spending per employee over the summer. Menlo Ventures' enterprise report said frontier labs capture most deployment spending, yet start-ups still take a majority of application spending. Via T L D R Founders and The Rundown A I.

Fourth, a niche worth knowing. Sifted notes the rise of defence roll-ups, where start-ups buy up small fragmented suppliers to gain scale and faster access to government contracts, though some investors doubt it suits venture returns. And on the bootstrapping side, Indie Hackers noted that beehiiv has reached three million dollars in monthly recurring revenue, while The V C Corner profiled RB2B, which reached ten million dollars in annual recurring revenue with a single full-time employee. Via Sifted, Indie Hackers and The V C Corner.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

Section four, policy and geopolitics.

The week in one line: the midterms did not change the foreign policy map overnight, but everybody is recalculating.

First, how it looks from abroad. International Intrigue argued that Iran, China, Russia and Europe are all adjusting. A Democratic Congress cannot dictate foreign policy, but it controls the Pentagon's purse. Beijing is pleased with the stabilised relationship but wary of a cornered president. The thing to watch is the Asia Pacific summit in China later this month, where Mr Trump may meet both Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin. Talks between Russia and Ukraine are due to resume in Abu Dhabi next week. Via International Intrigue.

Second, China's overcapacity. A new C S I S episode previews a joint report on how Chinese industrial overcapacity is landing in South East Asia, relevant to anyone discussing trade diversion and supply chains with government or enterprise people. Via C S I S.

Third, three stories that show political risk is travelling. In Paraguay the president resigned after the collapse of a fast-growing digital bank he had part-owned, which left half the country's adults locked out of their accounts. In France, unions mobilised hundreds of thousands of people in a national day of action over wages. And a leaked document suggests Russia agreed to supply Iran with ninety helicopter-mounted missile launchers. Via International Intrigue.

Now, over to community, events and marketing.

Section five, community, events and marketing.

This section is about ideas worth stealing.

The first idea comes from Fukuoka Now. Its Kyushu Roundtable is a small, curated room, around forty people, with a guest commentator and an application form, not an open ticket. The next, on the fourth of December at C I C Fukuoka, is about cycling and urban mobility, with a bonenkai afterwards. The tactic is to pick one civic topic that residents already complain about, and give it a format. That is how you build a community that governments listen to.

The second idea is from Priya Parker, who this week talked with the minister and author Jack Shitama about being a non-anxious presence. His point is that you can feel anxious and still lead a room well: pause and breathe, get clear on what you believe before a difficult conversation, say what you think without telling others what to think, and use a little playfulness. It is a useful brief for anyone hosting a tense panel or a board meeting.

The third idea is from Marketing Brew: creators are inventing unusual advertising inventory, and brands are buying it. The lesson is that the new media space is anything an audience pays attention to. Via Fukuoka Now, Priya Parker and Marketing Brew.

Now, one interesting thing to finish.

Section six, one interesting thing.

Marginal Revolution flagged a proposal in Delaware for what it calls an A I company: a separate legal entity whose day to day affairs are run by an A I agent rather than a person. It could sue and be sued in its own name and hold property. If that becomes law, the question stops being whether machines can think, and becomes whether a machine can own things. Via Marginal Revolution.
