Good morning, and welcome to Byte-sized for Tuesday the seventeenth of November. Six sections today, starting as always in Japan and the Asia Pacific.

Section one, Japan and Asia Pacific.

First, Fukuoka votes, and nobody wins. Sunday's mayoral election in Fukuoka produced no winner at all. Thirteen candidates stood, and none of them reached the legal threshold of twenty-five per cent of valid votes, so the city now has to hold a completely fresh election. Importantly, this is not a run-off between the top two: it is a new contest, and the field could change. For anyone working with the city on startup or international programmes, expect a longer period of caretaker politics and a pause on big new commitments. Fukuoka Now's Kyushu Roundtable will pick over the result at its final session of the year on the fourth of December, at C I C in Tenjin, alongside a discussion on cycling in the city and a year-end party. Via Fukuoka Now.

Second, Australia and the Philippines tighten their defence ties. At the A SEAN summit in Manila on Sunday, Canberra and Manila signed a new Defence Cooperation Agreement, designed to make joint operations quicker to organise and broader in scope. The subtext is deterrence, given the Philippines' running territorial disputes with China in the South China Sea. The deal also opens the door to Australian investment in maritime surveillance and drone technology across the archipelago. For Japan, which has its own reciprocal access agreements with both countries, this is another strand in a web of partnerships forming among America's allies in the region, increasingly without Washington at the centre. Via International Intrigue.

Third, Samsung becomes a kingmaker in European tech. Sifted reports that the Korean electronics giant has backed nine European startups this year, which together have raised four and a half billion euros, up from six companies raising around half a billion the year before. It has even led some of the continent's biggest rounds, including Mistral's three billion euro Series D. Bankers describe the logic simply: Europe has the research and deep tech, Asia has the factories, customers and capital to bring it to market. It is a pattern Japanese corporate venture arms could study closely, and a reminder that Asian strategic investors are now competing directly with Silicon Valley for Europe's best companies. Via Sifted.

And a lighter one to finish the section. In Kawanishi, in Hyogo prefecture, a professional wrestler called Billyken Kid has been elected to the municipal assembly, and has won a vote allowing him to keep his trademark yellow luchador mask on during council meetings. His argument was that he campaigned in the mask and voters chose him in it. Critics wanted a more conventional look, but the mask stays. Via International Intrigue.

Now, over to A I and tech.

Section two, A I and tech.

First, the campaign to bring back Jensen Huang. Last week Nvidia's board accepted Huang's resignation amid reports of multiple affairs and concerns linked to a suspected Chinese intelligence operation, and named the chief financial officer, Colette Kress, as interim chief executive. Now a petition calling for his reinstatement has passed a hundred and forty thousand signatures, including Elon Musk's, and more than seven thousand signers say they are Nvidia staff. Musk's argument is that many of Nvidia's supply arrangements were, in his words, a handshake with Jensen rather than a contract. The Financial Times reports Huang is privately weighing a return. Against that, the American Treasury Secretary, Scott Bessent, has called for an outsider to take the job, and has given the company ten days to answer questions about its culture and its compliance with chip export controls. Kress presents quarterly results today, so watch that call closely. Via The Rundown A I and International Intrigue.

Second, the agents get wallets. OpenAI has launched ChatGPT Wallet, starting with American Pro subscribers, which lets A I agents complete purchases without ever seeing the user's real card details, and requires a security check on every transaction. In Europe, Starling's founder Anne Boden is building a new bank designed from the start for agents paying on customers' behalf. But not everyone is welcoming the bots. Tom Tunguz points out that Amazon and United Airlines make much of their profit on extras such as sponsored listings and seat upgrades, which agents simply skip, so both now block A I shopping agents. Shopify, which takes the same cut whoever clicks buy, opened its checkout to agents in September. The lesson: whether a business welcomes agents depends entirely on where its margin lives. Via T L D R and Sifted.

Third, OpenAI reshuffles robotics. According to Fortune, OpenAI has folded its robotics team into its main research and product groups, arguing that general models such as G P T six Astra can now run robots directly without a separate, specialised brain. Several staff publicly disputed the framing, insisting the robotics programme is alive and well, with open roles still being filled. Either way, the bigger signal is that frontier labs increasingly believe general models can handle physical tasks, which matters for every robotics startup building its own model stack. Via The Rundown A I.

And one release worth noting from China: Alibaba's Qwen team has launched Qwen four Max, pitched as a generalist teammate for three-D modelling, game development, finance, law and healthcare, with smaller models in the family promised as open source. Via T L D R A I.

Now, over to startups and venture.

Section three, startups and venture.

First, the A I mega-round reality check. Anthropic has set its I P O price range at seventy to seventy-eight dollars a share, implying a valuation of up to about one point four trillion dollars, which is well below the two trillion figure being floated a couple of months ago. OpenAI, meanwhile, has closed the first eleven billion dollars of a round that may reach around twenty billion, short of the thirty billion it originally sought, at a one point two five trillion valuation. The gap between private hype and public pricing is narrowing, and that resets the benchmark for every late-stage A I company. Via The V C Corner.

Second, the funding barbell gets more extreme. A new report finds that first rounds, seed and Series A, have fallen to around half their twenty twenty-two levels, from about ten thousand two hundred deals a quarter to five thousand two hundred. Meanwhile, in the third quarter, just two hundred and ninety-nine rounds of a hundred million dollars or more took eighty per cent of all venture money. Related: subscription software companies are adding less new revenue than a year ago, with the steepest drop among cheap products under fifty dollars a month, while OpenAI and Anthropic have more than tripled their revenue this year. Budgets are moving, and founders selling small tools should notice. Via The V C Corner and T L D R Founders.

Third, does indexing Y Combinator work? The V C Corner revisits Yuri Milner's two thousand eleven offer to back every startup in a Y Combinator batch on the same terms. According to Y Combinator's own study, investors who backed at least three companies at every Demo Day from twenty eighteen to twenty twenty made a median of five times their money, against roughly one to one and a half times for the median venture fund of those years. The writer is rightly sceptical of self-reported numbers, but notes the batch you pick can swing returns by a factor of twenty-four, so consistency beats timing. With Demo Day on the second of December, it is a timely read for angels. Via The V C Corner.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

Section four, policy and geopolitics.

First, Ukraine talks restart, with Europe's security on the table. Last week America, Ukraine and Russia held their first in-person three-way talks since February, in Abu Dhabi, followed by American envoys visiting Moscow. President Zelensky says American negotiators floated a new idea: Ukraine cedes all of the Donbas in exchange for NATO membership. Russia, for its part, reportedly tabled demands that reach well beyond Ukraine, including pulling NATO forces back to their nineteen ninety-seven borders. The European Union has now named a negotiating team including Britain, France, Germany, Italy and Poland. The takeaway for government audiences: the conversation is shifting from how to end one war to how Europe's whole security architecture should look, which is exactly where Moscow wants it. Via International Intrigue.

Second, the Black Sea is no longer safe for merchant shipping. Geopolitical Monitor counts three hundred and seventeen vessels struck by Russian and Ukrainian drones in the Black Sea and the Sea of Azov between May and October. Its conclusion is stark: neither navy can protect commercial ships, so only a truce looks likely to reopen the sea. Add Yemen, where government-aligned forces have pulled back from the Bab al-Mandeb strait, leaving the Houthis in control of the gateway to the Red Sea, and two of the world's key shipping chokepoints are under serious strain at once. Via Geopolitical Monitor and International Intrigue.

Now, over to community, events and marketing.

Section five, community, events and marketing.

The idea worth stealing comes from CMX. This autumn they ran what they called a Circuit Tour: ten events in nine cities, meeting over a hundred community professionals in person. The clever part is that the tour was not an end in itself. Many stops were used to launch new local chapters, with chapter leads hosting, so the energy of a one-off event turned into something that keeps meeting after the tour bus leaves. If you run events across a region like Kyushu, consider designing each stop to leave behind a local host and a next date, rather than a thank-you email. CMX also shows a good nudge tactic: telling people they are at a hundred and eighty-nine award nominations against two hundred and ten last year, which turns a routine reminder into a shared target. Via CMX.

And a quick one from T L D R Founders: AppSumo's founder asked an A I assistant to check which of sixty-seven shipped changes had actually moved a metric. The answer was four. Worth running the same audit on your own launches and campaigns. Via T L D R Founders.

Now, over to one interesting thing.

Section six, one interesting thing.

Dan Wang, writing in Works in Progress, points out that most countries have one dominant city: Paris is seven times bigger than France's next largest, Seoul six times bigger than Korea's. America and China are different. Beijing is not China's biggest city, and Washington is not even among America's top five metro areas. Wang argues that this many-centred structure, with regions like Sichuan, Guangdong, Texas and Florida all competing with the capital, is part of what lets both superpowers combine dynamism with a powerful state. It is a thought worth holding onto as Fukuoka chooses its next mayor, twice.
