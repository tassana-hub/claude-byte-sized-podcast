Hello, and welcome to Byte-sized, week of the third to the ninth of August. This is the week in review, so instead of a straight news run, I'm pulling together the stories from the last seven days that still matter now that the dust has settled. Six sections today: Japan and Asia-Pacific, AI and tech, startups and venture, policy and geopolitics, community and marketing, and one interesting thing to close things out. Let's get into it.

This week in the Asia-Pacific, the story was instability: political upsets in Malaysia, a sharpening security picture around China, and a reminder that the region's defence planners are treating Japan's own posture as central to how the wider Indo-Pacific balance holds together. None of these stories broke in isolation either — taken together, they paint a picture of a region where domestic politics, great-power manoeuvring and alliance planning are all moving at once, and where Tokyo has a stake in how each of them lands.

First story. Anwar's coalition takes another beating. In Malaysia, Prime Minister Anwar Ibrahim's ruling Pakatan Harapan coalition suffered a heavy defeat in Negeri Sembilan state elections on the first of August, losing badly to a partnership between the Barisan Nasional and Perikatan Nasional blocs, who together won a supermajority of twenty-five of the state's thirty-six assembly seats. Pakatan Harapan's own seat share collapsed from seventeen down to eleven. It's the second big loss in a matter of weeks, after the coalition was routed in Johor back in July, and it follows an even heavier defeat in Sabah state last November. Anwar's term technically runs until February twenty twenty-eight, but the string of losses is fuelling speculation that snap national elections could come much sooner than that. Barisan Nasional and Perikatan Nasional are already expected to team up again to push for early elections in Melaka state, which would extend the same pattern that's now cost Anwar's coalition three state contests in a row. Via CSIS Southeast Asia Program.

Second story. China's missile test rattles the Pacific. CSIS's Pacific Airwaves podcast this week dug into a recent Chinese missile test and what it signals for the shifting balance of power across the Pacific. The conversation centred on how mutual defence commitments in the region are being tested by Beijing's growing willingness to demonstrate hard military capability, rather than just assert territorial claims through grey-zone pressure. For a region where alliance credibility is the whole game, that distinction matters. Via CSIS Southeast Asia Program.

Third story. Beijing and Pyongyang, old friends with new calculations. CSIS's Korea Chair flagged a notable warming between China and North Korea this week, describing it as a reset in the relationship that now extends beyond diplomacy into military affairs. That kind of deepening tie on the Korean peninsula is exactly the sort of development that gets read closely in Tokyo, given how directly it touches the security balance on Japan's own doorstep. Via CSIS Korea Chair.

Fourth story. New research on Japan's defence posture. On the policy research side, CSIS's Defense and Security Department flagged new publications this week specifically on Japan's defence posture, alongside its ongoing work on cybersecurity maturity model certification for defence contractors. Taken together with everything else this week, it underlines how allied planners are treating Japan's military modernisation as inseparable from the broader Indo-Pacific security picture, not a side issue to it. Via CSIS.

Now, over to AI and tech, where it was a week of tectonic plates shifting under the biggest labs and chipmakers, with a research result that's going to keep people up at night for a different reason entirely. If there's one throughline across this week's AI stories, it's control: who controls the chips, who controls the leadership at the top of the biggest labs, and, in the most unsettling case, who controls what AI is allowed to design.

First story. Google DeepMind's leadership shake-up. Google reshuffled its AI leadership this week in a way that caught markets off guard. Demis Hassabis moved from chief executive of DeepMind into the role of chair of DeepMind and chief scientist of Alphabet, while Jeff Dean, a twenty-seven-year veteran of the company and one of its most storied engineers, departed entirely to launch his own venture, called Discovery Loop. Alphabet's shares fell more than five percent on the news, which tells you how nervous investors are about any change at the centre of Google's AI strategy right now, even one framed internally as a promotion. Via TLDR AI.

Second story. The chip wars widen. AMD announced a deal to acquire the Toronto chip startup Taalas, whose technology hard-wires AI model weights directly into semiconductor metal layers during manufacturing, making inference dramatically cheaper and more power-efficient. AMD plans to fold that into its accelerator roadmap alongside its existing Instinct chips. In a related move this week, Anthropic confirmed it is building its own in-house chip design team for Claude, aiming to co-design hardware and software together to cut inference costs and boost speed at scale. That puts Anthropic alongside Google, Amazon and Microsoft in wanting to own more of its own silicon destiny rather than depending entirely on outside suppliers. Via TLDR Hardware and TLDR AI.

Third story. Scientists get AI to design viruses from scratch. In a genuinely startling piece of research published in the journal Science, Stanford and Arc Institute researchers used AI models called Evo to design sixteen entirely new, functioning viruses that don't exist anywhere in nature. They trained the models on millions of genomes, had them write new versions of a well-studied virus that infects only a common gut bacteria, then synthesised the results in the lab. Of two hundred and eighty-five designed viruses tested, sixteen turned out to be viable, with some replicating even faster than the natural version they were modelled on. The viruses are harmless to humans and are aimed at fighting drug-resistant bacterial infections, but the achievement raises an obvious concern: the same design capability could, in principle, be turned toward something far more dangerous, the same way cybersecurity tools sometimes get repurposed by attackers. Researchers involved were quick to note this is exactly why biosecurity oversight needs to keep pace with what these models can now do, rather than catching up after the fact. Via The Rundown and TLDR.

Fourth story. A worm called Shai-Hulud comes back bigger. On the security side, a new variant of the Shai-Hulud worm tore through the open-source software supply chain this week, compromising more than one thousand two hundred npm packages, including the widely used Keyv library, by hijacking GitHub Actions pipelines to inject malicious commits. Once installed, it aggressively harvests developer and cloud credentials to spread itself further. Security researchers are telling any organisation that installed the affected package versions to assume they have already been breached, and to rotate every secret immediately rather than waiting for a scheduled vulnerability scan. It is the same worm family that caused chaos earlier in the year, and its return, bigger than before, suggests the open-source supply chain still hasn't found a real fix for this class of attack. Every company that pulls in third-party packages without a serious review process is, in effect, trusting thousands of strangers by default, and this is what happens when that trust gets abused at scale. Via TLDR Information Security.

Now, over to startups and venture, where hard tech had a genuinely blockbuster week, and one long-running mystery finally got resolved.

First story. Yann LeCun's venture capital mystery finally resolved. The long saga of Yann LeCun's venture ambitions appears to have reached an ending. The former Meta chief AI scientist has joined a new AI-focused fund called two-two-four Ventures, where he will invest alongside Google DeepMind researcher Oriol Vinyals and AIX Ventures founder Shaun Johnson, with the trio investing exclusively through that vehicle. It follows an awkward earlier chapter in which LeCun was reported to be a non-managing general partner at a different fund, Extelligence Invest, before quitting after conflicting exclusivity arrangements with other funds came to light. LeCun has since put the episode down to, in his words, a miscommunication and a misunderstanding, without elaborating further. Whatever the backstory, it's a reminder that even the most senior names in AI are still working out where they land in a venture landscape that's reshuffling constantly around them. Via Sifted.

Second story. Hard tech's billion-dollar week. It was a blockbuster week for hard tech funding. Base Power Company raised one billion dollars at a thirteen billion dollar valuation to launch a new product called Base Core. Robotics manufacturer Hadrian raised one billion three hundred and seventy million dollars at close to an eight billion dollar valuation, with its chief executive making the case that, in his words, the world of atoms is a hundred times bigger than the world of bits. And nuclear fuel and reactor company Valar Atomics pulled in a billion dollars from Sequoia, on top of two hundred billion dollars in debt financing to build out its reactor pipeline. Terraform Industries also claimed a milestone in turning sunlight into hydrogen at two dollars per kilogram, a cost breakthrough that, if it holds up at scale, could make synthetic fuel genuinely competitive with fossil alternatives, and would give humanity a way to print the equivalent of refined oil from nothing but sunlight and air. Taken together, it's the clearest sign yet that venture money which spent the last few years chasing software is now chasing atoms just as hard. Via Not Boring.

Third story. Founders are professionalising fundraising. Elsewhere in venture, there's a clear trend toward founders treating fundraising as an operating process rather than an art form. This week brought crowdsourced trackers of exactly which startups are actively raising, by stage, sector and geography, alongside prompt packs designed to replicate the kind of diligence audit that used to cost six figures from a consultancy. It's a sign of just how commoditised the mechanics of raising money have become, even as the actual decision of who gets funded stays as subjective, and as relationship-driven, as ever. Via TheVCCorner.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

First story. Pakistan's quiet outsourcing of security. A sobering piece from Geopolitical Monitor this week argued that Pakistan is increasingly outsourcing its internal security to non-state actors, faced with mounting threats it cannot fully contain through formal state channels. The piece frames this as a slow devolution of the state's core function, one that raises long-term questions about who actually controls the use of force inside the country, and what that means for a nuclear-armed state already dealing with instability on multiple borders. Via Geopolitical Monitor.

Second story. China's dual economy problem. On CSIS's Pekingology podcast, economist Alicia García-Herrero unpacked what she calls China's dual economy: a country generating genuine breakthroughs in electric vehicles, battery technology, applied artificial intelligence and industrial robotics, while ordinary households face stagnant wages, weak consumption, and declining public confidence in the economy. The conversation raised a pointed question for Beijing, about whether President Xi's prioritisation of global technological leadership over domestic economic opportunity can hold if rapid AI adoption ends up displacing workers faster than it creates new ground for them to stand on. Via CSIS Pekingology.

Third story. The Middle East's medicine problem. CSIS's Middle East programme published a sharp piece this week on pharmaceutical self-sufficiency, noting that the region is one of the most import-dependent in the world for medicine, a vulnerability that's been thrown into relief by the risk of shortages linked to the Iran war. Saudi Arabia, Qatar and the United Arab Emirates have all launched national strategies to localise pharmaceutical manufacturing, but between twenty fifteen and twenty twenty-five, net pharmaceutical imports actually rose across all three countries, largely driven by demand for newer, patented drugs including the GLP-1 weight-loss and diabetes medications. There's a silver lining flagged in the piece though: as many of those drug patents expire in twenty twenty-six, the door opens for regional generic manufacturers to finally close some of that gap. Via CSIS Middle East Program.

Now, over to community, events and marketing, the practical takeaways from this week's stack. Two ideas this week, both about trust: how you earn it before someone joins your community, and how you borrow it from a story your audience already believes in.

First story, and one idea worth stealing: give your community windows, not just doors. A neat piece from Community Inc this week argued that most communities that want to grow are hiding too much value behind a login wall, when what actually builds trust is letting people see in before they're asked to join. The suggestion is to think of community access less like a locked door and more like a shop window: let people watch the conversation happening before you ask them to commit to it. It's a small reframe, but a useful one for anyone running a paid or gated community who's puzzled about why sign-ups have gone quiet. Via Community Inc.

Second story, and one more idea worth stealing: borrow a face people already trust. Marketing Brew's roundup this week pointed to brands leaning harder into cultural tie-ins that borrow an audience's existing goodwill, from a soccer club effectively hiring its own version of the fictional coach Ted Lasso to lead its marketing, to brands buying into existing late-night internet shows rather than building their own channel from a standing start. The underlying lesson for anyone in growth or marketing is that it's often cheaper, and faster, to attach yourself to a story people are already following than to start a brand new one from nothing. Via Marketing Brew.

And now, to close, one interesting thing.

Writer Adam Mastroianni argues, in a piece called Incentives Are For Losers, that we've become too comfortable blaming bad incentives for bad behaviour, as though being bribed into doing the wrong thing were some unavoidable natural law rather than a choice anyone could refuse. His comparison is Little League baseball: the kids who brought their own properly fitted batting helmets played fearlessly, while everyone stuck using the shared, ill-fitting, faintly unhygienic ones flinched at every single pitch. His point is that if you don't bring your own standards into a situation, you'll end up living by whatever flimsy ones are handed to you, and that's a failure of design, not a fact of life. Via Experimental History.
