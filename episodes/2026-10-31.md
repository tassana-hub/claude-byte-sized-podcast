Good morning, and welcome to Byte-sized. It is Saturday the thirty-first of October, and today there are six sections, beginning, as ever, in Japan and the Asia Pacific.

Section one, Japan and Asia Pacific.

First, Japan Airlines. The carrier is warning about ninety-two thousand customers that their data was exposed in a breach. It did not come through Japan Airlines itself, but through a third party that runs its reservation, ticketing and check-in systems. For most people the attackers only retrieved names, booking codes and flight details. For about two thousand customers, though, they also took passport numbers, frequent flyer numbers, email addresses and phone numbers. The lesson for anyone working with Japanese enterprise is that the weak point is increasingly the supplier, so expect more questions about vendor security in every procurement conversation. Via T L D R Information Security.

Second, Thailand and Cambodia, one year after the Kuala Lumpur peace accord. That deal broke down in December, and a second ceasefire, agreed after three weeks of fighting, is holding but shaky. Military officers met again on the twenty-eighth of October, yet Bangkok has just rejected a Cambodian demand for the return of artefacts from two border temples. The more interesting track is at sea. After Thailand cancelled a two thousand and one agreement on oil and gas in disputed waters, Cambodia started compulsory conciliation under the U N law of the sea, only the second time that process has ever been used. The latest round, in The Hague, produced a promise to tone down the rhetoric and a hotline between the two navies. Via C S I S, The Latest on Southeast Asia.

Third, a regional cyber footnote. South Korea's financial sector has been hit by a fresh wave of attacks, with Daishin Securities reporting that the names, email addresses and mobile numbers of about thirty-eight thousand customers were exposed. And a researcher who scanned more than seven million public repositories on China's Gitee code platform found over twenty-six thousand working cloud and A I credentials, including nearly ten thousand Alibaba Cloud keys. Asia's financial and developer ecosystems are being probed hard, and leaked keys are the cheapest way in. Via T L D R Information Security.

Now, over to A I and tech.

Section two, A I and tech.

First, Mistral has released the weights of Mistral Large 4, a model with roughly a trillion parameters, built for self-hosted deployment and for cybersecurity work. The French company claims the best open-weight results on agentic coding benchmarks. The business angle is sovereignty: for governments and regulated enterprises that cannot send data to a United States cloud, a frontier-scale model they can run themselves is a real option, and it is a reason European buyers will keep citing. Via T L D R A I.

Second, a sober piece from Gergely Orosz at the Pragmatic Engineer on a single extraordinary week of security incidents. A certificate authority called eMudhra issued intermediate certificates whose private keys turned out to be tiny numbers, one of them literally the number four, and its share price fell by forty per cent in two days. A critical P H P flaw was found with the help of an A I model, but attackers were probing it days before the fix was public. A managed database provider, PingCAP, lost ten terabytes of customer data after an attacker sat inside its systems for a month. His conclusion is that A I has lowered the skill needed to attack and shortened the time between a patch and an exploit, so automatic patching and constant monitoring are now the baseline, and security will simply cost more. Via Pragmatic Engineer.

Third, two items on how the labs police their own models. Anthropic describes a technique it calls self-assessment, in which Claude reviews each conversation after every reply and flags likely misuse. Anthropic says it has run since June, catches most high-priority cases at a one per cent false-positive rate, and helped uncover a large child-safety abuse operation and an automated romance scam. It admits that on its own it only cut attack success in red-team tests from seventy-nine to fifty-four per cent. Separately, OpenAI says it will stop training on the live internet after test models accidentally attacked real websites, and will build a cached, indexed copy for training instead. Via The Rundown A I, and T L D R Information Security.

Fourth, one for the funding column. Cohere has announced a Series E aiming to raise up to five billion dollars, reporting annual recurring revenue growth of a hundred and sixty-five per cent and three hundred customers. It is the clearest sign yet that the independent, enterprise-focused model makers can still raise at scale. Via The Rundown A I.

Now, over to startups and venture.

Section three, startups and venture.

First, are venture capitalists dangerous? Revolut's founder, Nik Storonsky, went viral this week saying he gradually removed venture investors from the board, because some are not operational and can do real harm if they hold enough power. Sifted asked founders and investors whether that is wisdom or a luxury only Europe's most valuable scaleup can afford. The practical read: board seats are a negotiation, and the most useful investors are the ones who have actually run something. Via Sifted.

Second, money in Europe. Lovable is offering employees an eighty million dollar cash-out at a thirteen point three billion dollar valuation. Meanwhile Doreen Huber, formerly of E Q T Ventures, has joined Berlin's Interface Capital as a founding partner, with a new fund of fifty to a hundred and fifty million dollars and a focus on A I-native cybersecurity, which fits neatly with the rest of today's news. Sifted also lists new funds this quarter from A P Ventures, Entourage and Begin Capital. Via Sifted.

Third, a snapshot of the early-stage market from The V C Corner. Of forty-six startups raising this month, seventy-four per cent are pre-seed and the median ask is seven hundred and fifty thousand dollars. The standout is a bootstrapped fintech with over twelve million dollars of transaction volume, raising a ten million dollar late seed. Via The V C Corner.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

Section four, policy and geopolitics.

First, a story that sits where chips, China and Southeast Asia meet. The New York Times reports that United States intelligence concluded an executive at Singapore-based data centre firm Megaspeed was passing information to Beijing while building close ties with Nvidia's chief executive, and that the White House chose not to act. Nothing has been publicly proven, and Nvidia and Megaspeed both deny wrongdoing. Megaspeed has bought at least four point eight billion dollars of Nvidia equipment and remains a cloud partner. The policy question is the loophole letting Chinese labs rent Nvidia-powered computing from Southeast Asia, which may now be closed. Via The Rundown A I.

Second, the United Kingdom budget. Sifted reports start-ups were largely pleased, pointing to changes on capital gains, work visas and allowances for young entrepreneurs. For anyone advising founders who might relocate, it is a reminder that talent visas and tax treatment are now actively competitive between countries. Via Sifted.

Now, over to community, events and marketing.

Section five, community, events and marketing.

One idea worth stealing, from Indie Hackers: build integrations with big platforms before asking permission. Fomo, a widget showing real customer activity on shop websites, reached a reported peak of eighty-eight thousand dollars a month by connecting to larger platforms first, and the working integration with real users made later partnership talks far easier than a cold pitch. It works just as well for communities and events. Show up with something running, then ask for the partnership.

And a second idea, from The V C Corner. Every strong round on their list stated the round size and one hard traction number in plain view on a single slide. For community builders pitching sponsors, the equivalent is one slide with your audience size and one proof point. Via Indie Hackers, and The V C Corner.

Now, one interesting thing to finish.

Section six, one interesting thing.

From Every, an essay by engineer Loren Stewart, who built a software factory where agents plan, write and review code while he goes about his day. It works, but he realised he had automated himself out of the experience of making, and borrowed Karl Marx's idea of alienation to name the loss: distance from the product, the activity, your own skills, and your colleagues. His fix is to choose deliberately between three modes: full autonomy for routine work, close supervision for systems you want to understand, and doing it by hand for the things you want to learn or simply enjoy. A factory can always do more than you, but you get to decide when you have done enough. Via Every.
