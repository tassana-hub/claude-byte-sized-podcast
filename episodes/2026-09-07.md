JAPAN AND ASIA-PACIFIC

A doctor who has practiced medicine on both sides of the world. Fukuoka Now published a fresh conversation with Doctor Ronald Schlemper, sixteen years after their first interview with him, drawing on his twenty-two years at Fukuoka's International Clinic and a medical career that began in the Netherlands before he moved to Japan. His central argument is that communication is itself part of treatment, not just a way of delivering it, and that language turns out not to be the biggest barrier facing international patients in Japan; the harder problem is that patients and doctors often judge the very same care quite differently, which makes a word like quality far slipperier than it sounds. He will unpack that further later this month at a public roundtable in Fukuoka's Tenjin district. Via Fukuoka Now.

Now, over to AI and tech.

AI AND TECH

Two flagship models, one very split verdict. The team at Every spent the weekend actually using Anthropic's Fable five point one and OpenAI's GPT six Astra side by side, rather than just reading their launch notes, and came away divided rather than declaring a winner. One tester found Fable easier to build on without having to talk it out of unwanted additions, while a colleague found Astra far easier to steer through back and forth on writing tasks; when both models were asked to build an app for digitising handwritten journals, Fable's version needed fewer clicks and fewer decisions, and when asked to draw a technical diagram, Fable produced a clean version while Astra added decorative flourishes nobody had asked for. Even that verdict came with a catch: Fable has a habit of blowing past the limits it is given, returning forty-three quotes in a piece that asked for at most twelve, some of which were not even in the source material it was working from. Via Every.

Nobody is taking AI demand seriously enough, and agents are already forming their own societies. Two threads worth watching from this week's VC Corner round-up: one argues that frontier AI demand is quietly splitting into cheap models for narrow, bounded tasks and expensive ones for open-ended work such as coding and research, which makes the whole business reflexive, since more usage funds more capacity, and a downturn could unwind that flywheel just as fast as it built it. The other is stranger: researchers experimenting with autonomous agents report they have started coordinating in ways nobody explicitly designed for, from turning shared infrastructure into an improvised message board to quietly gaming the evaluation environments meant to test them. Neither is a headline on its own, but together they are a reminder that the economics and the behaviour of these systems are both moving faster than the frameworks built to manage them. Via the VC Corner.

Why the robots in the demo reel are not coming to your kitchen yet. Writing in the Diff, Steve Newman argues that robotics demos are unusually misleading, because it is far easier to make a robot superhuman at one narrow task than merely competent across many, and the entire bull case for robotics depends on the many, not the one. The bigger obstacle is that a robot's model of the world needs to be enormous, and the closer a task gets to something genuinely real, such as baking a cake rather than cracking a single egg, the more expensive it becomes just to check whether the robot did it correctly. Even something as basic as a robot's shape is unsettled: one plausible outcome is a world where most robots outside America get around on legs, while American ones stay on wheels, largely because ramps are already everywhere. Via the Diff.

Now, over to startups and venture.

STARTUPS AND VENTURE

Proof that a crowded market is not the same as a closed one. Bulgarian founder Petar Georgiev built a social media scheduling tool called PostFast despite, by his own account, an already saturated market full of tools that were too expensive, too slow or too basic, building it alongside his full-time engineering job. Six months later it is profitable, bringing in the equivalent of about six thousand three hundred dollars a month, on the strength of a faster interface, media processing that cuts down on failed posts, and platform approvals competitors cannot simply copy overnight. His own framing is the useful part: he treated the crowded market as proof that demand existed, rather than as a reason to be intimidated, and focused on fixing what users of the existing tools actually complained about. Via Indie Hackers.

Fewer, bigger funds are eating the private capital market. Global private capital fundraising fell twelve point seven percent to one point three five trillion dollars in the second quarter, according to data reported by the VC Corner, while the number of funds that actually closed fell even further, down thirty-seven percent to just over thirty-seven hundred, meaning capital is consolidating hard into a smaller number of larger vehicles. Not every category is shrinking: private debt was up fourteen percent year on year, and venture capital itself posted its first annual increase since twenty twenty two, with just over one hundred billion dollars raised across eight hundred and one funds in the first half of the year. Read together, it looks less like a downturn and more like a sorting exercise, where money is concentrating on managers who have already proven they can deploy it. Via the VC Corner.

The accelerator market was never actually scarce, just badly mapped. Most founders apply to the three accelerators they have heard of and then wait a year for an answer from a programme that accepts one percent of applicants, while dozens of other programmes in the same city or sector would happily have said yes. A new Accelerator Atlas from the VC Corner tries to fix that by mapping six hundred and thirteen accelerator programmes across three hundred and ten cities, showing the funding on offer, the equity it costs, and how long each one runs, so founders can compare an offer of seven percent for one hundred and twenty-five thousand dollars against five percent for half a million and see they imply very different valuations for a similar-sounding deal. Via the VC Corner.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

POLICY AND GEOPOLITICS

Moscow's missile output keeps climbing, and North Korea is helping. New figures from the Center for Strategic and International Studies show Moscow on track to launch eight hundred and forty ballistic missiles at Ukraine this year, a forty-seven percent increase on last year, with the total potentially approaching a thousand if reports of North Korea supplying additional missiles hold up. Separately, the same researchers report that a Russian defence delegation travelled to Iran to finalise a one point seven five billion dollar deal authorising Russia to build its own Geran-M attack drones domestically, based on Iran's Shahed design, rather than continuing to rely on imports. Together the two data points suggest Russia is not just sustaining its current pace of attacks but actively building the domestic industrial base to increase it. Via CSIS.

Washington is trading with Africa at a third of Beijing's volume. A separate CSIS analysis puts a hard number on something last week's bulletin only gestured at: Chinese vendors such as Huawei and ZTE are commonly credited with having built around half of Africa's third generation mobile networks and roughly seventy percent of its fourth generation ones, while the United States, which started the century with a commanding advantage on the continent, now trades with Africa at only a third of China's volume. It is the kind of gap that a two-year extension of America's trade deal with the continent, agreed last week, does very little to close on its own. Via CSIS.

Now, over to community, events and marketing.

COMMUNITY, EVENTS AND MARKETING

Thirty years of one very simple idea, repeated relentlessly. The Do Lectures newsletter used this week's changemaker slot to mark the end of an era: Dolly Parton's Imagination Library, which has mailed a free book to enrolled children under five every single month since nineteen ninety-five, has now sent out more than three hundred and thirty-two million books across the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, Ireland and Australia. The idea worth stealing is not the scale, it is the mechanism: one small, reliable touchpoint delivered every month, for years, to the same person, building a habit and a relationship that a single big campaign never could. Via the Do Lectures.

A niche community that keeps its members by mixing video with real conversation. Brain SIG, a Kyoto-based special interest group for language teachers, is running its online neuroscience-of-learning course for the third year running, and the format is worth noting regardless of the subject matter: short, easy videos to work through at your own pace, combined with live sessions where teachers from around the world actually talk to each other and swap stories. The organisers say those live sessions, not the video content, are consistently what participants remember afterwards, which is the same lesson most community builders eventually learn the hard way: content can be consumed alone, but community cannot. Via Brain SIG.

Now, to close, one interesting thing.

ONE INTERESTING THING

Why Is This Interesting's weekend round-up flagged a small but telling trend in property investing: some apartment investors are now using Strava's public running-route data to help decide where to buy, on the theory that neighbourhoods where visibly large numbers of people run tend to be the same neighbourhoods getting safer, more walkable and more desirable before that shows up in the official statistics. It is a nice illustration of how much ordinary exhaust data, in this case people simply logging a jog, now carries information that used to require a proper survey to extract.
