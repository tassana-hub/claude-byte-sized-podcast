Section four, policy and geopolitics.

The Nvidia espionage story has moved on from scandal to evidence. International Intrigue has laid out the four biggest public red flags around Alice Huang, the former executive linked to Jensen Huang: an early career at state-owned employers who do not remember her, a sudden leap to running Megaspeed, which bought five to seven billion dollars of restricted Nvidia chips, a reshuffle of ownership into a new Singapore entity as American investigators closed in, and her disappearance since mid twenty twenty-five. American investigators found around fifty thousand of Megaspeed's chips had vanished, and Singapore has confirmed it is investigating the company. Intrigue's point is that modern intelligence work is a spectrum, from trained officer to useful contact, rather than a simple spy or not spy question. Via International Intrigue.

Via International Intrigue, and The Rundown A I.

Now, over to community, events and marketing.

Section five, community, events and marketing.

One idea worth stealing, from Sifted: the creator in residence. As audiences tire of A I-generated content, companies are bringing a trusted storyteller in-house to become a face of the brand, rather than paying creators post by post. Sifted points to Marks and Spencer Food, where a product development director's behind-the-scenes videos about new flavours have won the brand a younger audience. One survey it cites found seventy-eight per cent of people think a brand becomes cringey when it leans on A I too much. As one Sifted editor puts it, content is no longer scarce; trust is. Via Sifted.

And from Skift, a reframe for anyone who runs events or markets a place: more and more trips start with something to do, not somewhere to go. Live Nation research found seven in ten surveyed fans plan trips around events in places they have never visited, and that around sixty per cent of trip spending happens outside the ticket and the venue. The takeaway for organisers is to get into the purchase path straight after someone commits, with nearby places to stay, eat and explore, rather than waiting for them to plan the rest on their own. Via Skift.

Now, over to one interesting thing.

Section six, one interesting thing.

Adam Mastroianni at Experimental History argues that before launching any big social programme, we should sacrifice a goat, not for the gods, but as a ritual reminder of how little we know. His list of well-meant interventions that did nothing is long: three billion dollars of American school improvement grants, a decade of One Laptop Per Child in Peru, and a hundred million dollar gift that made New York University's medical school free yet changed neither who applied nor which specialties they chose. His diagnosis is an illusion of control: we are so sure our good intentions will work that we often never check, so the cure is not giving up, but humility and testing. Via Experimental History.
