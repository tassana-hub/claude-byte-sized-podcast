Good morning, and welcome to Byte-sized for Wednesday the fourth of November. Six sections today, and because this edition covers the days since our last episode, we have a little more ground to cover.

Section one, Japan and Asia-Pacific.

First, a ransomware attack that has put one of Japan's biggest internet groups on the back foot. The leadership of GMO Internet Group bowed at a news conference on Monday to apologise for a large cyberattack on its cloud services, which hit tens of thousands of users, including a major foreign exchange trading platform. GMO says the attackers demanded fifty-one million dollars and struck after the firm refused to pay. Four senior executives now face temporary pay cuts of twenty to thirty per cent. The knock-on effects are what matter for anyone dealing with Japanese enterprise: because GMO spans hosting, cloud, fintech and artificial intelligence, the blast radius is wide, and its bonds due in twenty thirty-one have already widened by about half a percentage point in spread. The group's chief put it neatly: before, cyberattacks were like a handgun, and now they are a machine gun. Via International Intrigue.

Second, a heist with a regional security angle. The F B I attributes the six billion dollar theft from the crypto exchange Binance last Thursday to North Korea's Lazarus hacking unit. The thieves got into the exchange's signing infrastructure and moved the money out in around twenty minutes, quickly swapping the stablecoins, which they knew would be frozen, for harder-to-track coins. Pyongyang denies it. The pattern is worth noting: roughly one point two billion dollars stolen in twenty twenty-four, one point six five billion in twenty twenty-five, and now six billion in a single day, which is a very efficient way around sanctions. Binance says it will cover the losses, so the wider market has avoided a crash for now. Via International Intrigue.

Now, over to A I and tech.

Section two, A I and tech.

First, a striking piece of research from M I T. It shows that OpenAI's latest model, G P T six Astra, can improve some other A I models by directly rewriting their internal weights, rather than putting them through more training. In the headline test, a small Qwen model scored under eight per cent on maths until Astra changed just over one hundredth of one per cent of its weights to stop it repeating itself, and its score jumped to forty-eight per cent. It did very little for Google's Gemma, which lacked a single obvious flaw. It is one small example, but it is a glimpse of models upgrading, auditing or editing one another. Via The Rundown A I.

Second, OpenAI has been busy in Abu Dhabi. It signed four agreements covering the oil giant ADNOC, the government and the state investor Mubadala. The most practical is a project to turn some Abu Dhabi rules into machine-readable text so A I can speed up permits, starting with food imports. A U A E group is also reportedly set to be the top investor in OpenAI's raise of up to twenty billion dollars, so the customer and the backer may well be the same party. Via The Rundown A I.

Third, a business-model question from T L D R Founders. An essay from the venture firm Andreessen Horowitz argues that A I applications earn their premium through four things together: reliable execution, trusted context, visible proof of results, and pricing that lets customers share in the efficiency gains. Another piece in the same issue is a useful warning for founders: if your promise depends on future model improvements, start with the work customers can finish today, because rivals can buy access to the same model. Via T L D R Founders.

Fourth, the politics of building all this. Around twenty American communities are voting on bans or pauses for data centres in this week's midterm elections, and OpenAI is reportedly preparing to rebrand them as A I compute campuses. A Democratic House could make life harder for A I firms trying to scale. Via The Rundown A I.

Section three, startups and venture.

First, Europe's defence money. British-German missile start-up Hypersonica raised a hundred million euros in a Series B co-led by Lightspeed and General Catalyst. Sifted notes that investors' attention has been dominated by small drones, and long-range strike capability is now the fast-rising theme as Europe tries to reduce its reliance on the United States. Via Sifted.

Second, the funds. Swedish investor Creandum, an early backer of Lovable, has raised seven hundred and fifty million dollars for its eighth and largest fund, amid a rush to back A I start-ups in ever larger early rounds. Sifted also counts one point one billion euros raised by European photonics start-ups this year, more than in the previous two years combined, as the A I boom runs into limits on moving data inside data centres. Via Sifted.

Third, a sharp essay highlighted by T L D R Founders on the McDonaldisation of venture capital. The argument is that venture is splitting between firms chasing exceptional returns and asset managers built to deploy enormous pools of money. The big managers are happy to double or triple their money, so they favour companies that can absorb billions in familiar markets, and that pulls founders towards businesses that fit the next large cheque. Worth bearing in mind when choosing who to raise from. Via T L D R Founders.

Section four, policy and geopolitics.

First, the fresh round of fire between the United States and Iran. The United States says it struck Iran's Qeshm Island in the Strait of Hormuz, reportedly in response to another Iranian attempt on American assets, while Vice President Vance says a deal could be close. International Intrigue's caution is that Vance has an incentive to sound optimistic with record fuel prices weighing on his party before the midterms. Via International Intrigue.

Second, Ukraine. Direct talks on ending the war are now confirmed for Abu Dhabi next week, with parallel talks with Europe on security guarantees for Ukraine. Meanwhile, hackers hit Ukraine's air-raid alert app, and Russia's own warning app was then hit with fake alerts. The point is less panic than eroding trust in official communications. Via International Intrigue.

Third, a trade note. The United States will end duty-free trade access for Rwanda and South Africa from twenty twenty-seven. Officially it is about standard criteria, but the analysis is that it is leverage over Rwanda's role in eastern Congo and South Africa's closeness to BRICS partners. For anyone advising firms with African supply chains, it is a reminder that trade preferences are now openly political. Via International Intrigue.

Now, over to community, events and marketing.

Section five, community, events and marketing.

One idea worth stealing, from Dense Discovery: put the gathering in the calendar and refuse to renegotiate. Writer Elizabeth Oldfield has hosted a shared dinner every other week for over a decade, and about fifteen hundred people have come through the door. It runs on cheap food, guests help to chop, lay the table and clear up, and nobody tries to impress. The backdrop is a figure from Derek Thompson: the share of Americans hosting friends or family at home monthly has fallen from forty-two per cent in nineteen seventy-five to twelve per cent this year. For event and community builders, the lesson is that a fixed rhythm and shared jobs beat a perfect format.

And a second idea, from T L D R Founders, on marketing. An analysis of Similarweb data puts United States email traffic up sixteen per cent in the first eight months of this year, even as organic search sends fewer visitors. The takeaway is to give your audience a reason to keep reading your emails and come back directly. Via Dense Discovery, and T L D R Founders.

Now, one interesting thing to finish.

Section six, one interesting thing.

From Works in Progress, a confession from editor Ben Southwood titled Against supplements. For years he recommended a long list of supplements, using a tidy three-part test: a plausible evolutionary story, a plausible mechanism, and some evidence of benefit. He now takes nothing. His diagnosis is that the method rewarded hunting for confirming evidence, and that he had come to believe around ninety per cent of research is nearly worthless, so that even large piles of nutrition papers can get the direction of an effect wrong. His advice is to supplement only with a specific reason, such as bloodwork or a doctor's advice. Via Works in Progress.
