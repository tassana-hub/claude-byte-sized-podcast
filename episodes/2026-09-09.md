First up, Japan and Asia Pacific.

Japan has made two significant moves on AI governance this week. New guidance says AI tools can be used in legal and judicial work, covering things like literature research, document preparation, and business restructuring, so long as the model isn't built for use in actual disputes such as lawsuits. Alongside that, Japan has adopted a Principles Code on intellectual property protection for generative AI operators: it isn't legally binding, but it's designed to boost transparency, and it applies to overseas companies offering AI systems and services inside Japan too. South Korea has moved in parallel, publishing its own non-binding AI ethics principles under its new AI Basic Act, built around human dignity, the public good, and sustainability, while also encouraging civil servants to write simpler, more AI-friendly reports so government systems can process them more easily. Via CSIS, the Washington based think tank.

Next, a nuclear story with real regional weight. American nuclear firms are courting Southeast Asia, with Thailand lifting its cap on direct clean power deals for data centres and other industries, and India issuing new rules to open its nuclear sector to private investment for the first time. The pitch from US companies is straightforward: as the region electrifies for AI and manufacturing, whoever supplies the power gets the long-term relationship. Via CSIS's nuclear policy briefing.

And a number worth sitting with: China's state research and development spending has now crossed one trillion dollars a year, a milestone one geopolitical analysis calls the end of the West's technological monopoly. It lands the same week as a separate briefing showing China's AI computing capacity surged a hundred and seventy seven percent year on year, with over five hundred billion dollars in planned infrastructure spending through 2030. Via Geopolitical Monitor and This Week in Startups.

And finally, a small but telling data point out of Japan. Readers of one geopolitics newsletter were asked whether America's Treasury Secretary was overstepping by publicly pushing the Bank of Japan to raise interest rates, and three quarters said yes, calling it a matter of central bank independence, while most of the rest argued that what happens to Japanese rates ripples through markets everywhere regardless. It's a small poll, but it captures a live tension: Washington leaning on Tokyo's monetary policy just as Japan tries to normalise rates on its own timeline. Via International Intrigue.

Now, over to AI and tech.

Anthropic has signed five hundred and seventeen billion dollars in compute agreements over the past eleven months, adding up to nearly fifteen gigawatts of capacity, mostly with Google and Amazon Web Services, plus a forty five billion dollar deal with the cloud provider Nscale. That is an enormous number even by this industry's standards, and it lands the same month the company confidentially filed for an initial public offering with the Securities and Exchange Commission, suggesting the compute build-out and the IPO timeline are now closely linked. Via TLDR.

OpenAI, meanwhile, is preparing to launch its own version of managed agents at its DevDay conference this year, following the model Anthropic already offers. The pitch is advanced models with strong computer-use skills at competitive prices, and OpenAI is reportedly also eyeing agent-driven advertising, which could put it in more direct competition with Meta and Google. Via TLDR.

Inside OpenAI itself, a new report shows just how much of its research is now being done by its own coding agents, logging the equivalent of three working days for every one a human puts in. The company says it's hit the internal target its chief executive set last year for an "automated research intern," with the typical researcher now burning through more than six hundred dollars a day in agent usage, and some well over seven thousand. Via The Rundown.

And a reality check on public sentiment: a new poll of over seven thousand American adults finds seventy percent are more worried than excited about AI, a rare point of agreement across party lines. Concern about data centres, job losses, and AI in schools runs high, and eighty one percent think Washington's current rules fall short, though voters don't particularly trust either party to fix that either. Via The Rundown.

Now, over to startups and venture.

French AI developer Mistral has raised a three billion euro Series D round led by Samsung, valuing the company at roughly twenty one billion euros, a sign that Gulf and Asian strategic investors are increasingly willing to write the biggest cheques in European AI. Via This Week in Startups.

Reusable rocket startup Stoke Space has closed the first tranche of a billion dollar Series E as it works toward reaching orbit and preparing a larger rocket for operations, though no lead investor has been named publicly. Via This Week in Startups.

And a sobering data point from Europe: fintech's share of the Nordic region's hundred fastest-growing startups has fallen from over a quarter two years ago to just fifteen percent this year, echoing a broader European fintech funding slump. Across the whole continent, only six venture-backed fintech exits have ever cleared a billion dollars since 2010, a reminder that a Klarna or Wise-style breakout is much rarer than the funding headlines suggest. Via Sifted.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

Serbia may have just damaged its own bid to join the European Union. Belgrade gave Ratko Mladić, the former Bosnian Serb military commander convicted of genocide at Srebrenica, a lavish state-adjacent funeral this week, complete with a government jet, honour guard, and live broadcast on state television. Brussels was furious: the EU's enlargement commissioner cancelled a planned visit and called the spectacle incompatible with European values. President Aleksandar Vučić is walking a tightrope ahead of next month's elections, trying to court nationalist voters without fully abandoning the EU accession process that underpins Serbia's economy. Via International Intrigue.

In Germany, the populist right Alternative for Germany party came first in Sunday's state election in Saxony-Anhalt, roughly doubling its vote share to forty four percent. Chancellor Merz has ruled out resigning, but his own Christian Democrats saw their support cut in half, and polling suggests much of the AfD's growth is coming from voters worried about immigration, security and economic stagnation rather than hardcore ideology, which will pile pressure on Merz to shift rightward. Via International Intrigue.

And on Iran, the International Atomic Energy Agency says it still can't verify Tehran's nuclear material more than a year after losing access to key facilities. France, Germany, the UK and the US are now pushing to refer Iran to the United Nations Security Council over the standoff, and in a separate development, the agency's chief has said a reactor built under the former Assad government in Syria was configured to produce material for possible use in nuclear weapons. Via CSIS's nuclear policy briefing.

Now, over to community, events and marketing.

One idea worth stealing this week: community strategist David Spinks has built what he calls the Community Mixboard, a set of sliders, rather than fixed either-or choices, for designing a community. Things like structure versus spontaneity, daily versus occasional contact, and low versus high barriers to entry all sit on a spectrum, and the trick is choosing where to set each dial deliberately, then moving it again as the community evolves, much like a producer riding the controls on a live mix. He's also careful to note that none of these settings are inherently good or bad, they're just choices, and the real harm comes from setting a dial without being honest with your members about where it's actually pointing. If you're building any kind of member community, it's a useful way to turn vague instincts into an actual design conversation with your team. Via David Spinks.

And a smaller practical note: facilitator Priya Parker went live this week with writer Anand Giridharadas for a session titled The Art of Fighting, focused on how to structure disagreement inside a group so that conflict actually produces something, rather than just producing noise or silence. It's a useful reminder that most communities don't fail because people disagree, they fail because nobody designed a container for the disagreement to happen safely in. Via Priya Parker.

Now, over to one interesting thing.

Economist Alex Tabarrok has a striking piece on what he calls invisible graveyards. After the United States effectively strangled nuclear power with new regulation in the nineteen seventies, construction costs that had been falling for two decades started rising instead, years before the Three Mile Island accident that usually gets the blame. One estimate suggests that if the earlier trend had simply continued, nuclear power could have been a tenth of its actual cost by 2015, potentially avoiding as many as nine and a half million premature deaths from fossil fuel pollution. Tabarrok argues a similar, quieter story has played out with genetically engineered microbes, regulated since the nineteen eighties under a law written for industrial chemicals, which has likely cost us bacteria that could detect buried landmines or extract rare earth metals. The lesson he draws is blunt: when you never let something get built, you never find out what you lost.
