JAPAN AND ASIA PACIFIC

We start in Tokyo, where US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has spent the week publicly leaning on the Bank of Japan's governor, Kazuo Ueda, to raise interest rates. Bessent has all but declared the era of Japan's ultra loose monetary policy over, and even claimed to have information the market doesn't. Behind the pressure sit two American worries: Japan's rock bottom rates have made the yen a cheap source of borrowed money that global investors use to buy assets elsewhere, and Tokyo holds more than one trillion dollars in US government bonds that it might have to sell off to defend its own currency. A joint intervention back in July barely moved the yen, which is sitting near forty year lows, so attention now turns to the Bank of Japan's policy meeting in the middle of this month. Via International Intrigue.

Further south, an unusually intense El Nino weather pattern is choking Southeast Asia. Peatland fires in Indonesia hit nearly ten thousand hotspots in August, and the resulting haze has spread across Malaysia and, for the first time since twenty fifteen, into the Philippines, where officials in Manila suspended schools as air quality turned acutely unhealthy. Separately, a string of tropical cyclones has brought monsoon flooding that has killed more than thirty people and caused roughly ninety eight million dollars in damage, and forecasters expect both the haze and the El Nino pattern itself to keep intensifying into the final months of the year. Via CSIS.

And a shorter one on chips. Chinese memory chipmaker YMTC is looking to raise close to five billion dollars in a Shanghai listing, following a strong debut from its rival CXMT. It is a reminder that China's semiconductor industry keeps finding fresh routes to capital at home, even while it remains largely cut off from Western chipmaking equipment. Via International Intrigue.

Now, over to AI and tech.

AI AND TECH

The big story is a fresh round of model launches. Meta released Muse Spark one point three, which Mark Zuckerberg pitched as frontier level performance that is almost too cheap to meter, while Google shipped Gemini three point eight Flash with meaningfully better coding, reasoning and agentic performance at the same price as its predecessor. Independent benchmarks now put Meta's model behind only Anthropic's latest releases despite its far lower running cost, and Zuckerberg has already teased a larger model in the pipeline, while Google's own AI chief admitted Gemini still sits a little below the frontier. Via TLDR and The Rundown.

Inside Meta, meanwhile, a smaller but telling shift: the company is moving its internal communications from Google Chat over to Slack, with AI chief Alexandr Wang arguing that Slack's conversational interface and developer tooling are simply better suited to AI agents. It is a small sign of a bigger trend, where workplace chat is becoming the control layer through which both employees and AI agents interact with company systems. Via TLDR.

And a warning from the hardware side. A security researcher found two separate flaws in Unitree's G1 humanoid robot that let an attacker within Bluetooth range seize full remote control of the machine, one through a poorly verified encryption key and another through a file upload bug in the robot's own AI service. As humanoid robots start moving out of labs and into workplaces, it is a pointed reminder that they inherit all the usual software security headaches, just with much higher physical stakes. Via TLDR.

There is also a more unsettling story about how far ahead the frontier labs are willing to look. OpenAI's next model, codenamed Astra, reportedly uses a technique where the model reasons over the same text in repeated loops before answering, squeezing more intelligence out without making the model physically larger. The catch is that some of that looped reasoning comes out as pure mathematics rather than readable text, making it harder for safety teams to monitor what the model is actually thinking, and OpenAI's own chief scientist has said he wants to avoid a race toward reasoning nobody can inspect at all. The company says it has dialled the technique back so Astra still shows its work for now, but the underlying tension, between squeezing out more performance and keeping models legible to their own creators, is not going away. Via The Rundown.

Now, over to startups and venture.

STARTUPS AND VENTURE

The headline funding story belongs to Mistral. The French AI startup is closing in on an oversubscribed three billion euro Series D, led by Samsung, with Nvidia, ASML and the European Union's own Scaleup Fund also putting money in. That is nearly double the one point seven billion euros Mistral raised just a year ago, and it confirms that Europe's most prominent AI challenger can still pull in serious strategic capital from both chipmakers and sovereign backers. Via Sifted.

Elsewhere, prediction market Kalshi has signed a multi-year deal to become the exclusive prediction market partner of the US Open, complete with branding, live market data and an integrity framework built with the tennis authorities to keep betting away from umpire calls and injuries. Critics argue the tie-up accelerates an uncomfortable fusion of professional tennis and gambling, but it also shows how quickly prediction markets are pushing into mainstream sport. Via TLDR.

And a founder story worth stealing. When Vox Media laid off sports writer Matthew Brown in twenty twenty, he launched a newsletter about the business side of college sports within seventy two hours. Extra Points now brings in more than twenty five thousand dollars a month, built on subscriptions, advertising and a research library of sports contracts and budgets, by deliberately staying small and deep rather than chasing pageviews. Via Indie Hackers.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

POLICY AND GEOPOLITICS

On the security side, fresh analysis argues that South Korea should revive stalled nuclear talks with Washington over enrichment and reprocessing rights by leaning on its energy security needs and its strategic value as an ally, while a separate report calls for tighter joint US and South Korean nuclear planning as North Korea continues to modernise its own arsenal. Both point to the same underlying dilemma facing policymakers: how far to extend the American nuclear umbrella over Asian allies without accelerating the very proliferation everyone claims to want to avoid. Via CSIS.

And a more optimistic read on AI's economic fallout. Economist Alex Tabarrok argues that even a scenario where artificial intelligence sharply cuts labour's share of income need not be a disaster for workers, because what matters is the size of the whole economic pie, not just labour's slice of it. His modelling finds that even in an extreme scenario, where labour's share falls to under a quarter of GDP but growth accelerates to around ten percent a year, keeping total labour income on its current path would need a transfer worth only about five percent of GDP, comparable to what payroll taxes already raise. The bigger point for policymakers, he argues, is that labour's share on its own tells you very little about how workers actually fare, without also knowing how much bigger the whole economy has become. Via Marginal Revolution.

And a smaller signal of the same anxious mood among allies. The Dutch central bank has shifted eighty six tonnes of gold out of New York and Ottawa to London, citing what it called increasing geopolitical unrest. Most of the transfer was done through sales and repurchases rather than physically shipping bullion, but the underlying message is hard to miss: after a run of unpredictable American electoral cycles and weaponised sanctions, some European central banks are quietly hedging their bets on the United States as an automatically safe place to keep their reserves. Via International Intrigue.

Now, over to community, events and marketing.

COMMUNITY, EVENTS AND MARKETING

One idea worth stealing this week is about the parts of a community nobody can see. Community builder Jon Levesque argues that the real work of any community happens in private group chats rather than public channels, where a small trusted circle can get things done quickly, but where that same lack of visibility makes it easy for power to be abused unnoticed. His practical takeaway is that anyone relying on private groups needs to deliberately build in checks, rotation and transparency, rather than assuming trust will simply take care of itself. Via Community Inc.

A second one on pricing. When the Gmail-based customer relationship tool Streak first found paying interest from Uber, its founder picked a number on the spot, got an immediate yes, and then simply kept adding ten dollars a month with every new customer until somebody finally pushed back. Streak now makes more than eight hundred thousand dollars a month, a reminder that most founders and marketers stop testing their pricing far too early. Via Indie Hackers.

Now, for one interesting thing to close on.

ONE INTERESTING THING

The team at Why Is This Interesting make the case that most of us effectively wake up twice: once groggy and anxious the moment the alarm goes off, and a second time, properly ourselves, once we have showered, moved, and had some music or conversation in our ears. Their small provocation is to treat the gap between those two beginnings as something worth shrinking on purpose, whether through a favourite album, a walk, or simply talking to someone, rather than assuming the fog will lift in its own time. Via Why Is This Interesting.
