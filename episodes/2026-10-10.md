Good morning, and welcome to Byte-sized for Saturday the tenth of October. Six sections today. This one covers everything since Wednesday's edition, so there is a little more to get through.

Section one, Japan and Asia Pacific.

First, Japan's beer makers. The country's fair trade watchdog has raided all four of the major brewers over suspicions that they ran a long-standing price cartel, keeping wholesale prices artificially high. At a time when Japanese households are grappling with persistent inflation after decades of flat prices, it sends a message to a famously tight-knit corporate world that business as usual may no longer be tolerated. Via International Intrigue.

Second, Taiwan's chip champion. TSMC reported September revenue of more than five hundred and eleven billion Taiwan dollars, up about fifty five percent on last year, driven by demand for advanced AI chips from customers such as Nvidia and Apple. It has also committed to using ASML's most advanced lithography machines. Anyone watching Japan's own chip revival, with its fabs in Kumamoto, is watching this number. Via TLDR.

Third, Asia's tourism arithmetic. Skift's monthly Asia round-up says the region is finally counting the receipts rather than the arrivals. Sri Lanka wants three million visitors, but would clearly prefer generous ones. Chinese Golden Week travellers went further afield while spending less on rooms, and Hilton chose Japan for the debut of its compact mid-market Motto brand. Hotel groups are following the money market by market, rather than with one regional template. Via Skift.

And fourth, a longer view. Tomas Pueyo's final instalment in his eight part series on Japan asks why the country went from industrial latecomer to the world's second richest economy, and then stagnated. His argument is that the same geography and social structure that powered the rise, dense cities, high literacy and a unified island economy, later became the constraint. It is a useful frame for anyone explaining Japan to a newcomer. Via Uncharted Territories.

Now, over to artificial intelligence and technology.

Section two, AI and tech.

The headline is speed. OpenAI has launched an Ultrafast mode for its latest GPT-6.1 model across the API, Codex and ChatGPT Work, promising up to eight times the speed of the standard mode with close to top-tier intelligence. It is priced at a premium, so the question for builders is which workflows are worth paying extra to finish faster. Via TLDR.

Second, Anthropic has released Claude Haiku 5.5, its fastest and cheapest small model, aimed at high-volume, cost-sensitive work. The Rundown suggests handing routine tasks to it and keeping the larger models for the hard thinking. In the same week Anthropic cut the price of cached reads on Sonnet 5.5 in half, and launched a cyber mission offering free vulnerability scanning for open source projects. Via TLDR and The Rundown.

Third, Google has unveiled a universal Gemini agent for knowledge work, media creation, coding and multi-step workflows across Workspace and other enterprise tools, with governance and cost controls built in. The enterprise agent race is now about management features as much as model quality. Via TLDR.

And fourth, a dose of reality on OpenAI. The company has told investors its annualised revenue is approaching fifty billion dollars, around twenty billion less than a figure reported only a week earlier. The gap appears to come down to accounting: Anthropic counts sales through its cloud partners, and OpenAI does not. Meanwhile three fired OpenAI safety researchers have gone public, arguing the dismissals chill internal dissent and could let the company avoid embedding outside auditors. OpenAI says the firings were about misconduct, not about raising concerns. Via TLDR and The Rundown.

Now, over to startups and venture.

Section three, startups and venture.

First, Europe's sovereign AI push. Aleph Alpha, recently merged with Canada's Cohere, released an open weight model called Kolibri aimed at regulated sectors, and Mistral followed with an upgrade it calls Le Chonk. Investors are excited that Europe now has more than one credible home-grown lab, though some warn against pitting the two against each other when the market is big enough for several. Via Sifted.

Second, what limited partners want. At a conference for female investors in Barcelona, LPs said fundraising is tougher than ever and liquidity is tightening. Their green flags: a thesis you can state in one sentence, and proactive honesty about team changes. Their red flags: claiming returns you have not realised, a deck with no numbers, and shopping around inside the same LP when one partner has already said no. Via Sifted.

And third, a large round in agents. Valon raised one hundred and fifty million dollars at a valuation of two point three billion to apply AI agents across mortgage servicing, while another start-up, Hone, founded by former staff of fast-growing AI firms, raised sixty million dollars in seed funding to build agents that run parts of a business over weeks or months. Via TLDR.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

Section four, policy and geopolitics.

First, the Trump and Xi summit. The CSIS Trade Guys unpack what came out of it, along with the US Trade Representative's meeting of G20 trade ministers in Milwaukee, and why the European Union is pressing China to limit exports of hybrid vehicles. For anyone advising on supply chains, the EU and China front is the one to watch. Via CSIS.

Second, shipping and the Middle East. Trading giant Trafigura reportedly paid seventy six million dollars to charter a single tanker from the US Gulf Coast to China, roughly ten times the pre-war rate, while Houthi missiles and drones hit airports in Riyadh and Abha. Energy importers in Asia, Japan included, should expect freight costs to stay painful. Via International Intrigue.

And third, undersea cables. CSIS has published research on China's use of dual-use research vessels for seabed data collection, with an interactive map of cable routes. It is a pointed reminder of why subsea infrastructure keeps appearing in Asia-Pacific security conversations. Via CSIS.

Now, over to community, events and marketing.

Section five, community, events and marketing. One idea worth stealing.

From Priya Parker, a community joiner fair. A library in Michigan hosted a simple event where local clubs and organisations set up tables so people could find something to join. More than three hundred came the first year, and this year doctors and therapists have been recommending it to patients. The takeaway: if your community is lonely, do not launch another programme, host a place where people can meet the groups that already exist. She pairs it with Sherry Turkle's argument that chatbots come after our capacity for attachment, and her simple advice: pick one thing you do alone, and invite a person to do it with you. Via Priya Parker's Group Life.

Now, one interesting thing.

Section six, one interesting thing. Works in Progress asks why Victorian Britain's elite, who rose late, drank a lot and spent months of the year at balls and dinners, ran the richest country on earth. The author's suggestion is that all that socialising was the work: it built a single tight-knit community across class and religion, which meant politicians accepted unpaid, prestigious public service and rivals rarely tried to wreck the country to beat each other. It is not an argument for cold baths or against them, just a reminder that there is more than one way to run a place. Via Works in Progress.
