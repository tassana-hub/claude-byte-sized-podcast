Good morning, and welcome to Byte-sized for Monday the sixteenth of November. A quieter weekend's post, so a shorter episode today, across six sections, starting as always in Japan and the Asia Pacific.

Section one, Japan and Asia Pacific.

First, China's rare earth squeeze, one year after the Busan deal. New research from C S I S finds that, despite the promises made when Washington and Beijing met in Busan last year, Chinese rare earth exports to the United States have not recovered. Between June twenty twenty-four and March twenty twenty-five, America imported an average of almost seventy-seven tonnes a month of rare earth compounds and metals from China. Over the past eleven months, that average has fallen to around nineteen tonnes, a drop of almost seventy-five per cent. For Japan, which learned this lesson the hard way in twenty ten and has spent fifteen years diversifying its supply through Australia and recycling, this is vindication of that strategy, and a reminder that the leverage has not gone away. Expect rare earths to come up in any economic security conversation around the A P E C summit later this month. Via C S I S.

Second, the missile production gap. Another C S I S study tracks how production of Patriot air defence missiles has doubled since the war in Ukraine began, from around three hundred a year to around six hundred, with a goal of two thousand a year by twenty thirty. Before the war, half of that output went to the United States and half to allies. This matters in Tokyo because Mitsubishi Heavy Industries builds Patriot interceptors under licence, and Japan has already loosened its export rules to send some back to American stockpiles. If Washington is serious about that twenty thirty target, Japanese defence manufacturing becomes part of the answer, and the debate about Japan's role as an arms supplier will only get louder. Via C S I S.

And one item from closer to home in education. The Mind, Brain and Education group of the Japan Association for Language Teaching is collecting short pieces for its January issue on what schools should teach but do not: how to spot a scam, check a political claim, fill in an online form, or stand up for yourself. The sample piece comes from Curtis Kelly, who designed an English content course at Osaka Gakuin University called The Psychology of Love. It became so popular that students made T-shirts, built a website and kept meeting after class, and he argues their enthusiasm drove deeper language learning. Submissions are open until the fifteenth of December, and anything from a single paragraph to a thousand words is welcome. Via the J A L T Brain S I G.

Now, over to A I and tech.

Section two, A I and tech.

First, the case for artisanal A I. Every's Sunday edition argues that when everyone uses the same handful of models, the default answer becomes cheap and your own judgement becomes the scarce thing. Two pieces of research point the same way. The lab Goodfire showed that most neurons inside one family of open models do one or a few understandable jobs, and that A I agents can predict when they fire with about ninety-five per cent accuracy. Separately, researchers at M I T showed that a frontier model can rewrite a smaller model's weights directly, without a training run, and a small company has already released a Mac tool that does this for between five and ten dollars of compute per model. Every's conclusion is that models designed around your taste, rather than grown from generic data, are perhaps a year or two from the mainstream. Via Every.

Second, the latest verdicts on the new models. Every's testers gave Anthropic's Fable five point two their top rating, six of seven testers calling it the best they have used for long and ambitious builds, able to work unattended overnight or across a weekend. The caveat is that it is slow and expensive, and that the highest effort setting is not always best: one tester found that the high setting beat the maximum on anything needing taste. Google's Gemini four Carbon, meanwhile, is a much stronger coder than its predecessor but does not know when to stop. Told to pick off only the clearest tickets in a backlog, it ran for more than four hours until someone stopped it. The practical advice is to give it one large job with a clear finish line, and to check what it touched afterwards. Via Every.

Third, how Meta built Muse. Lenny Rachitsky interviewed Mona Sarantakos, who has led product on Meta's Muse assistant from day one. Muse is now the number one app in the App Store and arguably the fastest-growing consumer A I product ever. It began as a weekend experiment running the OpenClaw agent on a Raspberry Pi, and the team deliberately waited seven months to launch, despite starting before most competitors. They now use swarms of agents to sift user feedback at scale. The lesson for product teams is that being first to start does not require being first to ship, if the extra months go into getting activation right. Via Lenny's Newsletter.

And a sober counterpoint from the economists. Tyler Cowen highlights a working paper by Charles Jones and Christopher Tonetti on what they call a weak links economy, where tasks depend on each other. In that world, automating a slice of the economy brings gains slowly, because output is held back by the slowest tasks. But the same dependence makes attacks far more damaging: in their example, a tenfold productivity gain in tasks worth one per cent of output raises total output by less than one per cent, while a tenfold collapse in those same tasks cuts it by sixty-eight per cent. Their conclusion is that the costs and benefits of A I can be extremely lopsided, and that markets may overinvest. Via Marginal Revolution.

Now, over to startups and venture.

Section three, startups and venture.

First, free money, carefully counted. The V C Corner went through forty-two startup programmes and verified the terms on thirty of them, which together add up to almost one point four million dollars in credits and plan value. Two thirds of that is cloud: Google and Cloudflare offer up to three hundred and fifty thousand dollars each, and Amazon Web Services up to two hundred thousand. The labs are the stingiest: Anthropic's startup cohort is currently full, and OpenAI routes its credits through partner venture firms. A realistic eighteen-month-old A I company with a three million dollar seed round can stack around seven hundred and fifteen thousand dollars, by choosing one tool per job. The warning is that credits on two clouds only become runway if you genuinely run on both. Via The V C Corner.

Second, what one airline turnaround teaches about change programmes. Skift reviewed two years of American Airlines earnings calls and counted more than fifty separate initiatives to close the gap with Delta and United, from seatback screens to fixing its Dallas hub schedule. Customer satisfaction is improving on every measure, but frontline staff are unconvinced: pilots point to a profitability gap of three to four billion dollars and profit sharing of only a few hundred dollars a head, against thousands at rivals. The lesson for any founder or executive is that fifty visible improvements do not add up to a strategy if the people delivering them cannot see the payoff. Via Skift.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

Section four, policy and geopolitics.

First, America's air traffic scare. On Friday the Federal Aviation Administration grounded every domestic departure in the United States for more than four hours, only the third nationwide ground stop in twenty-five years. The Transportation Secretary blamed ageing equipment, saying switches and routers had been overloaded with data. The Homeland Security Secretary said there was no evidence of a cyber attack, but notably would not rule out a rogue A I agent. Marginal Revolution used the moment to renew the case for privatising air traffic control, as Canada and others have done. For anyone talking to government about critical infrastructure, it is a fresh, concrete example of how fragile legacy systems have become. Via Skift, and Marginal Revolution.

Second, the voters are paying attention. A C S I S survey across seven American battleground states found that sixty-two per cent of people believe events abroad affect their daily lives a great deal or a fair amount, mostly through the cost of living, energy prices and tariffs. The same institute notes that ports carry most of America's overseas trade, contribute nearly two point six trillion dollars to its economy and support one in every eight jobs. Put together, the message is that trade and supply chains are no longer abstract foreign policy for American voters, which shapes what Washington will ask of partners such as Japan. Via C S I S.

Now, over to community, events and marketing.

Section five, community, events and marketing.

The idea worth stealing comes from Every's list of practical habits: turn your corrections into checks. Next time you repeat a task with A I, whether that is an event description, a newsletter or a sponsor email, paste in the output, say what you liked and what you did not, and ask the model to turn each comment into a simple yes or no test. Then use those tests to grade every future draft. For community managers who write the same kinds of announcements every week, it is a cheap way to make your house style stick. A second tip from the same source: when you are correcting drafts, ask the A I to name one pattern in your edits and one exception it cannot explain, and only explain that exception. Via Every.

And a smaller one from the DO Lectures: Jonathan Berry's film for World Kindness Day is built around a single idea, the small moments someone may not remember giving but you never forget receiving. It is a neat prompt for any community event: invite people to share one of those moments, and you have an opening round that gets everyone talking. Via the DO Lectures.

Now, over to one interesting thing.

Section six, one interesting thing.

Byrne Hobart points to a classic paper by Richard Radford, an economics student captured in nineteen forty-two who spent the rest of the war in prisoner of war camps. With everyone receiving identical Red Cross parcels and almost no productive work, the camps became a laboratory for pure trade. Prisoners settled on cigarettes as currency, suffered booms and busts, and found arbitrage everywhere, such as one prisoner who bought meat from Sikh prisoners in exchange for butter and jam from fellow English speakers. Hobart's point is that the modern economy is not so different: producing the basics takes very little labour, so most of our cleverness goes into allocation. Via The Diff.
