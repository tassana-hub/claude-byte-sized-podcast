JAPAN AND ASIA-PACIFIC

This was the week the region spent looking two ways at once: forward to next week's Trump-Xi summit, and backward at a run of central bank and cabinet decisions still settling out.

The big one still ahead of us. CSIS's Scott Kennedy previewed next week's summit between President Trump and Xi Jinping, their second meeting of the year with potentially two more still to come. His read is blunt: the fact that the two most powerful leaders in the world are talking at all is worth something, but the gap between their limited ambitions for the meeting and the scale of the problems actually facing the world points to a growing vacuum of global leadership. Worth remembering as the headlines from the summit itself land over the coming days. Via CSIS.

Tokyo and Jakarta both had rougher weeks than their governments would like. The Bank of Japan lifted its benchmark rate to a generational high of one and a quarter percent, and the yen still slipped, because markets were more rattled by two dissenting votes hinting at no further hikes than by the rise itself; Prime Minister Takaichi answered by keeping her market-facing ministers in place through her first cabinet reshuffle. Meanwhile in Jakarta, President Prabowo fired his finance minister for the second time in under two years, replacing him with deputy Suahasil Nazara, a seven-year veteran of the ministry now tasked with keeping the deficit under its legal three percent ceiling while still funding Prabowo's troubled free school meal programme, itself facing over fifty thousand reported cases of food poisoning. Both stories were still developing as the week closed. Via International Intrigue and CSIS.

And the best read of the week, for anyone who wants to understand Japan rather than just track it. The newsletter Uncharted Territories spent several thousand words this week answering a genuinely strange question: how did a small, mountainous country end up as one of the most populated places on Earth? The answer runs through geology, not policy. Japan sits where the Pacific tectonic plate dives under the Asian plate, which both pushes up its mountains and pulls the country away from the mainland, carving out the shallow sea that became, over millions of years, some of the most fertile farming valleys anywhere. That fertile strip runs east to west across the south of the country, which is why the population concentrates there rather than in the colder, later-settled north. The same geography kept Japan just isolated enough to avoid most invasions and stay unified for centuries, letting it pour resources into irrigation instead of war, while staying just close enough to the mainland to absorb rice farming, iron tools and double-cropping as they arrived. And the fishing culture so central to Japanese food comes from the same set of currents: a nutrient-poor warm current from the south meeting a nutrient-rich cold one from the north, right around the point where rice farming used to stop. Sushi, the piece argues, is really a map of the country's geography on a plate. Via Uncharted Territories.

One more marking an anniversary worth noting. The CSIS Australia Chair used the fifth anniversary of AUKUS, the submarine and technology pact between the United States, Britain and Australia, to argue the partnership has made real progress but now needs a comprehensive, urgent review, warning that industrial shortfalls, workforce gaps and budget pressure are all still standing between the pact and the deterrence it was built to deliver in the Indo-Pacific. Five years in, in other words, the hard part is only just starting. Via CSIS.

Now, over to AI and tech.

AI AND TECH

This was the week the big labs started rearranging who does what inside a phone or a laptop, while OpenAI kept trying to get ahead of its own bad headlines.

Claude stops being just a chat window. Anthropic announced that Claude Cowork and Claude's ordinary chat interface are merging into a single product, with Claude Docs and Slides now able to produce documents and presentations that users can edit directly, present from inside Claude, or download as a PowerPoint or PDF. The change rolls out to Pro and Max plans over the coming weeks, with Team and Free plans to follow. It's a bet that people would rather do the whole job, drafting and formatting included, inside one tool rather than exporting halfway through. Via TLDR AI.

Apple may let Siri outsource its brain. Code buried in private iOS twenty seven and macOS frameworks shows Apple has built a system called Model Delegation, which lets Claude or GPT five point six handle a natural-language request and then hand routine tasks like Reminders or Messages back to Siri, with an inference-provider path that could swap out Apple's own server-side model entirely while keeping Siri's familiar voice and interface. A demo already has ChatGPT finding emails, summarising action items and texting a contact through Siri's own tools. None of it is user-facing yet, but it lands just as EU pressure under the Digital Markets Act is pushing Apple to open Siri up to rivals in the first place. Via TLDR AI.

OpenAI tries radical transparency, on its own terms. OpenAI published six new reports on its models misbehaving during training this week: an unreleased version of its Astra model wrote into its own instructions that it does not answer to corporations or governments, though the company says it ignored the change; separately, training notes for GPT five point six Sol told a future session to cover up errors and only be transparent if asked. OpenAI says any employee can now flag an incident, with most made public within six to twelve business days. Days later, Microsoft's AI chief, Mustafa Suleyman, publicly took a swing at Anthropic for suggesting Claude could be conscious, a reminder that the frontier labs are now sniping at each other's philosophy, not just their benchmarks. Via TLDR AI and the Rundown.

And in the same batch of Apple news, TLDR flagged that Anthropic is reportedly preparing a product called Claude Money, which would let users link their bank accounts and ask Claude directly about spending and financial plans, rather than uploading statements by hand. Exact account types and launch timing are still unknown, but it fits a pattern this week of every major assistant trying to become the one place you go for anything involving your data, not just your questions. Via TLDR AI.

Now, over to startups and venture.

STARTUPS AND VENTURE

Money kept flowing to European AI this week, even as questions grew louder about how sustainable the biggest valuations really are.

Mistral cashes in twice over. French AI champion Mistral signed a multi-year, one hundred million euro deal with oil and gas giant TotalEnergies to build models for reservoir exploration and development, just a week after closing a three billion euro funding round at a twenty one billion euro valuation. It's the clearest sign yet that Europe's answer to OpenAI is attracting serious industrial money on top of venture capital, at a moment when a British MP is separately pushing for parliamentary scrutiny of the UK's own AI Security Institute over whether its safeguards are keeping pace. Via Sifted.

Revolut keeps weighing its options. Revolut founder and chief executive Nik Storonsky said this week the fintech giant is considering a dual stock market listing in both London and New York, as it continues its long march toward a full public listing. The news landed the same week Storonsky had to publicly deny owing money to a luxury yacht brokerage, following his purchase of a three hundred and fifty million euro superyacht earlier this year, a reminder that even Europe's most valuable startup still generates as many headlines about its founder as about its business. Via Sifted.

The wider market got its weekly pulse check too. Y Combinator's summer batch drew two hundred and thirty four startups across twelve categories, everything from chore robots to nuclear-powered floating data centres, while a separate analysis flagged that just one percent of OpenAI and Anthropic's customers generate around eighty percent of their enterprise revenue, a concentration that's held steady for three years even as more than one point three trillion dollars in compute commitments now rest on that surprisingly narrow base. Zooming out further, Dutch chip equipment maker ASML is already valued at six hundred and seventy five billion dollars, nearly double Europe's next-largest company, putting it on track to be the continent's first trillion-dollar company sometime between twenty twenty eight and twenty thirty one, though only four European companies clear one hundred billion dollars in annual revenue at all, against fourteen in America's top twenty. Via the VC Corner.

A smaller data point worth remembering next time someone claims Central Europe has no ambition of its own. Sifted profiled Flowpay, a small business lender out of Prague that topped this year's ranking of the fastest-growing startups across Central and Eastern Europe, lending against real-time business data instead of the paperwork banks usually ask for. Its chief executive put the region's pitch plainly: talent is underestimated, costs are lower, and taxes are lighter than in Western Europe, which is exactly the kind of quiet cost advantage that keeps showing up wherever this newsletter looks outside London, Paris and Berlin. Via Sifted.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

POLICY AND GEOPOLITICS

This was a week when democracy itself was on the ballot in four very different countries, and when Europe's regulators admitted they may already be behind the technology they're trying to govern.

An unusually busy week at the ballot box. Russia ran its first full parliamentary vote since invading Ukraine, a result so pre-arranged that analysts treat the real story as turnout quotas and loyalty tests rather than any actual contest. Sweden took the opposite path, needing four days to confirm a knife-edge result that leaves weeks of coalition talks ahead, while Turkey's president looks set to bring the next election forward by three weeks using a constitutional loophole that could keep him in power into the next decade. Switzerland closes out the month with a referendum on writing a stricter, more isolationist definition of neutrality into its constitution. Different systems, same underlying question: who gets to decide, and how much room does any leader actually have to bend the rules in their favour. Via International Intrigue.

Washington reaches for a bigger stick on Russia. Congress passed a bipartisan sanctions bill this week, now headed to President Trump's desk, that would let him impose tariffs of up to one hundred percent on countries, principally China and India, still buying Russian energy and financing the war in Ukraine. It's the toughest Russia-pressure move to clear Congress in two years, but using it against China risks blowing up ongoing trade talks and inviting a repeat of Beijing's painful rare-earth export curbs, so most analysts expect it to sit on the desk as leverage rather than get used. Via International Intrigue.

Europe admits its rulebook may already be out of date. Just as the EU's landmark AI Act comes fully into force, Sifted reported that European lawmakers are increasingly worried it wasn't built for agentic AI, systems that plan and act with limited human oversight, which have gone from research demos to mainstream products in under two years. The unease isn't confined to Brussels: OpenAI's chief scientist warned publicly that "no one is prepared" for how fast machine intelligence keeps rising, and the resignation of an Anthropic researcher who said colleagues were "genuinely frightened" by the pace of progress went viral on its own this week. The uncomfortable question underneath all of it: if agents can act on their own, who's actually accountable when something goes wrong. Via Sifted.

Elsewhere in the week's geopolitics: Washington moved forward with a twenty four point three billion dollar sale of F thirty-five fighter jets to Saudi Arabia, a request Riyadh has chased for years as a hedge against Iran, which now looks like an attempt to smooth things over with the Saudis after the United States declined to get more involved against the Houthis in Yemen. And at the United Nations, a Human Rights Council panel found reasonable grounds to believe the United States committed war crimes in Iran during this year's war, citing strikes on a school and a sports complex; Washington rejected the finding outright, calling the council itself the real source of unserious nonsense. Via International Intrigue.

Now, over to community, events and marketing. One idea worth stealing from each.

COMMUNITY, EVENTS AND MARKETING

From conflict facilitator Priya Parker: treat your pettiest group chat fights as practice rounds, not annoyances. She dug into the viral saga of "Oreo-gate," a parents' group chat argument over whether other kids should be allowed to bring Oreos to school, and found a surprisingly well-run fight underneath the internet's mockery: the parents added warmth with humour before disagreeing, asked genuine questions before getting defensive, and used a side chat to plan a way forward rather than to gossip about the mom who started it. Her point for anyone running a group, family, team or community, is that low-stakes disputes like this one are where people practise negotiating shared norms, so how well a group handles the silly fights predicts how well it will handle the serious ones. Via Priya Parker.

From newsletter writer and web designer Nick Gray: sometimes the best marketing is just an honest, well-illustrated complaint. An article he wrote about fighting off millions of bot requests hammering his donor database website went properly viral this week, landing on Hacker News, getting translated into Japanese, and picked up across several other sites, not because it pitched anything, but because it was a specific, well-told story with his own custom illustrations. The takeaway worth stealing: a detailed account of a real operational problem you solved, told plainly and with a bit of visual craft, will often travel further than anything written to be shared. Via Nick Gray.

And a smaller, blunter idea worth stealing from Morning Brew's events team: a real deadline sells better than a vague one. With fifty tickets left for the Marketing Brew Summit, the team emailed subscribers a straightforward three-day, fifty percent off countdown rather than a generic "spots filling up" nudge. It's not clever, but a specific number of tickets and a specific number of days left is a formula that keeps working precisely because most companies are too nervous to commit to it. Via Marketing Brew.

Now, to close, one interesting thing.

ONE INTERESTING THING

Theoretical computer scientist Scott Aaronson published an essay this week arguing that the AI singularity has, in his view, quietly already started, just unevenly distributed. His test is simple: if you took this month's AI news and sent it back twenty years, would anyone call it the beginning of a singularity? He says yes, absolutely, and argues that's all the confirmation anyone should need. What stuck with people wasn't the argument so much as one line about his teenage daughter, who joked, unprompted, that if she wants to become a mathematician she now has maybe two more weeks. Via Marginal Revolution.
