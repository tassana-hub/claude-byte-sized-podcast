The yen has fallen to its weakest level against the dollar since nineteen eighty six. It's down around three and a half percent against the dollar over six months, eleven percent over the past year, and roughly thirty five percent over three years, and on a broader trade weighted basis its value has halved over the past four decades. The Bank of Japan has spent close to seventy billion dollars this year alone trying to slow the slide, and is reportedly no longer telegraphing its interventions, instead springing them on currency speculators by surprise. Analysts blame a mix of short term pressures, including the wide gap between American and Japanese interest rates and higher oil prices forcing more yen sales to pay for energy imports, layered on top of longer running problems like Japan's shrinking workforce and heavy government debt. Prime Minister Takaichi has responded with a new stimulus package worth around two point three trillion dollars, aimed at technology and strategic industries, though critics warn the extra borrowing needed to fund it will only keep the yen under pressure. Via International Intrigue.

South Korea's memory chip makers are living through an extraordinary boom. One Samsung executive reportedly said this year's profit alone will exceed the company's entire cumulative semiconductor profit of the past forty years, with second quarter earnings expected to jump roughly eighteenfold on a year earlier. The driver is soaring demand for the memory chips that power AI data centres, a squeeze so severe that Apple was forced to raise Mac and iPad prices by up to a quarter last week, with memory prices industry wide up five to tenfold. In response, Samsung and SK Hynix have announced plans to invest around five hundred and twenty billion dollars over the next fifteen years to build a new domestic chip hub, though spread over that timeframe it's a more modest annual increase than the headline number suggests. Via Benedict Evans's newsletter and International Intrigue.

India's prime minister Narendra Modi is midway through an Indo-Pacific tour, having arrived in Jakarta on a trip that will also take him to Australia and New Zealand. Critical minerals and defence cooperation are high on the agenda, including talk of Indonesia buying India's BrahMos cruise missiles. Via International Intrigue.

Now, over to AI and tech.

Anthropic says it's found something like an internal notepad inside Claude's mind. New research describes an internal region the company calls J space, which behaves like a workspace holding the concepts Claude is actively using while it reasons, distinct from the visible chain of thought text it shows users. Anthropic says the structure emerged on its own during training rather than being deliberately built in, and when researchers swapped one internal concept for another, say spider for ant, in a question about how many legs something has, Claude's answer changed to match. When they artificially removed this workspace, Claude could still chat normally and recall facts, but its ability to solve multi step problems collapsed, suggesting this is where its harder reasoning actually happens. Anthropic is careful to say this doesn't settle whether Claude is conscious or feels anything, a live and sometimes tetchy debate the company has had with Microsoft's AI head Mustafa Suleyman. Via The Rundown and TLDR.

Anthropic also keeps signing eye watering infrastructure deals. Data centre operator TeraWulf has agreed a twenty year lease with the company expected to bring in around nineteen billion dollars in revenue, covering a Kentucky campus with roughly four hundred megawatts of computing capacity. Anthropic has separately released a self hosted enterprise gateway for rolling out Claude Code through Amazon and Google's clouds, and is reportedly in talks with Samsung about designing its own AI chip. Via TLDR and Benedict Evans's newsletter.

Nvidia's next generation AI server system has slipped by more than a year. The Kyber rack scale architecture, designed to link one hundred and forty four of the company's upcoming Rubin Ultra chips into a single giant computer, won't now arrive until twenty twenty eight, after manufacturing problems with a key circuit board. It's a sign that Nvidia's breakneck annual release schedule is starting to bump up against the limits of what factories can actually build. Via TLDR.

Cheaper, smaller AI models are starting to squeeze the market. Tencent has fully open sourced its Hy3 model, which needs less than half the hardware of the rival GLM five point two, though GLM still wins on coding benchmarks. Analysts think the shift toward smaller, cheaper models could trigger what one report called an AI margin collapse, as everyday tasks get handled by inexpensive models rather than premium ones. Small models are also proving useful in healthcare and agriculture in places without heavy data centre infrastructure, and one study found a cheap model can discard over two thirds of unnecessary retrieved text while still keeping ninety six percent of what actually matters. Via TLDR.

Now, over to startups and venture.

European fintech had a mixed day. Banking software firm Thought Machine has crossed one hundred million dollars in annual recurring revenue, with its chief executive eyeing a possible stock market listing in twenty twenty eight and wanting to double both revenue and customer numbers before then. Digital bank Starling is cutting around a hundred and thirty jobs as it leans further into automation, while buy now pay later firm Klarna won just under two billion dollars in an antitrust lawsuit against Google. Via Sifted.

The first European quantum computing company has floated on the Nasdaq. Finnish firm IQM listed via a merger with a shell company, with shares opening at around twelve dollars seventy, dipping, then recovering to close its first day near thirteen dollars and climbing further the day after. The float values IQM at around two point three billion dollars, a far lower multiple of revenue than the American quantum firm IonQ commands, though investors called the debut a solid one. Via Sifted.

A new survey suggests the tech workforce is splitting in two. Of nearly six thousand tech workers surveyed, whether someone feels amplified or diminished by AI turned out to be the single strongest predictor of how they feel about their career, ahead of seniority, role, or company size. Significant burnout jumped from forty five percent last year to fifty six percent this year, career optimism fell, and more than half of respondents said they wouldn't recommend a newcomer follow their own career path. Tellingly, fear of AI taking their job directly ranked low on the list of worries; people were far more concerned about being expected to do more for the same pay, or that the pace of work has simply become unsustainable. Via Lenny's Newsletter.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

Congo is playing hardball over cobalt. After exporters missed a July the fifth deadline, Kinshasa is reassigning unused export quotas to a new state run entity, a move mining giants Glencore and CMOC blame on a government system glitch rather than any fault of their own. Congo supplies around seventy percent of the world's cobalt, a critical mineral for batteries, so the standoff matters well beyond central Africa. Via International Intrigue.

British officials had their logins stolen. Several UK government email accounts were compromised after attackers exploited so called FortiBleed vulnerabilities, with the stolen credentials reportedly being sold on the dark web for around sixty thousand dollars. Researchers say the intrusion doesn't look state sponsored, but the attackers appear to be Russian speakers. Via TLDR.

Canada has chosen its next submarine builder, and it isn't South Korea. Prime Minister Mark Carney announced that Germany's TKMS, working on a joint German Norwegian Arctic capable design, will build Canada's fleet of around twelve new conventionally powered submarines, beating out South Korea's Hanwha, with deliveries expected in the twenty thirties. Via International Intrigue.

Now, over to community, events and marketing.

Here's an idea worth stealing if you work in marketing: stop worrying about clicks, start worrying about being named. Growth consultant Kevin Indig has spent the past year studying how people actually use AI search tools, and found that almost nobody clicks through on citations, even when a brand is the hidden source behind an answer. What matters far more is whether the AI actually says a brand's name out loud, since his research found only around one in eight brands that get quoted from also get mentioned by name. His advice for getting mentioned is to write like a wire reporter rather than a novelist, putting the most important facts right at the start, because AI systems weight the introduction heavily and barely read the middle. Via Amanda Natividad's newsletter.

And one for community builders: stop recruiting speakers, grow them instead. Skift's Women Leading Travel network fills its event stages almost entirely from members who are already active in the community between events, through smaller meetups, virtual workshops, and spotlight features, rather than through a cold outside application process. This autumn it's running leadership events on that model in New York, Montreal, Chicago, Las Vegas, and London. Via Skift.

Now, over to one interesting thing to leave you with.

A newsletter called Why Is This Interesting points out that America's measles cases, which averaged just four a week for three decades, have jumped to fifty eight a week since the start of last year, and argues it's a visible symptom of a much bigger problem: American scientists quietly leaving the country. Job applications abroad from American scientists rose by around a third last year, dozens of senior researchers have relocated to China, and the European Union has launched a five hundred million euro fund specifically designed to attract them to Europe. The piece's point is that research funding, like a vaccine programme, has knock on effects that take years to show up, and by the time the damage is visible, in outbreaks or in the technologies that never got invented, it's already too late to easily undo.
