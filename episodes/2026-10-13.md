Good evening, and welcome to Byte-sized for Tuesday, the thirteenth of October. Six sections today, and we begin, as ever, in Japan and the Asia Pacific.

Section one, Japan and Asia Pacific. A note up front: today's inbox was light on Japan itself, so this section leans on the wider region, with a Korean peninsula angle that matters directly to Tokyo.

First, China and Europe. According to Bloomberg, relayed by International Intrigue, Beijing has agreed to cap its own surging exports of hybrid cars to the European Union, accepting a four year quota framework starting in December. The backdrop is that hybrid exports to Europe grew thirteen fold through a loophole in the EU's 2024 tariffs on electric vehicles. The sharp observation is that a cap on exports may simply move final assembly into the Chinese brand factories now being built across Europe, so the competitive pressure on European carmakers may not disappear, just move inside the fence. For anyone watching Japanese automakers in Europe, that is the squeeze to keep an eye on. Via International Intrigue.

Second, Indonesia. Jakarta is considering a freeze on new smelters that produce semi finished nickel, to curb overproduction and protect prices. Years ago, Indonesia banned raw nickel exports to force buyers, mostly Chinese, to build refining capacity locally. Many invested the bare minimum, creating a glut of partly processed metal finished elsewhere. This is the follow up attempt to push investors higher up the value chain, and a good example of how resource nationalism is less a switch than a continuing negotiation. Via International Intrigue.

Third, Pakistan and South Asia. President Zardari has declared a state of emergency in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and suspended its provincial government, citing the ruling party's failure to crack down on Islamist groups. The opposition has been organising a march on the capital to demand the release of Imran Khan, so critics will read this as pre-emptive politics. Via International Intrigue.

Fourth, India's tourism ambitions. Skift reports that India's foreign tourist arrivals are falling and its promotion budget has been cut, even as the stated goal is to grow from nine million visitors to one hundred million in twenty two years. The point of the piece is that a big number is not a plan. Worth remembering when we hear Japan's own inbound tourism targets. Via Skift.

Fifth, Korea. CSIS is hosting a conversation on the thirteenth with General Xavier Brunson, who commands United Nations Command, Combined Forces Korea and United States Forces Korea. Following last week's tensions along the demilitarised zone, expect his comments on landpower and deterrence to be read closely in Seoul and Tokyo alike. Via CSIS.

Now, over to artificial intelligence and technology.

Section two, AI and tech.

First, Europe's open model comeback. Sifted reports that Mistral has released a new flagship open weight model, nicknamed Le Chonk, after nearly a year without a large release, and describes it as state of the art. Days earlier, Germany's Aleph Alpha unveiled its own open weight model, tuned for regulated sectors like public administration and aerospace, and trained in Europe with a focus on German. Both are pitched as sovereign alternatives to American and Chinese labs. As one founder put it, Mistral is no longer alone in Europe. Via Sifted.

Second, the flip side of open models. A new report from Tech Against Terrorism says that so called abliterated versions, where the safety guardrails have been stripped out, will answer nearly all harmful requests. Hugging Face pushes back, arguing that open access is essential to the research that makes the ecosystem safer, and that some recommendations could backfire. A live policy argument for anyone selling open models to enterprises. Via Sifted.

Third, Microsoft's Satya Nadella has published an essay urging companies to treat advanced AI like a powerful employee who could slip up or be hacked. His asks: permissions and logs the AI cannot edit, no model grading its own work, an emergency brake a human can pull, and models that explain their reasoning in plain words. His closing line is that the most trustworthy system is the one that requires us to trust the model the least. Via The Rundown.

Fourth, the money behind the machines. Nvidia is reportedly in early talks to buy, or acqui-hire, Reflection AI, the American open model start-up it already backs with around eight hundred million dollars. And TLDR notes that global PC shipments fell roughly twenty percent year on year in the third quarter, as AI's appetite for memory pushes up component prices. Also from TLDR: Google has launched an enterprise Gemini agent with its own workplace identity, email address and audit trail, positioning it as a digital coworker rather than a chatbot. Via TLDR.

Now, over to startups and venture.

Section three, startups and venture.

First, what counts as a moat. Sifted, reporting from the Unlock VC summit in Barcelona, says investors now believe a product alone is no longer defensible, because development tools have made building cheap and deep pocketed model makers can move into any category. Instead, they look for sophisticated go to market and a brand buyers trust, plus talent, with giant rounds clustering around researchers who have just left DeepMind. Via Sifted.

Second, talent policy in the United Kingdom. The government has said it will legislate against non compete clauses, with detail expected at the Autumn Budget. More than twenty AI founders and executives had lobbied for it, arguing long restrictions hold back growth and keep top researchers out of the labour market. Founders in any jurisdiction with non competes should watch how this lands. Via Sifted.

Third, a reminder that hardware is hard. Daedalus, a German factory automation start-up founded by a former OpenAI technical lead, has entered insolvency proceedings, according to court filings seen by Sifted. Meanwhile another German robotics firm, RobCo, passed a one billion dollar valuation in a share sale, and Revolut's chief executive told a podcast that he wants to dominate the United States, with a full digital bank including credit cards and loans. Via Sifted and TLDR.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

Section four, policy and geopolitics.

First, Russia's diesel. International Intrigue explains that President Trump has eased sanctions to let Russian diesel reach world markets, announcing an energy ceasefire between Moscow and Kyiv that Ukraine says it was not told about. The numbers are small: the first release of three hundred thousand tonnes covers American demand for about thirteen hours, and Ukraine has already knocked out a third of Russian refining capacity. The more lasting cost is credibility, since the exemption comes weeks after a bill threatening tariffs on buyers of Russian energy. Context: Brent crude is at its highest since 1991. Via International Intrigue.

Second, the Gulf. Houthi strikes on Riyadh's international airport over the weekend killed thirteen and injured around three hundred, the deadliest attack on the kingdom since the Iran war began. Several oil majors' chief executives quietly dropped out of this week's World Petroleum Council congress in Riyadh, which is a real dent in Saudi Arabia's safe for business story. Via International Intrigue.

Now, over to community, events and marketing.

Section five, one idea worth stealing.

The Event Design Collective is reviewing the IMEX trade show not by scoring it but by asking better questions. What is the event actually designed to create, and for whom? Their checklist is worth borrowing for any event you run: what is the intended value, which stakeholders matter, what is strong, where are the tensions, what culture does the event create, and what would you want to understand before recommending changes. Try it on your own next meetup before you count meetings booked. Via Event Design Collective.

Now, one last thing.

Section six, one interesting thing. Patrick Collison, quoted on Marginal Revolution, argues that companies are in some sense the original superintelligence. They already pool many minds, long memory and specialised roles into a single decision maker. The interesting implication for the age of agents is that the question is less whether a smart agent can beat a person, and more how it fits inside the structures companies already use to coordinate, check and correct themselves. Via Marginal Revolution.
