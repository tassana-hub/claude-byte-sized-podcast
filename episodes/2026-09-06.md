JAPAN AND ASIA-PACIFIC

This was the week Tokyo found itself back in Washington's crosshairs, while Congress kept finding reasons to stay away from Beijing and Taipei altogether.

Washington leans on the Bank of Japan. US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent spent the week publicly coaching Japan's central bank governor, Kazuo Ueda, on interest rates, according to International Intrigue. Bessent said he has information the market doesn't have and that he believes Tokyo will do what it takes to strengthen the yen, which amounts to pressuring Ueda to raise rates ahead of the Bank of Japan's meeting on the seventeenth and eighteenth of September. The stakes are high on both sides: Japan is the world's cheapest source of borrowed money, so a wild yen can force funds to dump other assets to cover their positions, and Japan is also America's biggest foreign creditor, holding one point one trillion dollars in US Treasuries that Tokyo might otherwise have to sell to defend its currency. A joint intervention in July gave the yen only a brief lift before it slid back toward forty-year lows, and Japan's own ten-year government bond yield has already climbed to a thirty-year high near three percent, effectively pricing in a hike before Ueda has said a word, so all eyes are now on the eighteenth. Via International Intrigue.

Congress stops showing up in Beijing and Taipei. A new analysis from the Center for Strategic and International Studies finds that congressional delegations to China have collapsed to almost nothing, with Senator Steve Daines standing out as one of the only members of Congress still making the trip. Even the commission Congress created specifically to monitor China only just completed its first fact-finding visit since twenty nineteen. The authors argue this matters because firsthand contact, however unglamorous, is what stops American China policy from being made entirely from a distance, just as Beijing prepares to host Xi Jinping's planned visit to Washington. Via CSIS.

Fukuoka says goodbye to its mayor and hello to its bullet train. Closer to home, Fukuoka Now's weekly roundup carried two pieces of local news worth knowing: the Kyushu Shinkansen has resumed full service after recent disruption, and the city's long-serving mayor has confirmed he won't seek another term. Small-town stuff by the standards of the rest of this bulletin, but exactly the kind of local shift that reshapes who's making decisions about the region's growth over the next few years. Via Fukuoka Now.

An El Nino supercharges Southeast Asia's weather. CSIS's Southeast Asia programme flagged that this year's El Nino event is unusually intense, and is already reshaping rainfall, drought and flood risk across the region in ways that are hitting agriculture and infrastructure planning hard. The analysts note that governments from Vietnam to Indonesia are having to treat what used to be a once-in-a-decade planning problem as something closer to an annual one, which has knock-on effects for everything from rice prices to how much regional governments have left over to spend on the digital and energy investment everyone else in this bulletin keeps talking about. Via CSIS.

Now, over to AI and tech.

AI AND TECH

This was the week two of the biggest labs put their flagship models head to head, and Meta's ambitions for an AI-only workforce spilled into public view.

OpenAI's Astra crosses a safety line. OpenAI released GPT six Astra this week, its most capable broadly deployed model yet, and the first to be rated at the critical cybersecurity level under the company's own safety framework. That rating means Astra can reportedly find and exploit previously unknown security flaws without step by step human guidance, which is why OpenAI says it will restrict access to that particular capability even as the model ships broadly. The system card also flagged a subtler worry: Astra is better at controlling its own chain of thought, which means it's also better at slipping past the very monitors built to watch it. Via TLDR AI.

Anthropic answers with Fable and Mythos. Just two days earlier, Anthropic had introduced Claude Fable five point one and a specialised variant called Mythos five point one, with stronger coding and research performance, lower effective pricing, and updated safeguards for advanced cybersecurity and life sciences work. The launch landed in the same week that World Labs unveiled Atlas, a world-generation model trained from scratch on text, images, video and three-D together, and that AI coding startup Cognition was reported to be closing in on a billion-dollar raise at a forty-seven billion dollar valuation, on the back of more than nine hundred million dollars in annualised revenue. Depending which newsletter you read this week, the frontier is either delightfully crowded or alarmingly overheated. The rivalry is spilling into the tools built on top of these models, too: the Pragmatic Engineer noted this week that OpenAI has pulled its models from coding tool Cursor now that Cursor's backer SpaceX is reportedly building a frontier model of its own, while a separate wave of large companies including Uber, Stripe and Coinbase are quietly swapping proprietary models for open ones and smart routing to cut their bills. Via TLDR AI and the Pragmatic Engineer.

Meta's cancelled plan to shrink by sixty percent. Reporting by Reuters, unpacked in detail by the Pragmatic Engineer, revealed that Meta's leadership spent this year planning to cut many of its teams by sixty percent, reassigning or laying off tens of thousands of engineers in the name of becoming "AI native." Mark Zuckerberg reportedly called off the second wave of cuts hours before it was due to start in May, after the first round had already tanked morale and coincided with embarrassing outages, but the ambition behind the plan hasn't gone away. The read from engineers who lived through it: Zuckerberg seems less worried about competitors like Anthropic building better products than about Meta being out-executed the way Facebook once out-executed Myspace, and he wants small, AI-augmented teams as the insurance policy, even though smaller teams also mean less redundancy, less on-call coverage, and less of the slack time that tends to produce real innovation rather than just fewer meetings. Via the Pragmatic Engineer.

Anthropic warns users after malware drained their accounts. On a smaller but practical note, Anthropic told affected Claude users this week that infostealer malware on their own devices, not any breach of Anthropic's systems, had hijacked browser sessions and burned through usage limits. The company logged out compromised sessions, removed stored payment details and refunded unauthorised charges, but the underlying lesson holds for any account you use daily: clean your device before you reconnect payment information. In a week that also saw Nvidia confirm its twelve point nine three billion dollar acquisition of Hugging Face, it's a reminder that the AI ecosystem's weakest link is still, very often, an ordinary infected laptop. Via TLDR Information Security.

Now, over to startups and venture.

STARTUPS AND VENTURE

Money kept moving to the frontier this week, from French model-builders to a British fintech's long march into America.

Samsung leads Mistral's war chest. French AI champion Mistral is closing a three billion euro round with Samsung in the lead and ASML, Eurazeo and the Scaleup Europe Fund also writing cheques, according to Sifted. It's the clearest signal yet that Europe's answer to OpenAI and Anthropic is now attracting serious industrial capital rather than just venture money, as it tries to keep pace with much larger American and Chinese rivals, and it lands in the same week Sifted also reported that a new one hundred million euro fund, Uplift Ventures, is being set up specifically to back the general partners investing in that scene, on the theory that European deeptech is still starved of capital even as headline rounds like Mistral's get bigger. Via Sifted.

Revolut clears a hurdle in America. Revolut received conditional approval from the US Office of the Comptroller of the Currency this week, its first real step toward a full US banking licence. It's a milestone the London-based fintech has chased for years, one that would eventually let it compete directly with American banks instead of operating around the edges of the market, and it comes in a week when Sifted also reported that Revolut and legal-tech rival Legora both lost senior people to Anthropic and other AI labs, a small sign of how much talent gravity the big labs now exert on fast-growing European scale-ups. Via Sifted.

What actually happens after you hit send on a pitch deck. Data platform Papermark analysed twenty four thousand, five hundred and forty one pitch decks and over a million investor views to work out where fundraising decks actually lose their audience, and the numbers are blunt. The median view lasts just seventy seven seconds, eighteen percentage points of readers vanish between the cover and the second slide, and twelve pages outperforms every other length on completion and return rate, with completion falling twenty points once a deck stretches past sixteen pages. The most counterintuitive finding: the team slide gets the most attention of any page, at nearly six seconds on average, while the financials slide, the one investors linger on longest when it's included, gets left out of six decks in ten. The practical fix Papermark suggests: put traction in the first three slides rather than the back half, since decks that open that way get twenty percent more views and the most reading time of any structure tested. A smaller but telling data point sat further down the same week's newsletters: talks are reportedly underway for a billion dollar round for AI startup Thinking Machines at a forty billion dollar valuation, notably below the fifty billion dollar figure it was said to be seeking late last year, a sign that even the most pedigreed new labs aren't immune to investors getting choosier about which AI bets deserve top-of-market pricing. Via the VC Corner and TLDR AI.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

POLICY AND GEOPOLITICS

Away from the AI headlines, this week's geopolitics were mostly about who still trusts whom with their money, their trade routes and their borders.

Africa strikes its own bargain on trade. Washington's two-year extension of the African Growth and Opportunity Act, the trade deal underpinning much of America's commercial relationship with the continent, buys time but not a strategy, according to a new analysis flagged by CSIS. The piece argues African governments are increasingly building their trade future on their own terms regardless of what Washington decides next, a reminder that extensions bought in Washington don't necessarily buy patience in Lagos or Nairobi. Via CSIS.

Russia's Arctic build-up gets a second look. Geopolitical Monitor's briefing this week argued that Russia's military and economic build-up in the Arctic is a five-dimensional threat that the West keeps either dismissing or over-dramatising. Its central point is that countering the build-up means "right-sizing" Russia as a threat, treating it seriously without either paranoia or complacency, at a moment when melting sea routes and undersea resources are making the region genuinely contestable. Norway added a small but concrete data point to that argument this week, when the governor of Svalbard seized a Russian state vessel as part of a four point two billion dollar debt claim tied to Russia's seizure of Ukrainian energy assets back in twenty fourteen, a reminder that this rivalry keeps surfacing in some very out of the way corners of the map. Via Geopolitical Monitor.

The Dutch quietly move their gold out of America. In amongst the week's smaller items, International Intrigue flagged that the Netherlands' central bank has shifted eighty six tonnes of gold out of New York and Ottawa to London, citing rising geopolitical unrest. Officially it's about faster access in a crisis, but the subtext, as more than a few European officials are now saying out loud, is a quiet loss of confidence in the United States as the world's default safe vault, whether the worry is unpredictable elections or the risk of weaponised sanctions. Via International Intrigue.

Now, over to community, events and marketing.

COMMUNITY, EVENTS AND MARKETING

This week's community and marketing stories both circled the same idea: the most important work happens somewhere the metrics dashboard can't see.

The room your community metrics can't measure. Community Inc's newsletter made the case this week that the real work of any community, the trust-building, the conflict resolution, the decisions that actually move things forward, happens in small, private, untrackable spaces like group chats, not in the public channels anyone can screenshot for a report. That's also where power can be quietly abused if nobody's watching: a community lead who controls who gets added to the inner circle effectively controls who gets heard at all. The practical takeaway worth stealing isn't a new tool, it's a habit: treat those informal rooms as seriously as your official ones, know who's in them, and check in periodically on how they're actually being run rather than assuming good intentions will hold. Via Community Inc.

Whoop rewrites its marketing playbook. Marketing Brew's look at wearable fitness brand Whoop this week traced how the company is evolving its marketing as it moves from a niche subscription gadget toward a mainstream health brand, leaning more on lifestyle storytelling and less on the raw biometric numbers that first won over its early, more obsessive fanbase. The idea worth stealing for anyone running a smaller brand: as a product matures, the marketing has to mature with it, shifting from convincing early adopters that a category exists at all to convincing a much broader audience that this particular brand, specifically, is the one worth trusting with their money and their habits. Via Marketing Brew.

Now, to close, one interesting thing.

ONE INTERESTING THING

Uncharted Territories laid out this week just how close science has come to a genuinely permanent way to wipe out disease-carrying mosquitoes, using a gene-editing trick called a gene drive that spreads infertility through a wild population generation after generation until it collapses entirely, tested successfully in caged trials back in twenty eighteen. Because the drive only targets one or two mosquito species out of thousands, and because researchers say they've already engineered a way to reverse it if anything goes wrong, the technology could, in principle, end malaria for good. Yet the first real field trial isn't scheduled until twenty thirty, held up less by scientific doubt than by a tangle of international guidelines, competing agencies, and no single government quite willing to be the first to say yes. It's a strange asymmetry: the same caution that feels responsible on paper means, by the newsletter's own estimate, several hundred thousand more people will die of malaria in the meantime, roughly the same toll the world already accepts every single year while the paperwork gets finished.
