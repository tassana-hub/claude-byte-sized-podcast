Good morning, and welcome to Byte-sized. It is Tuesday the tenth of November, and today there are six sections, beginning, as ever, in Japan and the Asia Pacific.

Section one, Japan and Asia Pacific. A quiet day for stories with a direct Japan label, so I have kept to the wider region, with the Japan relevance flagged where it is mine rather than the source's.

First, South Korea's energy exposure. A C S I S study of the Korean economy puts a number on something Tokyo knows well: South Korea imports close to ninety-four per cent of its energy, and around seventy per cent of its crude oil arrives through the Strait of Hormuz. With the Iran war still disrupting supply, the study's point is that a single hostile actor can cut that flow by force. Japan is in a very similar position, so this is a useful framing if you are talking to energy or infrastructure people in either country. Via C S I S.

Second, New Zealand. Saturday's election has produced a result too close to call, with voters punishing the two big parties and rewarding smaller, newer ones who may now become kingmakers. International Intrigue reads it as part of a wider splintering trend. For anyone doing business in the region, expect a few weeks of coalition arithmetic before policy direction is clear. Via International Intrigue.

Third, India and Asia Pacific travel. Skift reports that India's two biggest airlines hold quite different ideas about going global, each wanting a bigger share of international traffic that has long flowed through foreign carriers. In a separate item, PhocusWire notes that the Indian travel platform MakeMyTrip has taken a stake in the ride-sharing app Quick Ride, and that its Hot Twenty-Five Travel Startups list for twenty twenty-seven has just opened. Via Skift, and PhocusWire.

Fourth, a date for the diary. On Monday the sixteenth of November, C S I S is hosting an event on the strategic implications of the Russia and North Korea axis, asking how durable the partnership between Moscow and Pyongyang really is. If regional security comes up with government contacts, that is the live question. Via C S I S.

Now, over to A I and tech.

Section two, A I and tech.

First, the story everyone is talking about. Nvidia's board has ordered an independent review after reports that its chief executive, Jensen Huang, was targeted by a foreign intelligence effort. Mr Huang has admitted to an affair with Alice Huang, no relation, a former Singapore-based executive at a firm called Megaspeed, which American officials link to China's Ministry of State Security. He has apologised to staff and remains in post, and the board says it sees no reason to believe any secrets were compromised. He also has until the eleventh of November to answer a request from House Republicans for a private briefing. International Intrigue's sharper point is that espionage is rarely about one smoking gun; it is a mosaic of small insights, and the real question is whether the relationship influenced how Nvidia vetted its customers. Nvidia's shares fell around five per cent in pre-market trading. Expect Washington hawks to push for tighter chip export oversight, particularly around the South East Asia routes. Via The Rundown A I, and International Intrigue.

Second, A I building A I. T L D R A I highlights an essay arguing that Anthropic may be about to meet its own threshold of dramatically accelerated progress due to A I, and that some researchers think it already has. A companion piece looks at OpenAI's own report on research automation, which finds that agents are solving more complex tasks for researchers, but reliability is still low. So the loop is turning, but not yet smoothly. Via T L D R A I.

Third, agent memory. Anthropic has published a detailed look at how its agents build long-term memory, including a process it calls dreaming, where an agent uses idle time to check its notes against logs of past work and fix errors. The practical lesson for anyone building agents is that newer models are much better at judging what to save, and that explaining to the model why memory matters works better than rigid rules about what to write down. Via The Rundown A I, and T L D R A I.

Fourth, a quick tools round-up. Meta has added custom Spaces to its Muse app, letting users describe a small app, such as a gift tracker or project planner, and have it built. Thinking Machines Lab has released a new coding and vision model called Inkling Two Small, with open-sourcing planned for next week. And T L D R reports that Microsoft has quietly expanded usage limits on Copilot Chat for customers without separate Copilot licences. Via The Rundown A I, and T L D R.

Now, over to startups and venture.

Section three, startups and venture.

First, the money at the top. OpenAI has reportedly closed about eleven billion dollars in new funding at a valuation of one point two five trillion dollars, up from eight hundred and fifty-two billion in March. The round fell short of the thirty billion first reported, which T L D R reads as a sign of growing investor caution about A I valuations, even at this scale. Via T L D R, and The Rundown A I.

Second, a notable exit from Europe. Sequoia is closing its London office after six years and cutting its European scout programme, saying it will back European founders from the Bay Area instead. Sifted reports that three of the four London partners are staying in Europe, and the debate is now whether this is managed decline or an overreaction, and whether it opens the door to local investors. Alongside it, Swedish firm Creandum has raised seven hundred and fifty million dollars for its eighth fund, its largest yet. Via Sifted.

Third, where the capital is going. Sifted's data shows European construction technology startups have raised nine hundred and fifty-seven million euros across a hundred rounds so far this year, against three hundred and ninety million for the whole of last year, though a few heavyweights drive the total. British-German missile start-up Hypersonica raised a hundred million euro Series B, and on the A I side, Warp Sciences raised two hundred and fifteen million dollars to apply A I and robotics to drug discovery. The pattern is defence, physical industry and A I for science taking the big cheques. Via Sifted, and T L D R.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

Section four, policy and geopolitics.

First, Ukraine. Geopolitical Monitor argues that a frozen front in Ukraine is not a path to peace. Russia has used frozen conflicts in Transnistria, Abkhazia and South Ossetia to hold its neighbours back, and the author's warning is that a frozen Ukraine would work the same way. Worth having in your pocket whenever ceasefire talk comes up. Via Geopolitical Monitor.

Second, European hardware and China. Sifted reports that European hardware startups, led by defence and manufacturing, are working to remove China from their supply chains entirely, citing the risk of tariffs and export controls, restrictions on batteries and rare earths, and security concerns about embedded components. One photonic chip startup has decided to build its whole supply chain outside China. For enterprise and government audiences, it is a concrete example of de-risking moving from slogan to procurement. Via Sifted.

Third, American defence money. C S I S expects Congress to leave aside the administration's nearly seventy billion dollar supplemental defence request during the lame-duck session, given the short timeline and competing priorities. Via C S I S.

Now, over to community, events and marketing.

Section five, community, events and marketing. One idea worth stealing.

Skift looked at what a live concert revealed about the growing business of music-led trips, and the takeaway for anyone running events is that the experience people travel for is shared emotion, not logistics. If you run a community, design the moment people will talk about afterwards, and build the schedule around it. Separately, Priya Parker is running a session this week on rethinking the rituals that bring people together, a reminder that the best gatherings are deliberately designed, not inherited. Via Skift, and Priya Parker.

Now, one last thing.

Section six, one interesting thing. In Why Is This Interesting's media diet interview, an investor who analyses the luxury industry says he deliberately spends a lot of his reading time on material that contradicts his own thesis. He says that to form a solid opinion you need to hear the other side, and that sometimes he even changes his mind. It is a simple habit, and a good test of whether you are researching or just collecting reasons to agree with yourself. Via Why Is This Interesting.
