Good morning, and welcome to Byte-sized for Monday, the nineteenth of October. Six sections today, and we begin, as ever, in Japan and the Asia Pacific.

Section one, Japan and Asia Pacific.

First, a rough week for the chip makers of East Asia. Tokyo Electron, Japan's chipmaking equipment giant, fell seven point two per cent in a single day. The trigger was news that China's memory underdog, CXMT, has made progress on a new type of high density memory chip and has now captured around ten per cent of the global market. That leaves Japanese and Korean hardware firms feeling sandwiched between squeezed Western buyers on one side and rising Chinese suppliers on the other. SoftBank also dropped about nine per cent on its wider exposure to artificial intelligence investments. For balance, Tokyo Electron is still up around forty per cent this year. Via International Intrigue.

Second, Indonesia. The index provider MSCI says it is considering moving Indonesia's stock market from emerging to standalone status, which would remove it from the main global indices. Jakarta's benchmark fell eleven per cent in one day, its worst in decades, and is down forty per cent since January. MSCI points to opaque ownership, concentrated insider holdings and weak enforcement, a heavy blow to a government that promised eight per cent growth on foreign investment. Via International Intrigue.

Third, Cambodia's scam centres, where Japan has a seat at the table. A CSIS commentary asks whether the clean up is working. Cambodia passed its first anti-scam law in April, says it has shut more than two hundred centres, and has released some two hundred thousand people. It declared victory in August. But Amnesty International reports that more than seventy per cent of the compounds it identified appear to have been ignored, and that some were warned in advance and moved. Scams are estimated to make up between forty and sixty per cent of the country's economy, which is why elite networks are so hard to touch. September's Phnom Penh Declaration was backed by the United States, China, Japan and every ASEAN state except Thailand. The test now is whether the elites behind the compounds are ever investigated. Via CSIS.

Fourth, a number from CSIS to keep in your pocket: North Korea's overseas labour programme now covers more than one hundred thousand workers in at least seventeen countries, earning the state between four hundred and fifty and eight hundred million dollars a year. And Intrigue notes that the long delayed New Yalu River Bridge between North Korea and China has finally opened, after twelve years. Via CSIS and International Intrigue.

Now, over to artificial intelligence and technology.

Section two, AI and tech.

First, who is actually paying for AI. Ramp's October index finds that just one per cent of firms account for eighty per cent of spending on OpenAI and Anthropic. September spending per employee was three point one per cent below its July peak, even as total token volumes rose, and the index suggests falling model prices are the reason. A related note: five frontier models now score within five points of each other on one intelligence benchmark, yet the cost of finishing the same tasks differs by three point eight times. The practical lesson is to test your own real requests across candidate models before choosing a default. Via TLDR.

Second, ASML cannot keep up. It sold one hundred and thirteen lithography systems in the third quarter, up from eighty six, for record sales of eleven point seven billion euros. It now expects supply in both 2027 and 2028 to fall short of demand, even after a planned thirty per cent capacity boost, and some customers want to place orders for 2029 and 2030. Via TLDR.

Third, the other side of the boom: debt. Intrigue walks through a tech sell-off. CoreWeave, the rental landlord of AI computing power, has borrowed around fifty billion dollars, and bonds it issued in June at nine point six per cent are now being priced to yield fifteen. Oracle fell nine per cent in a day. The International Monetary Fund notes that AI hyperscalers now issue ten per cent of all new investment grade corporate bonds, up from one per cent two years ago. Yet TSMC posted a record quarterly profit of twenty three point six billion dollars. The old gold rush line applies: the seller of pickaxes tends to be the last to suffer. Via International Intrigue.

Fourth, travel meets agents. Booking Holdings has launched Vayo, an AI travel agent that organises bookings from a user's inbox and watches trips for savings and disruption, including reservations made outside Booking's own brands. The company plans to invest one hundred million dollars. Via PhocusWire.

Section three, startups and venture.

First, cybersecurity funding. Crunchbase counts six point eight billion dollars invested in the third quarter, up fifty per cent on the previous quarter and driven by larger rounds. Keyfactor raised one billion, Cyera four hundred million, and Visa agreed to buy BioCatch for two point four billion. Via TLDR.

Second, a warning for investors in defence technology. Sifted reports that European investors see fuzzy revenue reporting in the sector, with confusion between bookings and recognised revenue. One investor was shown revenue that came from a veterans' association, and another is often shown grant funding presented as defence sales. Classified contracts make checking harder still. The advice from one investor: accounting is accounting. Via Sifted.

Third, state money for deep tech. The British Business Bank has put one hundred and eighty million pounds into six AI and deep tech venture funds, including Entrepreneurs First. Its chief investment officer says the country's funding landscape does not match its technical excellence. Meanwhile Nvidia has spoken of two billion dollars of new investment across Europe. Via Sifted.

Section four, policy and geopolitics.

Two items worth having ready for conversations with government or enterprise people. First, the United States has proposed four hundred and twenty nine million dollars for conventional bunker busting weapons in its next defence budget, against an average of one hundred and twenty three million over the past ten years, as stocks of weapons for deeply buried targets run thin, according to CSIS. Second, Ukraine says SpaceX has capped the speed of military satellite terminals mounted on drones, with no warning, causing missions to be cancelled. Officials suspect it is deliberate, and a Ukrainian firm is developing an alternative. It is a vivid reminder of what depending on a private provider means. Via CSIS and TLDR.

Third, the Pacific. New Zealand is sending a six month team to help Fiji overhaul its security agencies, after Washington warned that China linked hackers had gained access to a regional organisation based in Suva. The United States has not named the target, though speculation points to the Pacific Islands Forum secretariat, which denies it. Intrigue reads it as Washington stepping up counter intelligence in a region where Beijing and Taipei are competing for recognition, and where Japan is also an active partner. Via International Intrigue.

Now, over to community, events and marketing.

Section five, one idea worth stealing.

From Priya Parker, a tool for community builders. Before you react to a group conflict, ask what kind of fight it is. She plots two things: how intense it is, and how much it touches the question of who we are as a group. Low on both is a kerfuffle. High intensity but low identity is a brawl. Quiet but existential is a hairline fracture. Loud and existential is a rift. Her example is a lesbian bar in Massachusetts that dropped its mask rule on Saturday nights to raise revenue. The owners saw a business decision, but members saw a betrayal of the bar's purpose. Spot the identity question early and you can answer it properly.

And one for marketers, from a TLDR piece on the app Cal AI. It paid fitness creators thousands of dollars to post and saw no spike in downloads, because viewers were not the right audience. It now reads the comments for people asking for help the app provides, and caps what it pays per thousand views at what those views earn. Check the comments for customers before paying for reach. Via TLDR.

Now, one last thing.

Section six, one interesting thing. Works in Progress, by Charles Mann, asks what on earth is Dekton, the new surface on his induction cooktop. The answer is porcelain. It is one of humanity's oldest high technologies, first made over three thousand years ago in China, and one of its newest. Europeans spent centuries failing to copy it, until an alchemist locked in a fortress attic and a mathematician finally got there in seventeen oh eight. Today, a slab pressed under twenty five thousand tonnes is nearly unbreakable, and porcelain scaffolds are even used to grow replacement bone. His son has never known a world that was not made of porcelain. Via Works in Progress.
