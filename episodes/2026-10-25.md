Good morning, and welcome to Byte-sized, the week of the nineteenth of October. It is Sunday, so this is the week in review, in six sections, and we begin, as ever, in Japan and the Asia Pacific.

Section one, Japan and Asia Pacific.

Where the week landed: the region's story was about supply chains and who controls the critical pieces, from memory chips to rare earths, with Japan caught in the middle.

First, the chips. At the start of the week Tokyo Electron fell seven per cent in a single day, after news that China's memory maker CXMT had made progress on a new kind of high density memory and now holds around ten per cent of the global market. By midweek the picture was sharper: CXMT expects a nine month profit of nearly twenty billion dollars, against a small loss a year earlier, with revenue jumping from about four and a half billion to nearly thirty eight billion. The company credits shortages of memory driven by artificial intelligence. The caution is that memory booms end when new capacity floods the market, and its claim to have the latest generation in mass production is unverified. Even so, it explains why Japanese and Korean equipment makers have felt squeezed all week. Via International Intrigue and T L D R.

Second, rare earths. Japan may have only months, not years, before it runs out of yttrium, which goes into jet engines, semiconductors, electric vehicles and L E D lighting. China restricted exports last year, after Prime Minister Takaichi suggested Japan might respond in any war over Taiwan, and Tokyo is now lobbying Washington for help. If you sell into Japanese manufacturing, supply chain resilience is now a boardroom topic. Via International Intrigue.

Third, China's own numbers. Third quarter growth was four point five per cent, which keeps the five per cent target within reach, but retail sales are crawling at one per cent and property investment is down another twenty per cent this year. Growth is coming from state funded high technology manufacturing and record exports, which is to say Beijing is replacing weak confidence at home with subsidised demand abroad. Via International Intrigue.

Fourth, a quieter cluster of signals. Japan's bigger regional partners are also reshuffling: Vietnam signed a free trade agreement with the European Free Trade Association after fourteen years of talks, Accenture is reportedly exploring a sale of its Greater China consulting business for up to one and a half billion dollars, and the index provider MSCI is considering demoting Indonesia from emerging to standalone status, after which Jakarta's market fell eleven per cent in a day. The common thread is companies and investors hedging away from single points of exposure. Via International Intrigue and T L D R.

Fifth, closer to home for anyone in Kyushu. The Japan and United States Keen Sword exercise began on the nineteenth, running for eleven days across Kyushu and Okinawa. In Fukuoka, Mayor Takashima has said he will not name a successor ahead of November's election, after sixteen years in office. The plans for the former Kyushu University Hakozaki campus came into focus, with homes, research space, a major food destination and a proposed international school, and the Ramen Tech start-up week opened in Tenjin earlier this month. One small note for foreign residents: Nanzoin temple in Sasaguri now charges tourists five hundred yen, but residents of Japan enter free. Via Fukuoka Now.

Sixth, the artificial intelligence race, seen from Japan and China. Speeda's round-up for Japanese readers framed the debate over slowing down as a prisoner's dilemma, and made a sharper point about access: prices for models anyone can buy are falling fast, but the most capable systems are increasingly handed out by vetting rather than price. For Japanese firms the question is how to qualify for the top tier. Then on Friday, China's DeepSeek was reported to have raised about fifteen billion dollars, with Tencent and the battery maker C A T L among the backers, days after American agencies were told to remove its A I. Expect that to sharpen the debate in Tokyo and Seoul about which stack to build on. Via Speeda A I News, T L D R and The Rundown A I.

Seventh, two small Japan signals worth keeping. For the first time since the survey began in two thousand and eight, fewer than half of people in Japan, forty nine point eight per cent, now pay for a monthly newspaper subscription, a warning for anyone building subscription media. And Japan's space agency is set to launch its Martian Moons mission, aiming to bring a sample back from Phobos by twenty thirty one, a quiet showcase of Tokyo as a trusted deep space partner. Via International Intrigue.

Now, over to artificial intelligence and technology.

Section two, AI and tech.

Where the week landed: it was a week where artificial intelligence stopped being a product story and became an infrastructure and security story.

First, the biggest story of the week by far. Two critical flaws in P H P, the language behind roughly seventy per cent of websites, were found when researchers pointed powerful A I models at the code in August. Humans had dismissed the bugs as harmless memory quirks. Someone leaked details two days before the planned patch, and with A I help attackers built a working exploit in about six hours, a gap that used to be measured in weeks. Cloudflare reported thousands of attempts, then millions after the official announcement. America's National Security Agency told anyone who could not patch to take systems offline, and government sites in several European countries did just that. More than half of P H P servers run old versions that no longer get regular updates, so the clean up will be long. If you run a website, update this weekend. Via International Intrigue, The Rundown A I and T L D R.

Second, the agent economy arrived at the big suppliers. Anthropic launched Claude Duet, a personal agent that can act for you around the clock, with your sign off required before anything is deleted, paid or sent. Microsoft, meanwhile, described A I agents as akin to insider threats and launched Guardian Agents in Defender, while reportedly planning to pay Anthropic more than two billion dollars a year to use Claude in Copilot. And American banks say they are scrambling to detect and block unauthorised third party agents poking around customer accounts. The lesson of the week is separating the supply of intelligence from the authority over it. Via T L D R and The Rundown A I.

Third, what agents do to the companies building them. Anthropic reported that by August the hours worked by its employees' agents outnumbered human hours by six to one, junior staff fell from twenty seven per cent of technical hires to nine per cent, and engineers ship about five times as many code changes as six months ago. The Pragmatic Engineer added that Anthropic built its own source control system after Git struggled at thirty thousand pull requests a day, while a separate analysis of twenty seven companies asks whether higher A I spending has truly changed shipping speed. Via The Rundown A I and The Pragmatic Engineer.

Fourth, who pays for it all. OpenAI is reportedly closing in on a round at a valuation of about one point two five trillion dollars, led by investors from the United Arab Emirates. A Ramp index found that one per cent of firms account for eighty per cent of spending on the two leading labs, and OpenRouter reports that open weight models are about sixty per cent of tokens but only twenty six per cent of spending. Frontier capability is concentrating in a few hands while cheaper models carry the volume. Via T L D R.

Fifth, a few quick moves from the labs. OpenAI acquired Orchestra Bio, a platform that helps biopharma teams coordinate drug development, and is now also selling its models and Codex through Google Cloud, a sign that the old exclusive partnerships are loosening. Apple has confirmed an event on the twenty eighth of October in Los Angeles, where it is expected to show its first touch screen Macs. And Agility Robotics will use Nvidia's new computing modules as the exclusive onboard brain of the general sale version of its Digit humanoid, which tells you where physical A I is heading. Via T L D R.

Sixth, a note on the security side. Beyond P H P, the week brought a Capcom breach involving eleven point seven million accounts, malware in a popular A I developer library on the Python package index, and a leak of thousands of shared conversation links from Meta's new assistant, found through public domain name records. The pattern is the same: the tools are new, the mistakes are old. Via T L D R.

Now, over to start-ups and venture.

Section three, start-ups and venture.

Where the week landed: money is flowing, but toward a narrower set of winners, and increasingly toward the customers of the chip makers.

First, Sifted's analysis of Nvidia's European tour, which ended in Berlin: as in the United States, Nvidia tends to back the companies that buy its chips, so the largest sectors it has funded in Europe are compute buyers and A I cloud builders. Investors are divided on whether that is healthy. Via Sifted.

Second, the Nordic split. Stockholm start-ups have raised four and a half billion euros so far this year, more than double last year, led by Legora, which hit an eight point eight billion dollar valuation this week, and Lovable. Oslo has raised just two hundred and seventeen million euros, down from three hundred and forty million. As one Norwegian investor put it, the ultimate joy for a Norwegian start-up is to be sold really fast, and the missing piece is growth capital. Via Sifted.

Third, a reminder about fintech. Global fintech funding fell twenty one per cent, even as Ramp applied for a national trust bank charter and a code testing platform called Blacksmith reached a billion dollar valuation just two months after its previous round. Money is chasing infrastructure for the agent era. Via T L D R and Sifted.

Fourth, some European signals. Madrid's Seaya Nova raised six hundred million euros for a growth fund and is opening in London, and the median Italian early stage round this year is one point eight million euros, up from one million in twenty twenty four. Radical Ventures, the Canadian firm, has a new fund of more than a billion dollars and is betting on the wave of London labs founded by former DeepMind researchers. And a cautionary tale from The Generalist and others: Foursquare was acquired about a month ago, ending a seventeen year story as a zero dollar outcome for equity holders. Via Sifted and T L D R.

Fifth, the founder playbook. The V C Corner launched G R Angel, with fifty three companies backed, one point two billion dollars raised by those companies, one unicorn and a first exit, a company that went from one hundred thousand to seven million dollars in annual revenue in a year and sold to an A I lab before its Series A. And Indie Hackers profiled founders whose fifteen thousand dollar product bet, with a thirty thousand dollar loan hanging over them, now earns twelve thousand dollars a month selling templates. Via The V C Corner and Indie Hackers.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

Section four, policy and geopolitics.

Where the week landed: a week of pressure points where technology and security meet.

First, North Korea. A series of missile tests, a warning from Kim Yo Jong that border loudspeakers will be switched back on, and a C S I S discussion asking whether the launches are a prelude to a summit. Separately, C S I S estimates North Korea's overseas workers now number more than one hundred thousand across at least seventeen countries, earning the state hundreds of millions of dollars a year. Via C S I S and International Intrigue.

Second, the Pacific. The C S I S Australia Chair released an interactive map of who is partnering with whom on security across the Pacific islands, which is useful for any conversation about where influence is shifting, with New Zealand heading to the polls. Via C S I S.

Third, two items to watch today. Brazil votes in its presidential runoff, with polls showing the two candidates tied, and Ukraine says its Starlink connectivity is still throttled, even as the American president claims it is fixed. Via International Intrigue.

Fourth, trade and tech policy. The White House has opened new trade negotiations on wind turbines, about seventy eight per cent of which are made in China, after a national security investigation, which could mean more tariffs. C S I S's critical minerals programme and its Japan Chair continue to frame the same worry as the yttrium story: dependence on a single supplier is now a security issue, not just a cost issue. Via International Intrigue and C S I S.

Fifth, Europe. Chancellor Merz confirmed that Germany is the target of intense espionage after the arrest of a Ukrainian national suspected of passing military secrets to Russia, and Uncharted Territories asks why France has been in chaos, after weeks in which tens of thousands vandalised and burned schools, cars and shops. Founders in Paris are bracing for more uncertainty. Via International Intrigue, Uncharted Territories and Sifted.

Sixth, a health security note. Nigeria has reported its first confirmed Ebola case, as the outbreak centred on Congo becomes the second biggest in history. Via International Intrigue.

Now, over to community, events and marketing.

Section five, community, events and marketing.

Where the week landed: the best ideas this week were small, intimate and cheap to copy.

First, one idea worth stealing. Priya Parker posed what she calls a Magical Question at a family dinner spanning three children, several adults and her seventy eight year old father: what is something specific your mother or father did that made you feel really loved as a child? A question works when everyone is interested in answering it and in hearing everyone else's answer, and this one asked each person to be a child again, which equalised the table. Her father told two stories she had never heard. Use it at your next mixed generation gathering, or at the opening of a community event. Via Priya Parker.

Second, a format signal. Setlog, a Japanese and Korean app that asks you to record two seconds an hour and shares the stitched day only with chosen friends, has reached about ten million users in under a year, a sign that people are shrinking from public feeds into trusted circles. Scan Club also flagged a new app for closed communities. Marketing Brew, meanwhile, notes that brands are turning to analogue photography. Via Scan Club and Marketing Brew.

Third, an event idea worth borrowing. Fukuoka Now runs a free, English language Kyushu Roundtable at C I C Fukuoka, with a small panel and a clear theme. The next, on the thirtieth of October, is titled Fukuoka's Next Chapter and asks what the city should prioritise after sixteen years under one mayor. A single question, a local venue and a limited number of seats make for a format any community manager can copy. Via Fukuoka Now.

Now, one interesting thing to close.

Section six, one interesting thing.

A new paper traced how long wealth shocks persist in England, from seventeen hundred to today. The question is whether a windfall endures and even compounds across generations, or fades. The answer, in the title, is that it fades in less than three generations, which implies far less of today's wealth traces back to events before nineteen hundred than most of us assume. Via Marginal Revolution.
