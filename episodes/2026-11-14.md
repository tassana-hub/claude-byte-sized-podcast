Good morning, and welcome to Byte-sized. It is Saturday the fourteenth of November, and today there are six sections, beginning, as ever, in Japan and the Asia Pacific.

Section one, Japan and Asia Pacific.

First, Japanese money is backing Europe's robots. Agile Robots, the Munich-based robotics company, has raised a five hundred million dollar round led by SoftBank, with Nvidia and Hitachi also taking part. Two of Japan's best-known industrial and investment names are in the same deal, which says something about where Japanese capital sees the next wave of automation coming from. For anyone in Tokyo talking to manufacturers, it is a reminder that Japan's large groups are now buying exposure to physical A I abroad, not only building it at home. Via Sifted.

Second, Europe and Asia are drifting closer. Bankers at the tech investment bank G P Bullhound recently spent two weeks in Seoul, Osaka and Singapore, and say European deep tech came up in almost every meeting. Their summary is neat: Asia has the factories and the customers, Europe has the inventions and is short of growth capital. The clearest example is Samsung, which has become one of European tech's kingmakers this year. Before twenty twenty-six it had led only two small rounds on the continent. This year Samsung Electronics itself led Mistral's three billion euro Series D and a two hundred million euro round for the A I infrastructure company Euclyd. If Korean and Japanese corporates keep writing cheques of this size, European founders will start building Asia into their plans from day one, and Japan has a chance to be the first stop rather than an afterthought. Via Sifted.

Third, a quieter story worth tracking. Indonesia's vice president has stepped down, which International Intrigue flags as leaving a possible power vacuum in one of the region's most overlooked but important countries. Indonesia is a major destination for Japanese investment in nickel, batteries and infrastructure, so any political instability in Jakarta is a live question for Japanese boardrooms. Details are thin for now, as International Intrigue is taking a short break and returns on Monday. Via International Intrigue.

And one for the China watchers. The C S I S Freeman Chair has released a podcast episode going behind the scenes of the Chinese Communist Party's Fifth Plenum, the meeting where Xi Jinping moved to strengthen the Party and his own control of it. The panel, including the political scientist Victor Shih, looks at why plenums still matter under one-man rule and what this one means for relations with the United States. Alongside it, the C S I S Korea Chair is running a live session taking stock of the American and South Korean alliance. Both are useful background for Japan's position between Washington, Beijing and Seoul. Via C S I S.

Now, over to A I and tech.

Section two, A I and tech.

The story of the week has reached its end point: Jensen Huang has resigned as chief executive of Nvidia, and from its board, with immediate effect. He co-founded the company thirty-three years ago and took it from gaming graphics cards to the hardware behind modern A I. The exit followed his admission of infidelity after reports linking him to a woman American officials suspect of being a Chinese intelligence asset, then a New York Times report of a second relationship with an Nvidia executive, and, after the resignation, a Reuters report of a third with another employee. The finance chief, Colette Kress, becomes interim chief executive, and long-time director Harvey Jones becomes Nvidia's first ever board chair. The board says the product roadmap is unaffected, and analysts point out it is largely set through twenty twenty-eight, so the real uncertainty is about the next big architecture bets and new deals. President Trump has publicly suggested Tim Cook for the job. Nvidia reports quarterly results on the seventeenth of November, and House committee chairs want a closed-door briefing from Huang. Via The Rundown A I, T L D R A I and T L D R Hardware.

Second, Google is having a good week while its biggest rival in chips is in turmoil. It released Gemini 4 Hydrogen, a fast, efficient model aimed at coding and agent work, priced at one dollar fifty per million input tokens. It opened pre-orders for its first Gemini-powered smart glasses, built with Samsung, with frames from Warby Parker and Gentle Monster starting at three hundred and ninety-five dollars. They go on sale in December in the United States and South Korea, and Google is pitching privacy as the selling point: bystanders' faces are blurred before Gemini sees them, and recording stops if the light is covered. Meta currently ships about four in five A I glasses, so this is a direct challenge. And Google DeepMind released AlphaGenome 2, which predicts what stretches of D N A do in specific cell types, roughly doubling the share of disease-causing variants it can identify compared with the first version. Via The Rundown A I, T L D R A I and Not Boring.

Third, the agent economy keeps creeping into real transactions. Replit's coding agent can now buy business supplies and run Google Ads campaigns, with the owner approving the final total before payment. Meta's assistant, Muse, can now phone businesses on your behalf for all paid subscribers. And Travelport completed a live flight purchase made by an A I agent, using existing airline distribution and payment systems. For enterprises, the question is shifting from whether agents will transact to who controls the rails when they do. Via T L D R A I, T L D R Founders and PhocusWire.

And a forensic footnote with policy weight: an analysis of publicly released models suggests that DeepSeek, Moonshot A I, MiniMax and Ant Group have had access to Nvidia's export-controlled Blackwell chips, based on numerical fingerprints in six models released since April. It does not establish where the chips were or whether any rules were broken, but it lands awkwardly in a week when Nvidia's China ties are under the microscope. Via T L D R A I.

Section three, startups and venture.

First, what Huang's exit means for European startups. Nvidia has been the most active backer of megarounds in Europe, joining thirteen rounds in the past twelve months, including Revolut and Mistral, and Huang toured Europe only a month ago pledging billions. Not everyone is worried. One American investor told Sifted that Huang's departure does not change the demand picture: hyperscalers, national A I programmes and the big labs are still short of capacity, with budgets locked in for years. Meanwhile, the defence money keeps flowing in Europe: Uforce raised seven hundred million dollars backed by the United Kingdom's National Wealth Fund, and Munich-based S E three raised thirty million dollars led by Accel for voice-controlled drone software. Via Sifted.

Second, a shift in how venture firms find deals. Sequoia has just closed its scout programme after seventeen years. At the same moment, Northzone, the investor behind Spotify and Klarna, has launched one, called Northstars, with twenty-five operators from A I-native companies such as ElevenLabs and Black Forest Labs. Most are not senior executives but chiefs of staff, heads of engineering and talent leads, the people who actually see which teams are good. Via Sifted.

Third, two sharp lessons on the business of software from T L D R Founders. A well-designed business agent can double what a customer pays each year, because it taps labour budgets rather than software budgets, while most bolt-on A I features barely move the price. And a cautionary tale: the founder of Groove, a profitable five million dollar support software company, has stopped selling new subscriptions, because A I is shrinking his customers' support teams and with them the number of seats they pay for. Seat-based pricing is quietly becoming a liability. Via T L D R Founders.

Section four, policy and geopolitics.

The Nvidia story is now formally a national security matter in Washington. Congressional committee chairs are seeking a closed-door briefing from Huang, and the board, which earlier said it had no reason to believe security or company secrets were compromised, will now pick a successor who inherits Nvidia's role at the centre of American export controls. Read alongside the evidence of Blackwell chips in Chinese models, expect pressure for tighter enforcement, which matters to Japanese chip equipment makers and to Tokyo's own economic security policy. Via The Rundown A I and T L D R A I.

Second, the international picture remains crowded. International Intrigue reports early tension in the peace talks between Ukraine, the United States and Russia, and says Vice President Vance has been removed from the American talks with Iran. Separately, Ukraine's President Zelenskyy says that SpaceX's Starshield service has been limited so that terminals moving faster than about one hundred and sixty kilometres an hour lose their link, which breaks its use for drone interception. SpaceX denies any change, and it is unclear who would have imposed such a limit. That is a reminder of how much battlefield capability now depends on a single private company. Via International Intrigue and T L D R Hardware.

Now, over to community, events and marketing.

Section five, community, events and marketing.

One idea worth stealing comes from Priya Parker, writing about traditions that no longer fit. Families, friend groups and teams change, but their rituals often do not, and a gathering that once brought people together can start asking too much of them. Her first question is the useful one: what is still precious here? Before changing the format of anything recurring, whether it is a family dinner, an annual offsite or a community's monthly meetup, work out what meaning people are actually protecting. Sometimes the form has to change so the meaning can survive. If you run a long-standing event, try putting that question to your regulars before you redesign it. Via Priya Parker.

And a second, tactical one from the go-to-market world. The startup Needle booked three hundred and twenty-one sales calls on LinkedIn in two months by writing only to people who had just commented on posts about the problem it solves. Those people replied about a third of the time, against roughly one in ten for followers of the company page. A I found the comments and drafted each note, but a person approved every message, and the team kept that human step after hundreds of attempts to remove it. For community builders, the lesson travels: reach people at the moment they have shown they care, and keep a human in the loop. Via T L D R Founders.

Now, over to one interesting thing.

Section six, one interesting thing.

Next Wednesday, Voyager 1 will become the first human-made object to be one light-day from Earth, forty-nine years after it was launched. Its signals will take a full twenty-four hours to reach us. And yet, as Not Boring points out, after all that time it has covered only about one thirty-four-trillionth of the width of the observable universe, so at this pace the full journey would take around one point six seven quadrillion years. It is a fine thing to hold in mind: a triumph of engineering, and a reminder of how much is still left to explore. Via Not Boring.
