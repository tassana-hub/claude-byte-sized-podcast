Section one, Japan and Asia Pacific.

First, Nippon Steel gets to work on an American icon. After its long and politically fraught takeover of U S Steel, the Japanese steelmaker is now trying to bring the American company's ageing plants back to life, starting with its oldest mill outside Pittsburgh. This is the part of the deal that never made headlines during the fight in Washington: the slow, expensive work of modernising old American capacity with Japanese capital and methods. If it works, it becomes the template people in Tokyo point to when they say Japanese investment is the friendly way to reshore American industry. Via Why Is This Interesting.

Second, a sobering number on Taiwan. A new C S I S study by Seth Jones and Daniel Byman, with Eric Heginbotham, asks whether China's People's Liberation Army is really ready for a fight. Their headline figure: if an invasion of Taiwan were launched this year, China's entire amphibious fleet would come to fewer than one hundred ships, backed by about eighty landing craft. That fleet is the centre of gravity of any invasion, and it is a small set of targets set against the large stocks of missiles and drones held by Taiwan and the United States. For Japan, whose southern islands sit right next to any such operation, the study is a useful corrective to the idea that an invasion is simply a matter of when Beijing decides. Via C S I S.

Second, stop describing your style to A I, and show it instead. Every's head of evaluations, Mike Taylor, has been trying to get A I to write like him since twenty twenty. What finally worked was not a description of his voice but a test. He took a couple of dozen of his own published pieces, stripped each one down to a plain brief, had a model draft from each brief, and then asked a separate A I judge which of two drafts sounded more like the original, swapping the order to cancel out bias. He kept only the changes to his writing instructions that raised the score. The striking result is that a writing skill tuned to one particular model can beat a stronger model running generic instructions. He now goes from an opinion to a finished piece in about an hour instead of a day. Via Every.

PitchBook counts two hundred and nineteen and a half billion dollars of venture deals involving Nvidia so far this year, more than a quarter of all global venture value. Section four, policy and geopolitics.

First, the memory chip squeeze. C S I S warns that the United States faces an increasingly sharp shortage of memory chips, because the few suppliers are prioritising high bandwidth memory for the hyperscalers building A I data centres, which now take up to seventy per cent of available capacity. That leaves ordinary electronics makers short and tempts them towards Chinese memory, which C S I S argues brings security risks of its own. For anyone in procurement, memory is the quiet bottleneck behind next year's hardware prices. Via C S I S.

Second, Africa is shopping for new partners. American direct investment in Africa has fallen from a peak of sixty-nine billion dollars in twenty fourteen to under thirty-eight billion last year, while Chinese lending collapsed from nearly twenty-nine billion in twenty sixteen to about two billion in twenty twenty-four. C S I S says that gap is being filled by middle powers, especially in the Gulf. It is the same story Japan is playing out in Southeast Asia: when the great powers pull back, the middle powers with money and patience move in. Via C S I S.

And third, will A I growth reach the developing world? A new National Bureau of Economic Research paper models a world where A I services are traded globally, and warns that poorer countries could be left behind, lacking the complementary skills to use A I and being priced out by lower productivity. Tyler Cowen pushes back: A I already improves rainfall prediction and crop management, so it will not only help rich-country lawyers, and law in poorer countries is so bound up with politics that local lawyers are protected anyway. He expects the gap between rich and poor countries may widen, but poor countries still end up better off than without A I. Via Marginal Revolution.

Section five, community, events and marketing.

One idea worth stealing: give every talk a take-home tool. Every has turned each filmed talk from its first conference, Thesis twenty twenty-seven, into a skill file that attendees can install in their A I agent and use to apply the speaker's method to their own work. One, from a session on dividing work between people and agents, uses what they call a compound sandwich: you decide what to build at the start and judge the result at the end, and agents do the work in between. There are twenty-three in all. For anyone running events, it is a neat answer to the old problem that conference talks are forgotten by Monday: package each talk as something people can use, not just rewatch. Via Every.

And one for community builders. Pam Warhurst co-founded Incredible Edible, which links more than one hundred and twenty groups across the United Kingdom and over seven hundred worldwide, in school gardens, orchards, hospital grounds and high streets. The lesson is in the Do Lectures' own summary: one local act, a global movement. Start with something visible that anyone can copy, and let the copying be the growth strategy. Via The Do Lectures.

Now, over to one interesting thing.

Section six, one interesting thing.

Daughters may help keep parents alive. Using Danish records, researchers compared parents of only children who had a stroke at the same age and differed only in whether their child was a son or a daughter. Five years later, parents of daughters were one and a half percentage points more likely to be alive, and daughters were more likely to move nearer, less likely to be married and had fewer children. The surprising part is that parents of daughters also used more professional rehabilitation, which suggests that family care does not replace formal care so much as help people actually use it. Via Marginal Revolution.
