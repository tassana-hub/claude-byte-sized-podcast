Section one, Japan and Asia Pacific.

First, the American and Chinese economic relationship, seen from the inside. The C S I S Chair in Chinese Business and Economics has published a white paper from its twelfth year of the U S China Global Economic Order Dialogue, a small, closed-door group of American, Chinese and international experts. Section two, A I and tech.

The biggest story in the industry this week has taken another turn. Huang has run the company since he co-founded it in nineteen ninety-three, has roughly sixty direct reports, and is effectively the face of the A I chip business, so there is no obvious playbook for replacing him. Third, the agents are arriving at the front door, and some businesses are locking it. United Airlines is blocking unauthorised A I agents from its app, website and outside booking channels, citing account security, accidental purchases and federal rules that require a human to sign off on certain steps. Contrast that with Expedia and Booking, which are partnering with agents, and Andreessen Horowitz, which this week described a coming business-to-agent economy, noting that Shopify says orders arriving from A I search grew nearly thirteenfold in a year. And a quick one for anyone running enterprise A I projects: PayPal says it raised the accuracy of its internal A I assistant from forty-three to ninety-five per cent without changing the model at all. Via T L D R Fintech.

Section three, startups and venture.

Europe's chip startups are suddenly flush. Fractile, a British company designing chips to run A I models in data centres, has confirmed a five hundred and forty million pound round backed by the United Kingdom's Sovereign A I fund and the British Business Bank, the largest semiconductor round Europe has ever seen. Sifted's data shows European chip startups have raised about three point two five billion euros so far this year, almost four times the whole of last year, with most of it going to challengers to Nvidia in inference. In the same week, Nvidia's venture arm and Unity joined a seven hundred million dollar round for Emulate, a London-based DeepMind spin-off. The flip side: Nscale may delay its planned thirty-five billion dollar listing until next year after the global sell-off in tech stocks. Via Sifted.

Second, Mark Cuban's view of the bubble. In a long interview summarised by The VC Corner, Cuban calls himself the biggest proponent of A I there is, but warns that the United States could get into a debt bubble because of how much the hyperscalers are borrowing. The VC Corner cites reporting that the five biggest issued one hundred and twenty-one billion dollars of corporate bonds last year, against an average of twenty-eight billion a year in the five years before. Cuban's practical advice is more useful than his macro view: start with one narrow, repeating task that nobody has time to staff. His own first agent watches for lawsuits and contract bids by large healthcare companies, then drafts freedom of information requests for the contracts. Via The VC Corner.

Third, a reality check on software pricing. Research covered by T L D R Founders finds software-as-a-service renewal prices rising eight to twelve per cent a year, with aggressive vendors pushing fifteen to twenty-five per cent, and Canva's Teams plan up more than three hundred per cent. Companies that start negotiating renewals ninety days early save, on average, almost half. Worth passing to whoever owns your tool budget. Via T L D R Founders.

Section four, policy and geopolitics.

The Nvidia leadership crisis is also a national security story, and that is how it will be discussed in Washington and Tokyo. Members of Congress are pressing for Huang's exit specifically because of the alleged intelligence links, at a moment when Nvidia's chips sit at the centre of American export controls on China. Whoever leads Nvidia next will inherit not just a company but a policy portfolio, and allies such as Japan, whose own chip strategy leans heavily on American partners, will be watching for any shift in how strictly the company handles controls. Via The Rundown A I.

Second, regulators are learning to live with A I rather than fence it off. The American Office of the Comptroller of the Currency has issued a supervisory framework for how banks use advanced and agentic A I, telling examiners not to treat A I as a separate risk category and saying banks need not set a separate A I risk appetite. It calls this a technology-neutral approach. For anyone in conversation with Japanese financial regulators, it is a clear signal of the direction of travel in the United States. Via T L D R Fintech.

One idea worth stealing: the adult goodie bag. Scan Club reports that party favours for grown-ups have become a TikTok obsession, with hosts sending guests home with everything from a pinch of good salt to custom-wrapped lighters. The insight is that in a digital age people want tangible proof they were there. For anyone running a meetup, a dinner or a conference side event, a small, thoughtful take-home object is a cheap way to make the evening memorable and to get it photographed and shared. The same newsletter notes brands leaning into visibly human craft for their holiday campaigns, from handmade puppets to stop-motion and paper animation, as a counterweight to A I generated content. Via Scan Club.

Now, over to one interesting thing.

Section six, one interesting thing.

Tomas Pueyo asks what happens to professions when A I makes the work ten times cheaper, and offers a neat bit of arithmetic using American tax returns. Professionals already file about eighty-eight million of the roughly one hundred and sixty-six million returns each year, so even if cheaper advice brought in every remaining filer, the number of customers could rise by less than ninety per cent. If prices fall by ninety per cent, the industry's total revenue would still shrink by at least four fifths. Whether a job grows or shrinks, he argues, comes down to that single question: does the fall in price unlock enough new demand to make up for it. Via Uncharted Territories.
