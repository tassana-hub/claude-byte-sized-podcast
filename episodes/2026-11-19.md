Good morning, and welcome to Byte-sized for Thursday the nineteenth of November. Six sections today, starting as always in Japan and the Asia Pacific.

Section one, Japan and Asia Pacific.

First, the three-way summit and the view from Tokyo. President Trump meets Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin together today, the first ever sit-down between the leaders of the three countries, with nuclear non-proliferation officially on the agenda. Xi used his warm-up talks with his "old friend" Putin to say Moscow and Beijing should lead the move towards a multipolar world. International Intrigue doubts any meaningful deal will come out of it, but argues that Xi and Putin still win simply by having an American president sit at the table and appear to accept their idea of great-power spheres of influence. The Council on Foreign Relations, flagged in the CSIS nuclear policy briefing, adds that a breakthrough on bringing China into arms control is unlikely. For Japan, that framing is the worry: any hint of carving up regions between big powers leaves allies like Tokyo and Seoul wondering where they sit. Via International Intrigue and CSIS.

Second, what it means for North Korea. The CSIS Korea Chair, Victor Cha, recorded a special episode of his podcast, The Impossible State, with the North Korea scholar Andrei Lankov and the Russia specialist Maria Snegovaya, on what the summit could mean for Pyongyang. The questions they focus on are what Putin hopes to gain, whether a separate Trump and Kim Jong Un meeting is back in play, and how the summit could reshape Russia's growing military partnership with North Korea. For Japan, any Trump and Kim channel that skips over abductions and short-range missiles is a familiar fear, so this is worth watching closely over the next few days. Via CSIS.

Third, Malaysia starts trading access to rare earths. On a state visit to Kuala Lumpur, President Macron raised France's ties with Malaysia to a strategic partnership and won an exemption from Malaysia's strict ban on exporting raw rare earths. Malaysia brought in the ban in twenty twenty-four to force more processing at home, but it created a bottleneck of stranded material, so the government is now easing the rules case by case for partners who invest locally. A French firm, Carester, announced a large local joint venture in July. Japan is one of the biggest players in Malaysian rare earths and has spent years building supply outside China, so the signal for Tokyo is clear: in Southeast Asia, access to critical minerals now comes in exchange for investment in local factories. Via International Intrigue.

And fourth, a political crisis in Mongolia. President Ukhnaa says he will resign over accusations, which he denies, that he took twenty-five million dollars in kickbacks from a huge railway project carrying coking coal into China. Mongolia has also had three prime ministers in nine months. That instability makes it harder for the landlocked country to keep up its "third neighbour" policy of balancing China and Russia with partners such as Japan, America and South Korea. Via International Intrigue.

Now, over to A I and tech.

Section two, A I and tech.

First, OpenAI's new models get rough reviews. OpenAI has released G P T six point two Astra, its top model, and G P T six point two Sol, a cheaper everyday model, in ChatGPT, its Codex coding tool and its developer platform. On the independent Artificial Analysis rankings, Astra now sits second, behind only Anthropic's Fable five point two, and Sol matches September's top model at roughly fifteen per cent of the cost per task. But there is no real improvement in coding, Sol actually slips backwards there, and some early testers were scathing, with one calling it the worst model OpenAI has released. OpenAI's own safety assessment also puts Astra at its "high" threshold for A I self-improvement, a first for a public release. The Rundown's read is that the timing is awkward, landing days before Anthropic goes public. Via The Rundown A I and T L D R A I.

Second, Nvidia keeps growing without its founder or China. Nvidia reported third-quarter revenue of about one hundred and sixteen billion dollars, with data-centre sales up a hundred and ten per cent, and guided to around a hundred and thirty billion dollars next quarter while assuming zero data-centre revenue from China. On the same call, the board chair said the company does not intend to bring back Jensen Huang, despite Huang's first public statement admitting "serious mistakes" and saying he would be honoured to return. Nvidia has set up an executive committee to oversee export-control compliance, and Huang testifies before Congress on the first of December. Via T L D R Hardware, The Rundown A I and International Intrigue.

Third, A I finds a hole in Java, and NATO buys in. A startup called Calif says OpenAI's cyber model helped it find a critical flaw in Java, the programming language running inside nine in ten of America's largest companies, which let it break into a login system used by NASA and CERN. Oracle shipped a fix on Tuesday. On the same day, OpenAI revealed that NATO's technology and cyber agency is using its security models to protect allied networks, and that Europe's cybersecurity agency has already used them to find four flaws in E U systems. After a month of attacks and outages, this is the clearest sign yet that A I is now on both sides of cyber defence. Via The Rundown A I and T L D R InfoSec.

And fourth, the cheaper end of the market gets crowded. Microsoft released seven new models built to be good enough at a low price, including a coding model in GitHub Copilot that reaches about ninety-eight per cent of OpenAI's coding quality at about forty per cent of the price. Google's Gemini app can now call businesses, book appointments and shop within a spending limit you set. For anyone buying A I for a team, the practical point is that "good and cheap" options are multiplying faster than the headline frontier models. Via T L D R A I.

Now, over to startups and venture.

Section three, startups and venture.

First, a five billion dollar round for A I drug discovery. Isomorphic Labs, the Alphabet-backed spin-out from DeepMind, has raised five billion dollars, co-led by Greenoaks and Sequoia, only six months after a two point one billion dollar round, at a reported valuation of around fifty billion dollars. The money pays for its drug-design engine, new foundational biology models, and its first programmes heading towards clinical trials, with partners including Eli Lilly, Novartis and Johnson and Johnson. Via Sifted and T L D R Hardware.

Second, robots and agents keep pulling in money. Genesis A I raised a five hundred million dollar Series A at a three and a half billion dollar valuation to build Eno, a wheeled robot with no head that skips the human form altogether. In London, Model M L, which builds personal A I agents for bankers, consultants and investors, raised two hundred and fifty million dollars led by General Atlantic and QED. And in Paris, Remanence raised a thirty million dollar seed round to turn the work traces of A I agents into specialised models whose weights the customer owns. Sifted also notes Europe is bracing for Anthropic's listing this week, with many hoping a strong debut frees up capital for the continent's own A I companies, and others fearing it could trigger a correction. Via Sifted and T L D R Hardware.

Third, the "SaaS-pocalypse" may be overstated. Software shares lost an estimated two trillion dollars earlier this year on fears that A I would replace business apps. But Stripe's new weekly index of non-A I software companies billing through its platform shows revenue up more than thirty per cent on a year ago. The caveats matter: most of those firms sell self-serve products paid by card, not the big enterprise names whose shares fell, and companies that shut down simply drop out of the index. Still, it is a useful corrective to the idea that ordinary software businesses have stopped growing. Via T L D R Founders.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

Section four, policy and geopolitics.

First, the biggest banking leak in history. A consortium of more than fifty newsrooms has published Inside Deutsche Bank, built on fifteen million leaked emails, chats and audits, which is larger than the Panama Papers. Among the early findings: by twenty twenty, just seventeen clients on an American Treasury list generated half the bank's private Russian revenue; payments tied to suspected North Korean front companies went through, some with "North Korea" written in the transfer; and in twenty nineteen the bank moved around twenty-two billion dollars a year of Colombian business without filing a single suspicious activity report. Deutsche shares are down nearly eight per cent this week, and the bank says these issues date back many years. For anyone with European banking relationships, expect compliance teams to get much stricter, very quickly. Via International Intrigue.

Second, export controls: more enforcement, less help. A CSIS commentary, written with the University of Georgia, says the American Commerce Department is moving from engaging with industry to simply enforcing export controls. Rules are changing through website postings and letters to individual companies rather than formal regulations, penalties are rising, and lists of risky buyers are not being kept up to date, so firms have to make more judgement calls themselves. The authors propose clearer guidance, shared industry best practice, more trained compliance staff, and A I screening tools with humans checking the results. Small and mid-sized firms will feel this most, including Japanese suppliers selling into American supply chains. Via CSIS.

Now, over to community, events and marketing.

Section five, community, events and marketing.

One idea worth stealing, from Ramp: brand as infrastructure. Ramp's in-house designers kept getting asked to remake the same decks and one-pagers for other teams. So they handed that work to a Slack agent that builds new materials from approved examples the designers create, with automated checks for broken layouts and missing fonts before anything goes back. One contractor automated about ninety per cent of the company's whitepaper work in her second week. The trick is the loop: every new hand-made piece becomes another example the system learns from, so the designers spend their time on things nobody has made before. Via T L D R Founders.

And a second idea, from David Spinks: build a village, not just a sounding board. In the middle of a product launch, with two of his three children ill, Spinks says what saved the week was neighbours and family who actually took work off his plate. He is applying that to his new small-group masterminds, of six to ten people, where the norm is to roll up your sleeves, for example by reviewing each other's sponsorship decks or showing someone how you set up your sales system, rather than just giving advice. For anyone running a community, the test is simple: do your members only listen to each other, or do they actually help each other get work done? Via David Spinks.

Now, over to one interesting thing.

Section six, one interesting thing.

When MTV switched off its round-the-clock music video channels, a solo creator known as Flex rebuilt the experience in forty-eight hours from an apartment in Albania. The free, ad-free site has grown from about twenty thousand to more than a hundred and thirteen thousand hand-picked videos, with software doing the checks and a human deciding what goes on each channel and in what order. Measuring the archive has thrown up some lovely findings: Def Jam videos cut every two point seven seven seconds on average, while Sub Pop's grunge videos really do have the least movement of any major independent label. The point, Flex says, is not a library you search, but a channel you leave on that takes you somewhere. Via Why Is This Interesting.
