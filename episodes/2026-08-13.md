Hello, and welcome to Byte-sized for Thursday, the thirteenth of August. Six sections today, starting with Japan and Asia-Pacific.

Sony and Taiwan's TSMC are in talks to build a joint image sensor plant in Kumamoto, in southern Japan, in a deal reportedly worth six point four billion dollars. The investment adds to Kumamoto's rapid transformation into one of the most important chip manufacturing hubs in Asia, building on TSMC's earlier decision to put fabrication plants there. For Japan, it's another marker of how the country has positioned itself as the trusted manufacturing partner of choice as global chipmakers diversify away from single points of failure elsewhere in the region. Via TLDR.

North Korea fired a ballistic missile toward the sea off its east coast this week, days ahead of major joint military exercises between South Korea and the United States that Pyongyang has long condemned. Both South Korean and Japanese authorities confirmed the launch. It's a familiar pattern of North Korean signalling before allied drills, but it lands against a wider regional backdrop of persistent worry about Beijing's own military build-up, from expanding submarine fleets to a nuclear stockpile that keeps growing. Via CSIS.

Chinese AI chip designer Moore Threads has filed for a Hong Kong stock listing to raise capital for global research and development. The company, founded by former Nvidia executives, is positioning itself alongside Huawei and Cambricon to capture China's domestic AI data centre hardware market while Nvidia faces continued American export restrictions. It's a reminder that even as leading Chinese AI labs still lean heavily on Nvidia chips to train their frontier models, the domestic alternative stack is being built out with real capital behind it. Via TLDR.

Microsoft is also expanding its footprint in the region, negotiating with TSMC to reserve manufacturing capacity for more than three hundred thousand of its next-generation Maia AI accelerator chips. It's another sign of how central Taiwanese foundries remain to the entire AI buildout, whatever the political tension hanging over the island. Via TLDR.

Now, over to AI and tech.

Nvidia is trying to turn AI computing power into Wall Street's next asset class. The company announced financing platforms with Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs and KKR, aiming to raise more than five hundred billion dollars in outside capital to build so-called AI factories, the data centres that train and run AI models. Chief executive Jensen Huang is pitching this compute capacity as a durable, reusable investment, but critics point out these are non-binding agreements rather than signed deals, and the entire pitch rests on what the hardware would actually be worth if a paying customer walked away. Via TLDR.

There's been a wave of leadership change at the top of the industry this week. OpenAI's long-serving chief operating officer, Brad Lightcap, is leaving to start a new venture, the latest in a run of senior departures as the company prepares for its stock market listing. Over at Google, Koray Kavukcuoglu has been promoted to head Google DeepMind, taking charge of Gemini's development as the company chases OpenAI and Anthropic, just as Google announced that the Gemini app has passed one billion monthly active users. Via TLDR.

Anthropic says it will start embedding an invisible watermark into text generated by Claude, though it hasn't disclosed exactly how. Security researchers reckon it works as a subtle statistical bias in word choice, which should survive ordinary copying and pasting, though determined attackers could likely strip it out by rewriting or regenerating the text. It's a small but telling move in the industry's slow build toward being able to tell human and machine writing apart, at exactly the point when that distinction is getting harder to make. Via TLDR.

Coding tool Cursor looks set to expand beyond writing code and into reviewing it too. The company appears to be preparing a wider launch of a platform called Cursor Review, which syncs with repositories pulled in from GitHub and runs an automated pull request pipeline that flags when a human's judgement is needed, letting people and AI agents work through open pull requests together. It's part of a broader shift in how coding tools are positioning themselves, not just as autocomplete, but as a genuine second pair of eyes on a codebase. Via TLDR.

Now, over to startups and venture.

More companies became unicorns in the first half of this year than in the whole of last year combined. A hundred and ninety five companies crossed the billion dollar valuation mark in the first six months of twenty twenty six, according to Crunchbase data, with robotics and AI leading the surge. China's DeepSeek and Seychelles-based crypto exchange OKX were among the standouts, reaching fifty billion and twenty five billion dollars respectively, with the United States and China dominating overall. Via TLDR.

Robotics startups alone have raised twenty three billion dollars so far this year, underlining just how much investor appetite has shifted toward physical AI and automation, on top of the software wave that came before it. Via TLDR.

In Europe, a trio of former LocalGlobe investors are reportedly in early talks to raise a new venture fund, while Nick Blair, the youngest son of former UK prime minister Tony Blair, is seeking two hundred million dollars for a new defence technology startup. Both point to the same trend: European venture capital is increasingly chasing two of its hottest categories right now, experienced operators striking out on their own, and defence technology drawing serious money as the continent rearms. Via Sifted.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

Iran's supreme leader, Mojtaba Khamenei, has resurfaced after months out of public view, ending speculation that he was gravely injured or even dead following this year's war with Israel and the United States. Almost immediately afterwards, decrees issued in his name filled six of the most powerful military posts left vacant by that war, installing hardline loyalists across the Revolutionary Guard, the regular armed forces, and the Basij militia that enforces internal repression. Analysts at International Intrigue read the appointments as a clear signal: Iran's surviving hardliners are consolidating control at home and showing zero interest in external compromise, whatever the truth about Mojtaba's health. Via International Intrigue.

Pakistan's foreign minister has described a new defence pact his country signed with Saudi Arabia and Turkey as purely defensive and open to other nations in the region. The agreement adds another layer to the web of Middle Eastern security arrangements being renegotiated in the aftermath of the Iran war, as regional powers hedge against future instability without committing fully to any single bloc. Via CSIS.

Back in Washington, the general running the Golden Dome missile defence programme has warned it is in real trouble, after the White House opted to seek most of its twenty twenty seven funding through a contested budget manoeuvre rather than a straightforward appropriation from Congress. Without reliable funding, he warned bluntly, there is no Golden Dome. It's a reminder that even flagship defence programmes rest on unglamorous questions of budget process as much as on technology. Via CSIS.

Now, over to community, events and marketing.

One idea worth stealing: tell your best customer stories out loud, not just in writing. For the third year running, the dating app Hinge is reviving its "No Ordinary Love" campaign, which tells real stories of couples who met on the platform, but this year it's leaning hard into audio, including its first full audiobook, aimed at reaching Gen Z daters who increasingly prefer to listen rather than read. The brand's marketing chief says audio lets them tell deeper, more emotional stories than a written format allows. If your best customer stories are still sitting in a written case study, it might be worth asking whether they'd land harder read aloud. Via Marketing Brew.

One idea worth stealing: keep the ordinary rituals going, especially when things are hard. Sifted spoke to founders and employees at Ukrainian startups about how they keep team morale alive under the constant strain of war, and the answers were strikingly ordinary: quiz nights, picnics, flexible time off after a bad night of attacks, and a lot of explicit permission to say when things aren't okay. One founder said going on holiday abroad actually made him feel worse, because he lost the sense of control that comes from being with his team. It's a reminder that under real pressure, the basics of team culture matter more, not less. Via Sifted.

Now, over to one interesting thing.

Researchers studying more than a decade of Russian survey data found something uncomfortable: life satisfaction among ordinary Russians rose significantly after the twenty twenty two invasion of Ukraine, even after accounting for people's material circumstances. The gains were largest among ethnic Russians and in military-industrial regions, smaller in Moscow and St Petersburg, and sharply lower near the front line, a pattern the researchers say is hard to explain away as people simply telling pollsters what the regime wants to hear. By twenty twenty four, though, more people were refusing to answer the survey at all, which the authors suspect means the picture is quietly getting harder to read. Via Marginal Revolution.
