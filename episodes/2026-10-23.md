Good morning, and welcome to Byte-sized for Friday, the twenty third of October. Six sections today, and we begin, as ever, in Japan and the Asia Pacific.

Section one, Japan and Asia Pacific.

First, a social app that is booming in Japan and South Korea. Setlog is less than a year old and already has around ten million users. It prompts you to record a two second clip every hour, then stitches the day into a short day in the life video that you share only with chosen groups of friends. No editing is allowed, so what people see is your actual day, not your highlight reel. Scan Club reads it as a sign that online social life keeps shrinking from public feeds towards small, trusted circles, and a reminder to look beyond North America for what might arrive next. Via Scan Club.

Second, the C S I S Japan Chair has rounded up its recent work. The themes are telling: how Japanese elite opinion is shifting on nuclear weapons, what Prime Minister Takaichi's approval ratings mean for her second year in office, the implications of Japan's new national intelligence bureau, food security with a fresh look at rice, and the potential for American and Japanese cooperation in creative industries. It also highlights discussions on Japan as a maritime power facing China. If you are talking to government people about Japan, those are the live conversations. Via C S I S.

Now, over to AI and tech.

Section two, AI and tech.

First, the story of the week for anyone who runs a website. Two critical flaws in P H P, the language behind roughly seventy per cent of the web, were found with help from an OpenAI model, and patches have now been pushed. The bugs are around a decade old and let attackers take over servers without logging in. The American National Security Agency has urged anyone who cannot patch immediately to take systems offline, and government sites in Finland, Germany and the Netherlands did just that. Cloudflare says early attacks are emerging, and OpenAI says more significant finds are still being fixed. The lesson: old code is more fragile than anyone assumed, and AI is now finding the holes faster than people can. Via The Rundown A I and T L D R.

Second, Anthropic has launched Claude Duet, a personal agent app that can act on your behalf around the clock, even when your devices are off. It carries over memory and connectors from Claude, and can check out through Shop Pay and Stripe's Link using one time virtual cards. Anything involving deleting, paying or sending a message needs your sign off first. It is rolling out in waves to waitlisted American Pro and Max subscribers. Via The Rundown A I.

Third, OpenAI has acquired Orchestra Bio, a platform that helps biopharma teams coordinate drug development, and will fold it into its life sciences effort to build agents that connect scientific discoveries to clinical studies and regulatory work. Separately, OpenAI is selling its models and Codex through Google Cloud. Via T L D R and The Rundown A I.

Fourth, a quick one for software teams. Blacksmith, a continuous integration platform, hit a one billion dollar valuation after a sixty six million dollar round led by Sequoia, just two months after its previous raise. Its revenue has grown seventeen fold as A I coding agents generate more code that needs building and testing. Via T L D R.

Now, over to start-ups and venture.

Section three, start-ups and venture.

First, fintech funding is concentrating. Globally it fell twenty one per cent year on year to ten point six billion dollars in the third quarter, while deal count dropped thirty one per cent to its lowest level since at least twenty eighteen. Investors are favouring payments, stablecoin rails and agentic commerce, and asking everyone else for proof of durable demand. In that climate, Ramp, fresh from raising one point eight five billion dollars at a sixty billion dollar valuation, has applied for a national trust bank charter in the United States. Via T L D R.

Second, Europe keeps minting big rounds. Legal A I company Legora raised five hundred and fifty million dollars at an eight point eight billion dollar valuation, and the finance planning firm Pigment raised two hundred and fifty million in a Series E led by the European Union's Scaleup Europe Fund. Also, Yann LeCun and Mistral's Arthur Mensch backed Enact Intelligence's thirty million dollar pre seed, said to be Switzerland's largest. Via Sifted.

Third, Salesforce has agreed to buy the customer engagement platform Braze for about five point six six billion dollars in cash, and Braze shares jumped more than fifty per cent. Via T L D R.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

Section four, policy and geopolitics.

First, Britain's competition regulator has proposed much lower fees and fewer technical restrictions for companies that want to use the iPhone's contactless payment technology, a direct attempt to loosen Apple's grip on tap to pay. Via T L D R.

Second, big banks are now industrial policy players. JPMorgan says it has invested five billion dollars of its own equity in companies tied to American national security, across advanced manufacturing, energy, supply chains and defence technology, on top of around two hundred and thirty billion dollars financed through its wider initiative. Expect more of this kind of private capital lining up behind government priorities. Via T L D R.

Now, over to community, events and marketing.

Section five, community, events and marketing. Two ideas worth stealing.

First, from Scan Club, the unusual power of a pinned post. A court has ordered an influencer to pin a public apology to the top of her feeds for three and a half years after losing a defamation case. Whatever you think of the case, it shows how valuable that top slot is, and how it can be both an asset and a liability. The takeaway: treat whatever sits first on your page, your community space or your event site as your most valuable real estate, and choose it deliberately. Via Scan Club.

Second, from Skift, a lesson in community resilience. Attendees of the Grace Hopper Celebration, an event that was cancelled, are rallying to create new programmes of their own. When an official gathering disappears, the community can still build one. One idea worth stealing: keep a small group of members who could run the event without you. Via Skift.

Finally, section six, one interesting thing.

From Why Is This Interesting, a Japanese car with a lesson in restraint. Toyota's Century, first built in nineteen sixty seven, is a limousine favoured by Japan's boardrooms, ministries and the Imperial household. It is now a standalone brand and, from twenty twenty eight, will be sold in America through a small group of Lexus dealers. The current model is hand finished, with a phoenix badge that takes six weeks to carve and black paint sanded by hand three times. The writer's point is that the product is the absence of friction, and that Toyota is attempting something rare: to export restraint at a serious premium.
