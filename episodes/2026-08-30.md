Hello, and welcome to Byte-sized, week of the twenty fourth of August. Six sections again, but this Sunday we're looking back across the whole week rather than just one day. Let's get into it.

JAPAN AND ASIA PACIFIC

It's been a week of Tokyo quietly proving how central it still is, on chips, on currency, and on missiles. Prime minister Sanae Takaichi is heading into fresh cabinet reshuffle season still riding what analysts call an approval shield: six months after leading her party to a two thirds majority, her public support tracks the polling patterns of Japan's most resilient postwar leaders. That shield held would-be rivals quiet through the week, but it's conditional, and the autumn parliamentary session will test whether her domestic agenda is actually landing with voters. Via CSIS.

The clearest vote of confidence in Japan came from the chip industry. Memory makers Kioxia and Sandisk confirmed plans to invest more than thirty one billion dollars in Japan through 2032, including eleven point three billion dollars for a new plant in Kitakami. With Washington and Beijing still fighting over export rules, and Washington now weighing tariffs of its own, Japan is positioning itself as the safe, well capitalised base for the memory hardware that AI data centres everywhere are racing to build. Via TLDR.

Earlier in the week, CSIS's Trade Guys podcast broke down a joint United States-Japan currency market intervention, a reminder of how central the Tokyo-Washington relationship remains to managing global trade friction even while attention drifts toward tariffs elsewhere. And on the security side, Japan's test firing of US made Tomahawk cruise missiles, paired with its own push for long range strike capability, appears to have shifted Kim Jong un's nuclear calculus more than anything coming out of Washington, according to CSIS's nuclear policy briefing. Via CSIS.

A smaller but telling story came out of Taiwan, where prosecutors have indicted a senior Nvidia manager and two Super Micro Computer executives for smuggling restricted AI servers into China, rerouted through Japan and Indonesia to dodge export controls. It's a reminder that insider risk sits inside even the biggest chip firms, and that Taiwan is now more willing than ever to help police America's export rules. In the same regional picture, Vietnam has officially overtaken China, Mexico and Taiwan to record the world's largest goods trade surplus with the United States, a sign that Trump's tariffs haven't so much closed America's trade deficit as relocated it, as firms like Apple and Nike shift production to de-risk from China. Via International Intrigue.

And on the hardware side of Asia's AI race, Beijing wrapped its second annual World Humanoid Robot Games this week: over two thousand robots and more than six hundred teams, the vast majority Chinese, competing in genuine autonomous tasks rather than remote controlled stunts. Chinese firms including AgiBot and Unitree are said to account for roughly three quarters of humanoid robots shipped worldwide, helped by a nearly fully localised supply chain, while Washington has just moved to restrict imports of foreign advanced robotic hardware. Closer to home, a Japanese startup called Letara raised sixteen million dollars to build bigger hybrid rocket systems for the space, defence and security markets, a reminder that Japan's small space technology scene keeps punching above its weight even while China's robotics industry grabs the headlines. Via International Intrigue and This Week in Startups.

Now, over to AI and tech.

If one thread ran through this entire week, it was Nvidia and Anthropic converting revenue into raw scale. Nvidia posted a blowout quarter, ninety six billion dollars, more than double what it made a year ago, with data centre sales alone hitting eighty nine billion, and it's guiding for a hundred and eight billion next quarter. Through the week it was also reportedly circling Hugging Face, the open source hub where developers share and test AI models, in a deal that started out being pegged at twelve point nine billion dollars and by Thursday was being framed as a thirteen billion dollar bid, with some reports suggesting it still isn't fully signed. Whatever the final number, the logic is simple: own the hub where developers live, and you help lock in your chips as the industry default. Via TLDR and International Intrigue.

Anthropic had an even bigger week. It struck a six year, forty five billion dollar deal with Nscale, a UK based AI infrastructure specialist, to lease four hundred and sixty megawatts of capacity from a new West Virginia campus running Nvidia's next generation chips. A federal judge blocked the Pentagon's attempt to blacklist the company after it refused to let the military use Claude without restrictions. It launched Claudeforce with Salesforce, pulling live customer data straight into Claude. And by Thursday, reports emerged that Anthropic is gunning for the biggest initial public offering on record, targeting a hundred billion dollar raise at a two trillion dollar valuation, more than double what SpaceX is worth, backed by revenue that's already running at sixty five billion dollars a year, seven times what it made last year. Being the AI lab with the firmest safety lines is carrying real commercial weight now, not just legal protection. Via TLDR, Sifted and International Intrigue.

Underneath the mega deals, the balance of power in AI usage is shifting east and cheaper. Chinese models are reportedly now dominating traffic on OpenRouter, the AI routing marketplace, as American startups quietly switch away from OpenAI and Anthropic to save money: DeepSeek charges around fourteen cents per million tokens against OpenAI's five dollars. Z.ai's new GLM model posted steep gains on security benchmarks, and an anonymous model calling itself Ox Alpha processed twenty six trillion tokens in four days on OpenRouter before anyone worked out who built it. Stripe, reading the same trend, agreed to acquire OpenRouter itself for more than seven billion dollars, turning a model routing service into core payments infrastructure, though a lot of that traffic flowing to Chinese models could bring Stripe new regulatory headaches. Via TLDR.

And a quieter but important story: companies are increasingly building their own AI coding agents rather than buying one. The fintech firm Ramp built a tool called Inspect with a team of five and a half people; it now produces three quarters of all code merged at the company, up from sixty percent back in January. Mental healthcare company Headway built its own Claude based tool, called Eddy, running inside its own cloud with every conversation sandboxed. Stripe, Block and Shopify have all done the same. Anthropic, for its part, spent the week pushing outward from software into physical equipment too, opening a research preview of what it calls the Model Hardware Standard, designed to let AI agents operate microscopes and robotic arms using plain language instead of custom code, with Tecan, Qiagen, AWS and Raspberry Pi already on board. The message for founders: the assistant you rent may not be the one your compliance or workflow needs actually require, and the boundary between AI software and AI hardware is getting thinner by the week. Via Every, The Pragmatic Engineer and The Rundown.

Now, over to startups and venture.

The clearest data point of the week came from Sifted: between January and the end of August, seven hundred and thirty eight US headquartered investors wrote cheques into European companies, chasing early stage, AI, deep tech and fintech deals. But that money is lumpy. Just three companies, the defence firms Helsing and Quantum Systems and the robotics scaleup Neura Robotics, accounted for nearly forty four percent of everything German startups raised this year. Europe's own answer to frontier AI, the Italian model maker Domyn, pushed its total raised past a billion dollars and is aiming for two billion by year end, even as France pointedly ruled out OpenAI for sensitive government cybersecurity work in favour of homegrown Mistral. Via Sifted.

A genuine growth story worth revisiting: the AI meeting notes app Granola went from nothing to a one point five billion dollar valuation in three years. Its playbook was a deliberate year in stealth, cutting half its planned features before launch, refusing to build a generic meeting bot despite investor pressure, and pivoting from a simple notepad into what its founders call a context layer for AI, one that now plugs into other tools instead of trying to do everything itself. Via The VC Corner.

And two cautionary tales on the listing side. Buy now, pay later firm Klarna saw its shares plunge nearly twenty five percent this week after warning that weak German consumer spending would hurt full year revenue. Part of the problem is structural: Klarna's core markets are European, but it listed in the US, where it's relatively small and under followed, missing out on the index inclusion and investor familiarity a European listing might have brought. Meanwhile China's fast fashion giant Shein has filed for its third attempt at going public, this time in Hong Kong, hoping to raise one point eight billion dollars at a twenty five billion dollar valuation, a steep comedown from its hundred billion dollar peak in twenty twenty two after a US listing collapsed under political pressure and a London listing fell apart when Beijing itself refused to bless it. Sometimes where, and whether, you list matters as much as how you perform. Via TLDR and International Intrigue.

One more theme worth carrying forward: starting a company has arguably never been cheaper. The share of startups built by a single founder has grown from under a quarter in 2019 to over a third last year, helped by the running cost of AI tools falling roughly a hundredfold in two years. But solo led startups, despite making up over a third of new companies, still capture only around fifteen percent of venture funding, a gap investors haven't fully closed. Via The VC Corner.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

The strangest story of the week came from the Spanish enclave of Ceuta, where more than seventy thousand people crossed from Morocco in a single day at the end of July, after a viral, and possibly coordinated, message claimed that swimming across the border meant migrants could no longer be instantly returned. Most went back, but around five thousand remain, straining a city of eighty four thousand and reigniting a decades old dispute over who Ceuta actually belongs to. Spanish authorities suspect Moroccan involvement, given Rabat's history of using mass border crossings as leverage, though a genuine viral panic Moroccan officials simply chose not to stop remains the more likely explanation. Via Uncharted Territories.

In Washington, the week brought a real mystery: CIA director John Ratcliffe made a surprise, very public trip to Moscow, flying in on a US military transport rather than a discreet private jet. The leading theory is deterrence, echoing a 2021 visit that correctly warned Putin the West knew he was planning to invade Ukraine, this time amid intelligence suggesting Russia may test NATO's resolve directly. Quieter theories point to stalled Ukraine peace talks, pressure over Iran, or Americans still held in Russian jails. Nobody in either capital is confirming which it was. That came in the same week Washington launched what it's calling an economic D Day against Iran, threatening secondary sanctions across digital assets, gold, technology, aviation and shipping, while trade talks between the US and Canada collapsed altogether, with Washington doubling car tariffs to fifty percent and Canada promising dollar for dollar retaliation on twenty billion dollars of American goods. Via International Intrigue.

The week's most tragic story came from the Himalayas, where flash floods along the Nepal-Tibet border killed at least a hundred and seventy seven people, with fifteen hundred more missing, after an ice and rock avalanche dammed a river that then gave way. Warmer temperatures have shrunk Nepal's glacial ice by a third over three decades, and the timing was especially cruel, since the area was packed with pilgrims from several faiths heading to the sacred Mount Kailash. Via International Intrigue.

On trade, CSIS analysts offered a useful corrective to all the tariff headlines: countries seem to have found the playbook for handling Washington's threats. Despite the fifty percent tariff threatened against Canada, it actually touches less than five percent of Canadian imports, and the average real US tariff rate sits around eleven percent, far below the numbers in the headlines. Countries are increasingly responding with vague, nonbinding framework agreements that let Washington claim a win while quietly preserving the status quo. The moments that actually shifted US behaviour this year were the forceful ones, Iran closing the Strait of Hormuz and China tightening its grip on critical minerals exports, not the threats themselves. Via CSIS.

And closer to home for the tech industry, Meta agreed to pay up to eighteen billion dollars to settle a landmark case brought by forty seven US states over teen social media harm, forcing real guardrails across Facebook and Instagram, including limits on how much time young people can spend scrolling. It's one of the largest consumer protection settlements in tech history, and a signal that state attorneys general, not just federal regulators, are now setting the pace on platform accountability. Via International Intrigue.

Now, over to community, events and marketing.

Two ideas worth stealing this week, both about where attention actually lives now. Marketing Brew's dispatch on professional pickleball showed how a niche sport quietly turned itself into a serious sponsorship business, the kind of brand deal that used to go only to established sports; Zoom ran the same playbook by attaching itself to Apple's Mac Mini relaunch. The lesson: credibility and sponsorship dollars follow audience size, not sport size, and pickleball's organisers built the fan base first and let the pitch write itself.

The second is about where people actually want to spend their evenings. TLDR flagged a rise in what's being called soft socialising: low pressure, activity based meetups where the point is the puzzle or the trivia, not the small talk. Puzzle events are up a hundred and fifty one percent year on year, mahjong meetups up ninety four percent. If you're building a community, the takeaway is the same as pickleball's: give people something to do together, not just a room to stand around in. Set against that, CMX's latest community industry report has a sobering number of its own: only seventeen percent of community professionals feel confident they can prove their work's business value, down from a quarter last year, even though ninety three percent are now using AI tools and in-person events are on the rise again. Doing the work and proving the work, it turns out, are still two different jobs. Via Marketing Brew, TLDR and CMX.

Now, to close, one interesting thing from the week.

Works in Progress ran a sharp essay arguing that American public transit is bad largely because of what the author calls scope creep. Buses and trains are asked to cut emissions, create union jobs, beautify neighbourhoods and reflect endless rounds of community input, all on top of their actual job of moving people, and every one of those extra goals costs money that could otherwise buy more frequent service. The examples are startling: Miami Dade's electric buses break down roughly every six hundred miles, seven times more often than diesel ones, so that on any given day only six of the agency's seventy five electric buses are actually running, while Boston's Green Line extension added a three kilometre bike path, after just thirteen people asked for it at a community meeting, at a cost of twenty million dollars. The core argument is simple: every dollar spent satisfying a side goal is a dollar not spent making transit fast and frequent enough that people who could drive choose not to. Via Works in Progress.
