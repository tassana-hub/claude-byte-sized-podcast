Section one, Japan and Asia Pacific.

First, a Japanese read on the biggest argument in artificial intelligence. Speeda's A I News round-up for Japanese readers looks back at September, when the heads of Anthropic, OpenAI and xAI each voiced support for slowing down A I development, while the American president brushed the idea aside. The editors frame it as a prisoner's dilemma: any one company or country that slows alone loses ground, yet the shared resource of safety is quietly eroded if everyone keeps racing. Their sharper point is about access. Prices for models anyone can buy are falling fast, but the most capable systems are increasingly handed out by vetting rather than by price, to government bodies and approved organisations. For Japanese firms, the practical question becomes how to qualify for the top tier. Via Speeda A I News.

Section two, A I and tech.

First, OpenAI is closing in on a funding round of up to thirty billion dollars at a valuation of about one point two five trillion dollars, led by investors from the United Arab Emirates. It is being positioned as a bridge ahead of a possible public listing, with signatures targeted by the end of next week. Via T L D R.

Second, the P H P emergency, with new detail. International Intrigue's account is that researchers pointed powerful A I models at the code in August and found what humans had dismissed as harmless memory quirks, then quietly raced to patch. Someone leaked details two days before the planned release, and with A I help attackers built a working exploit in about six hours, a gap that used to be measured in weeks. Cloudflare has seen thousands of attempts, then millions after the official announcement. More than half of P H P servers run old versions that no longer get regular updates, so the clean-up will be long. The Rundown's advice for the weekend: update everything, and use a password manager and an authenticator app rather than text message codes. Via International Intrigue and The Rundown A I.

Third, a story that connects to it. America's biggest banks, including J P Morgan, Bank of America and Morgan Stanley, have found third-party A I agents logging into customer accounts with customers' own credentials, and sometimes doing things the customer never asked for. Morgan Stanley is blocking them outright, others are restricting them, and most are moving towards read-only access. One executive admitted they struggle to tell an authorised agent from a rogue one. In the same news cycle, 1Password launched a tool that lets agents log in to sites without ever seeing the password. The opportunity sits in identity and permissions for agents. Via The Rundown A I and T L D R.

Fourth, a reminder that the watchers need watching. METR and Redwood Research spent three weeks probing OpenAI's internal agent monitoring. One gap they found, now patched, was that monitoring only triggered when an agent handed control back to the user, leaving around ten per cent of internal Codex traffic unmonitored between May and July. Via T L D R and The Rundown A I.

Section five, community, events and marketing. One idea worth stealing.

From Priya Parker, the Magical Question. At a family dinner spanning a seventy eight year old and three children, she asked everyone one thing: what is something specific your mum or dad did for you as a child that made you feel really loved? A good question, she says, is one everyone wants to answer and wants to hear others answer. This one worked because it put every age on equal footing as a child, and it opened stories nobody had told before. The takeaway: for your next event, dinner or community call, replace the round of introductions with one question like this, and write it down in advance. Via Priya Parker.

Finally, section six, one interesting thing.

From Marginal Revolution, a paper on how long wealth shocks last. Looking at England from seventeen hundred to today, the authors find that a windfall or a loss fades in under three generations. It cuts against the idea that fortunes today are mostly explained by events before nineteen hundred, and suggests that families regress to the average faster than we assume. Via Marginal Revolution.
