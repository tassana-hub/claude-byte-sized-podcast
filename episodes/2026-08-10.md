Hello, and welcome to Byte-sized for Monday, the tenth of August. Six sections today, starting with Japan and Asia-Pacific.

China and North Korea rebuild trade ties. Two-way goods trade between China and North Korea has hit two point seven billion dollars, the highest total since North Korea sealed its border back in early twenty twenty. The first half of this year is already nineteen per cent ahead of the same period last year, according to Chinese customs figures. Analysts at the Center for Strategic and International Studies read this as part of a broader reset in the relationship, alongside renewed high-level diplomatic contact between Beijing and Pyongyang. For a region watching Chinese and North Korean alignment closely, that is a meaningful shift after years of pandemic-era distance. Via CSIS.

New Zealand's startup scene passes one hundred and thirty billion. New Zealand's venture-backed startups now carry a combined valuation of around one hundred and thirty three billion New Zealand dollars, after the sector raised nine point eight billion dollars since twenty twenty. That has produced eight unicorns and two decacorns, and analysts rank the ecosystem highly for capital efficiency. The gap now is later stage funding, needed to help those companies grow into larger, more durable businesses rather than stalling out early. It is a reminder that the interesting startup stories in the Pacific are not only happening in Tokyo, Seoul or Singapore. Via The VC Corner.

Now, over to AI and tech.

The best AI agent builder is hiding inside Microsoft. A piece doing the rounds this week argues that Microsoft's Copilot Studio is quietly the strongest tool available for building AI agents, mainly because it lets agents call on other agents to collaborate on a task without extra setup work. The catch, according to the writer, is actually finding it, buried under confusing onboarding, broken links, and the roughly eighty different things Microsoft now calls Copilot. If your organisation already runs on Microsoft's stack, this is worth a proper look before building agent tooling from scratch elsewhere. Via Every.

A week living inside ChatGPT's voice mode. The team at Every spent a week testing OpenAI's live voice mode, using it to fix bugs, draft outlines, book flights, and even run coding agents while cooking dinner. One engineer read technical documentation aloud and had the assistant answer questions against his live code, while the company's chief operating officer found the mobile app could not yet reach into the work already open on his computer. Their verdict was blunt: impressive, not quite there yet, and obviously where things are heading. Via Every.

AI model makers race to control the whole stack. Model builders and AI agent companies are increasingly merging the training and deployment sides of their business instead of keeping them separate, matching models more tightly to specialised tools to improve both economics and control. At the same time, the major frontier AI providers kept cutting prices this week as competition shifted toward cheaper inference and better value per task. The same week also saw competing policy letters from industry groups, exposing real disagreement over how frontier AI development should be governed. Via The VC Corner.

Google just funded the startup its own best people left to build. Four longtime Google researchers left to start Discovery Loop, an effort to automate scientific experimentation, and Alphabet is backing the company from day one despite effectively funding its own departures. The same team previously helped develop talent that went on to build Anthropic, OpenAI, Safe Superintelligence, Thinking Machines and Mistral, which is part of why investors are paying such close attention. It says something about how porous the boundary between big labs and new AI startups has become, when the parent company ends up as an investor in the spin-out. Via The VC Corner.

Now, over to startups and venture.

How Cursor builds a high-talent-density team. Adam Ward, head of talent at the coding tool Cursor, laid out his approach to hiring in a wide-ranging interview, arguing the traditional recruiting funnel is what he calls a funnel of doom that produces mediocre hires. His alternative is a three-step playbook: scope the role precisely, map the market of people who could do it, and then pursue them relentlessly rather than waiting for applications to arrive. He also flagged the rise of the forward deployed engineer as a role every fast-growing AI company should now be thinking about. Via Lenny's Newsletter.

Venture funds look better on paper than in the bank. New data from Carta shows venture funds raised three point nine billion dollars across eighty six new funds in the first quarter, with paper fund performance improving thanks to higher valuations across most vintages. Actual cash returned to investors remains limited, and capital is increasingly concentrating into larger funds backed by fewer, bigger anchor investors. It is a sign that the venture market is recovering on valuation but not yet on liquidity. Via The VC Corner.

Fresh money keeps flowing into venture funds. Index Ventures announced two billion dollars in new funds to back startups from seed through growth stage across Europe, the United States and Israel, while Robinhood Ventures launched a second fund targeting fintech, AI, infrastructure and consumer technology. Smaller, more focused vehicles closed too, including a five hundred and forty eight million dollar fund for lower middle market industrial and manufacturing businesses in North America. Combined with the Carta numbers on paper valuations, it suggests investors are still willing to commit fresh capital even while actual cash returns stay slow. Via The VC Corner.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

China's nuclear stockpile, and the torpedo math behind it. China has roughly doubled its nuclear warhead stockpile in five years, from around three hundred to six hundred, with the Pentagon's last projection putting it over a thousand warheads by twenty thirty. Separately, defence analysts estimate the United States would need to fire somewhere between sixty and one hundred and twenty torpedoes a week in a prolonged conflict with China, a rate current stockpiles likely cannot sustain. Taken together, the numbers are less about any single crisis than about a widening capability gap that shapes how seriously any Taiwan contingency has to be taken in Washington and Tokyo alike. Via CSIS.

Why low orbit satellites matter for human rights. Researchers at the Center for Strategic and International Studies are making the case that low earth orbit satellite internet, sitting at around one thousand two hundred miles up or less, deserves treatment as a human rights issue rather than just a commercial one. Its low latency and cheaper launch costs make it useful for keeping governments functioning and civil society connected during conflict, when ground infrastructure often fails first. The policy question now is how to keep that connectivity available and neutral, rather than something a single company or government can switch off. Via CSIS.

Now, over to community, events and marketing.

One idea worth stealing: obsess over your landing page like it is a product. A newsletter founder called Dru Riley spent two years and seventeen documented versions rewriting his landing page, after reading a single forum post about a competitor converting at forty per cent of visitors. He tested everything, headlines, badges, even a single icon, and eventually pushed his own conversion rate past that original number. The writer behind this week's piece admits to copying Dru's layout outright and now sees similar results, and the real takeaway is the process: treat your landing page, welcome email and thank you page as living tests, not things you set once and forget. Via Growth in Reverse.

One idea worth stealing: run the small experiment before the big decision. The team behind the Do Lectures spent three weeks working from Seville instead of their usual base by the sea, treating it as a deliberate experiment rather than a holiday. Without an itinerary or the pressure to make it perfect, they settled into a rhythm of a few focused hours each morning followed by unstructured exploring in the afternoon, and came away healthier and calmer for it. Their conclusion was that big life decisions, like moving house or changing career, feel paralysing mainly because people treat them as permanent, when they could instead be run as small, reversible experiments first. Via The Do Lectures.

And finally, one interesting thing.

The Balkan fertility puzzle. The Balkans appear to be the only part of Europe, outside central Asia, where fertility rates have risen since twenty fifteen, with Montenegro, Bulgaria, Moldova and Serbia now sitting above every Western European country except France. That is strange, because neighbouring, culturally similar countries like Ukraine, Belarus, Poland and the Baltic states show no similar rebound, and Balkan societies are not noticeably more religious or family oriented than those neighbours. The economist who shared the observation, passing on a reader's question, admits he has no real theory for why it is happening. Sometimes the most interesting finding is the one nobody can yet explain. Via Marginal Revolution.
