Good morning, and welcome to Byte-sized for Saturday, the seventeenth of October. Six sections today, and we begin, as ever, in Japan and the Asia Pacific.

Section one, Japan and Asia Pacific.

First, a rough day for Japan's chip names. Tokyo Electron, the chipmaking equipment giant, fell about seven percent in a single session, after China's memory maker CXMT said it has made progress on a new high density type of DRAM and has passed ten percent of global market share. Samsung and SK Hynix were also under pressure. SoftBank slid roughly nine percent on worries about its wider exposure to AI. The picture is of East Asia's hardware leaders squeezed between nervous Western buyers on one side and a rising Chinese supplier on the other. Worth remembering that Tokyo Electron is still up about forty percent this year. Via International Intrigue.

Second, a CSIS read on Cambodia's scam centres, with Japan in the story. Cambodia passed its first anti-scam law in April, says it has shut more than two hundred compounds, and in September hosted a conference that produced the eleven point Phnom Penh Declaration. Every ASEAN member except Thailand signed, along with the United States, China and Japan. The catch, according to CSIS and Amnesty International, is that the elite networks behind the industry have not been touched, and that many compounds may simply have moved out of sight. For anyone working on trade, travel or fraud in the region, the signal is that the headline closures and the reality may not match. Via CSIS.

Third, Indonesia's market shock. MSCI is considering moving Indonesia from emerging to standalone status, which would remove it from its global indices. Jakarta's benchmark fell eleven percent in a day, and is down about forty percent since January. MSCI points to opaque ownership and weak enforcement, a hard blow for a government that promised eight percent growth on foreign investment. Via International Intrigue.

Now, over to artificial intelligence and technology.

Section two, AI and tech.

First, open weights keep climbing. Meta has released Muse Spark Open, an eight hundred and sixteen billion parameter multimodal model with a one million token context window, built for coding and long running agent work. The striking detail is that it can run locally across two high end Apple Mac Studios. Separately, Vercel's October gateway index says open weight models now handle more than seventy percent of tokens and nearly a quarter of spend, with share of spend more than four times higher than in June. Nearly one in three teams switched their main lab in September, a record. Loyalty to any single model provider is thin. Via TLDR.

Second, ASML says it cannot keep up. It sold one hundred and thirteen new lithography systems in the third quarter for record sales of eleven point seven billion euros, and now expects supply in twenty twenty seven and twenty twenty eight to fall short of demand, even after a planned thirty percent capacity increase. Some customers want to place orders for twenty twenty nine and thirty. Via TLDR.

Third, a trend in how companies build software. The Pragmatic Engineer reports that Ramp and Stripe have each built internal platforms where non engineers create their own websites and tools, and that both are taking off. Expect more firms to follow, which changes what internal teams are for. Via The Pragmatic Engineer.

Now, over to startups and venture.

Section three, startups and venture.

First, a warning light in AI infrastructure finance. Corporate bonds issued by CoreWeave in June at nine point six percent are now trading at prices implying around fifteen percent, as lenders worry about a company spending nearly a quarter of revenue on interest. Oracle fell nine percent in one session, and the IMF notes that AI hyperscalers now issue about ten percent of new investment grade corporate bonds worldwide, up from one percent two years ago. For founders, the message is that cheap capital behind the AI build out is no longer guaranteed. TSMC, meanwhile, posted record quarterly profit of twenty three point six billion dollars. Via International Intrigue.

Second, the British Business Bank is doubling down on AI and deeptech venture funds, announcing investments in six firms including Evantic, led by former Sequoia partner Matt Miller, and Entrepreneurs First. Sifted puts the total at one hundred and eighty million pounds, though its subject line gives a lower figure, so treat the number with care. Nvidia also touted two billion dollars of new European investment. Via Sifted.

Third, a diligence tip. Sifted reports that European defence tech startups are blurring bookings and recognised revenue, and in one case counting a grant logo as defence revenue. Investors say the fix is plain old accounting: ask for the paperwork. Via Sifted.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

Section four, policy and geopolitics.

First, Starlink and Ukraine. Ukrainian officials say SpaceX capped speeds on military Starshield terminals mounted on drones, with no warning, causing connection drops and cancelled missions. A Ukrainian firm is now developing its own alternative. The lesson for any government or enterprise buyer is dependency risk on a single commercial network. Via TLDR.

Second, chips and export controls. Reuters reports that Solidigm wants American approval to ship more chipmaking tools to SK Hynix's fab in Dalian, China, as a condition of building a new American memory factory. Industrial policy and export policy are becoming one negotiation. Via TLDR.

Third, one for the diary: a new bridge linking China and North Korea has finally opened after twelve years of delay, their largest land crossing, just weeks after a similar link with Russia. Via International Intrigue.

Section five, one idea worth stealing.

From Skift's report on IMEX in Las Vegas: destinations that stood out came with stories, not just booths. Washington, D.C. is countering negative sentiment by telling the stories of immigrant business owners who call the city home. If you run events or a community, lead with the people, not the logo on the stand. Via Skift.

Now, one last thing.

Section six, one interesting thing. Works in Progress, with Charles Mann, asks what on earth Dekton is, the new material on his expensive induction hob. The answer is that it is essentially porcelain, one of humanity's oldest high technologies and one of its newest. Europe spent centuries failing to copy Chinese porcelain until a Saxon alchemist's captive, working with a mathematician, finally cracked it in seventeen oh eight. Today pressed porcelain slabs, non slip tiles and even bioceramic hip scaffolds are quietly reshaping the world around us. Mann's son, he notes, has never lived in a world that was not made of porcelain. Via Works in Progress.
