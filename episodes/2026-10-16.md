Section two, AI and tech.

First, Anthropic has outgrown Git. With AI generated code pushing roughly thirty thousand merged changes a day, the company built its own version control system, called Scion, and moved its main code repository across in five months. It assumes engineers are always online, uses a central server with server side commits, and cut processor and memory needs by around two hundred times, while code checkouts for testing dropped from minutes to seconds. The wider point: when writing code gets cheap, the tooling around it becomes the bottleneck. Via TLDR.

Second, Nvidia in Britain. At an event in London, Jensen Huang announced minority stakes in five UK companies, spanning molecular modelling, infrastructure planning, automated discovery, quantum error correction and biological models. He also told the room that Britain tends to find the one reason not to win, out of a million ways to do it. Via Sifted.

Third, the shopping agents arrive. Shopify is opening its catalogue to personal shopping agents, and to tools such as ChatGPT, Copilot and Gemini, while keeping a human sign off on every purchase. Meanwhile JPMorgan is blocking outside AI agents from moving customers' money directly, citing security and liability, while building its own tools. Two of the biggest commerce gatekeepers are drawing the line in different places, and that line is where the next fights will be. Via TLDR.

First, Sifted's second AI one hundred ranking of Europe's most promising AI startups. Stockholm's Tandem Health, an assistant for clinicians used in fourteen countries, tops the list. Half the companies are vertical specialists, fourteen apply AI to the physical world, and thirteen of last year's list have since become unicorns. Y Combinator is on the cap table of thirteen of this year's hundred. Via Sifted.

Third, a pricing puzzle. Monzo, valued at five point nine billion dollars, trades at about three times revenue, while Revolut's latest share sale values it near nineteen times revenue. Same sector, very different multiples. Via TLDR.

Section four, policy and geopolitics.

A study commissioned by a United States technology industry group says European cloud sovereignty rules could cost the continent one hundred and fifteen billion euros over a decade. Treat the number carefully, since it is a modelled scenario from an interested party, but expect it to feature in Brussels debates about digital independence, and in any conversation with enterprise buyers weighing where to host their data. Via TLDR.

Now, over to community, events and marketing.

Section five, one idea worth stealing.

From Fukuoka Now, the format itself. Their roundtable is timed to a live local issue, the mayoral election, and opens with one simple question: what is one thing you would change? Two short openers, a moderator, a reception beforehand, and room for the audience. If you run events, topical timing and a single easy question beat a long programme. And a second, from Skift's meetings desk: Timeleft charges nineteen ninety nine a month to seat strangers at dinner, and keeps names private until the night, with conversation prompts built in. Design the introduction, not just the venue. Via Skift.

Now, one last thing.

Section six, one interesting thing. Why Is This Interesting looks at bibliotherapy, a word coined in nineteen sixteen. In the First World War, librarians wheeled book carts into military hospitals, and one British scholar drew up a fever chart of prescribed reading. For soldiers with shell shock, one prescription was Jane Austen: not a book explaining trauma, but a story about the ordinary complications of being human. The suggestion is that information is not the same as consolation. Via Why Is This Interesting.
