Section one, Japan and Asia Pacific.

First, a serious breach at one of Japan's biggest internet companies. G M O Internet says an attacker got into its Onamae Desktop Cloud service, which is used by financial traders. The hackers reportedly demanded around fifty million dollars, and G M O shut the service down on the twenty-first of October. Up to twenty-seven thousand servers may be affected, customers have reported unauthorised activity in their trading accounts, and some alleged customer data has now appeared online. The company is verifying the leak, asking users to reset passwords and collecting customer devices to check for malware. For anyone selling cloud or fintech services into Japan, this is the kind of incident that will shape procurement questions for months. Via T L D R.

Second, Japan is leading on nuclear abolition again. A United Nations General Assembly committee has adopted, by majority vote, Japan's annual resolution calling for the abolition of nuclear weapons. It is a routine diplomatic ritual in one sense, but in a year of loud nuclear signalling elsewhere, it keeps Tokyo's long-standing position on the record. Via the C S I S Nuclear Policy News, citing the Japan Times.

Section two, A I and tech.

First, Google DeepMind has unveiled Genie 4, its latest world model. It generates photorealistic, interactive environments in real time, now with its own sound, and characters inside the world that can hold live conversations. Scenes stay consistent for up to two minutes, double the last version. A demo showed a simulated humanoid robot playing checkers, which hints at the real ambition: training robots inside generated worlds. It is in research preview for trusted testers, with Google's top tier subscribers and partners next. Via The Rundown and T L D R.

Second, Anthropic is moving into drug development. It shared early results with pharmaceutical partners and said it is now running its own programmes, including acquiring Mithril Medicine, a one-person biotech with a muscular dystrophy candidate that showed promise in mice. At one partner, Claude drafted a key regulatory filing in four hours instead of roughly three months. Anthropic's own recent filing warns these bets may never generate revenue, but the direction is clear: the frontier labs now want to own scientific outcomes, not only sell tools. Via The Rundown and T L D R.

The start-up Axiom has completed the first fully computer-checked proof of the classification of finite simple groups, often described as the longest proof ever written, at around fifteen thousand pages. Dozens of A I agents worked for twenty-four days and produced over forty million lines of code in the Lean proof language. They even caught a false claim in one step and patched it. Via The Rundown.

Fourth, the chip and cash race. Google will put its T P U chips inside Oracle's cloud from twenty twenty-seven, the first time a rival cloud will offer them. Section six, one interesting thing.

Experimental History tells of a researcher who inserted a hundred deliberate errors into ten psychology papers and asked several A I models to find them. No single model did especially well, but together they caught ninety-three per cent. A second study found both human and A I judges preferred A I peer reviews to those from a paid Harvard service. The author's point is that humans were rarely doing that quality control at all, so A I flagging problems is not replacing labour, it is doing labour that never happened. Via Experimental History.
