JAPAN AND ASIA-PACIFIC

A rich few days for the region, starting with a striking piece of research on the most sensitive subject in Japanese security policy. CSIS's Japan Chair has published a new survey of Japanese strategic elites, meaning current and former scholars, business leaders and officials, on the question of nuclear weapons. Despite a worsening security environment, from North Korea's missile programme to China's expanding arsenal, the clear majority still oppose Japan acquiring its own bomb, consistent with the wider public mood. But the researchers found two specific triggers that could shift that: a drawdown of American forces on the Korean Peninsula, and, more powerfully, South Korea itself going nuclear. Japanese elites said a nuclear South Korea would move their support more than a US withdrawal would, suggesting a cascade effect where one country's proliferation pushes its neighbours to follow. Via CSIS.

Staying with security, but in a very different register, a Japanese language AI newsletter from Uzabase's Speeda service flagged that Japan's government has adopted a non-binding code requiring AI developers to disclose more about their training data, arriving in the same week the EU opened its first formal information requests to OpenAI, Anthropic and Google DeepMind under the AI Act, and China's top court issued its first nationwide judicial guidance on AI disputes and deepfakes. Three different regulatory philosophies, converging on the same set of questions about who answers for an AI system's behaviour. Via Speeda Intelligence.

And a lovely piece of pure geography from Uncharted Territories, asking why the largest city on earth sits on some of the most hazardous ground imaginable. Tokyo has thirty seven million people, a subway network so big it would take five days to walk across, and was built on land prone to earthquakes, volcanoes, tsunamis and typhoons. The answer, it turns out, isn't density, Tokyo isn't even in the world's top fifty most crowded cities, it's sheer surface area. Tokyo sits on the Kanto Plain, by far the largest flat, farmable expanse in Japan, which meant it could feed, and eventually house, more people than anywhere else in the country. Geography decided the capital long before politics did. Via Uncharted Territories.

Now, over to AI and tech.

AI AND TECH

The biggest single number this week belongs to Anthropic, which has committed eleven point six billion dollars over seven years to Akamai's cloud infrastructure to support its growing computing needs, an agreement that could expand by a further nine billion dollars and includes warrants giving Anthropic roughly a five percent stake in Akamai. It's another sign of just how much of the AI boom's capital is now flowing not into flashier model headlines but into unglamorous compute and infrastructure deals behind the scenes. Via TLDR.

Security researchers, meanwhile, had a rough week with Meta's new autonomous assistant, Muse. A zero day vulnerability, demonstrated by researcher Patrick Wardle, allows any local app or terminal command on a Mac to redirect Muse's transcription to an attacker controlled server, handing over account access to a tool that can already read email, send messages, check calendars and use the microphone and camera. Amazon has already started blocking Muse's shopping requests as unauthorised. It's a reminder that giving an assistant sweeping permissions across your digital life is only as safe as its weakest technical seam. Via TLDR.

And a smaller, more human story about what AI is doing to how companies are organised. A widely shared essay this week, flagged by both Lenny's Newsletter and TLDR's product digest, argued that AI native companies are flattening hierarchies and handing individuals far more autonomy and far more fluid roles, on the theory that as execution gets cheaper and faster, human judgement, not process, becomes the scarce resource. Worth remembering next time a start up boasts about having no middle management. Via Lenny's Newsletter.

Now, over to startups and venture.

STARTUPS AND VENTURE

A genuinely useful piece of engineering news for anyone running AI agents at scale. NVIDIA published research called Sol Pi, in which they pointed an AI system at the "harness", the scaffolding of tools, context and feedback loops around a model, rather than at the model itself, and let it hunt for waste across fifty one tasks. Out of one hundred and fifty two candidate fixes, four survived testing, cutting token use by roughly forty five to forty nine percent and API costs by fifty to fifty four percent against the standard Codex and Claude Code harnesses, all without touching the underlying model. NVIDIA estimates that's worth roughly nine to thirteen dollars an hour of savings on agent work, and the code is openly available. The lesson for founders watching their inference bill climb: before switching models or waiting for prices to fall, check the plumbing first. Via the VC Corner.

On the creator economy side, Growth in Reverse told the story of Heather Cox Richardson, a Boston College history professor who didn't know what a newsletter was in 2019 and now earns seven figures a month from one. Her Facebook following built slowly for five years, then took off once she began tying breaking news to historical parallels ahead of the 2020 election. It's a reminder that in the newsletter economy, distribution built patiently over years can suddenly compound once the content finds its moment. Via Growth in Reverse.

And from Europe, Sifted's Anne Sraders sat down with Andreessen Horowitz general partner Anish Acharya, who argued European founders should stop measuring themselves against Silicon Valley and instead build companies that are, in his words, unapologetically European, serving markets and customers that only make sense in a European context. A16z has no plans to reopen its London office, closed less than two years after opening in 2023, but Acharya said the firm is actively looking at more deals on the continent. Via Sifted.

POLICY AND GEOPOLITICS

The United Nations General Assembly's annual leaders' week in New York produced a genuinely striking Trump line: the president told the hall he would decide after the American midterm elections whether to strike a deal with Iran that allows it to rebuild, or, in his words, annihilate the Islamic Republic. Analysts reading the timing see an attempt to preserve maximalist leverage while blunting the war's political cost before voters go to the polls. Elsewhere in the same week, Brazil's Lula used his traditional opening speech to appeal to voters at home ahead of his own tight election, and Jordan's King Abdullah warned that Israel's recent strikes into Syria, Lebanon and Iran now amount to a regional threat rather than a series of isolated actions. Via International Intrigue.

Closer to the ground, CSIS's Middle East programme flagged a serious test of the US-Saudi relationship. Houthi attacks on Saudi Arabia have continued with limited American support, and separately, Syria saw its most widespread protests since the fall of the Assad government after fuel prices jumped by up to ninety four percent this year, a direct knock-on effect of the wider Iran war disrupting regional energy supplies. Analysts at CSIS argue Gulf states are now having to absorb security shocks that a decade ago they would have expected Washington to help shoulder. Via CSIS.

And a smaller but nagging story about the fog beneath the Arctic. Geopolitical Monitor noted that Norway's defence minister insists NATO never loses track of Russian submarines in northern waters, but NATO's own spokesperson has been notably more cautious in public, and two undersea cables cut off the Norwegian coast remain unattributed months later. It's a useful corrective to any confident public statement about undersea surveillance capability. Via Geopolitical Monitor.

Now, over to community, events and marketing. One idea worth stealing.

COMMUNITY, EVENTS AND MARKETING

The Event Design Collective made a case worth sitting with even outside the events world: AI can generate an agenda, summarise research and throw out format options in seconds, but it cannot answer the genuinely hard questions, what should this event change, for whom, and what should deliberately not be automated. Their term for this middle path is augmented event design, using AI to widen the field of options while keeping the actual judgement calls human. It's a useful framing for anyone tempted to let a model make a decision that was never really about information in the first place. Via the Event Design Collective.

And a small, concrete marketing example from Marketing Brew: Inter Milan's social team grew its followers, impressions and views not by posting more, but by posting less, trading volume for a smaller number of sharper posts. In an attention economy where most brands default to flooding the feed, it's a reminder that restraint can be the more sophisticated strategy. Via Marketing Brew.

Now, to close, one interesting thing.

ONE INTERESTING THING

A new NBER paper by Dora Costa and Matthew Kahn puts a number on something most families feel but rarely measure: grandmotherhood is arriving much later, and it's shrinking the years grandchildren actually get with a healthy grandmother. Under the fertility patterns of 1960, the median age of first becoming a grandmother was forty four; under today's patterns it's fifty seven, cutting the years of healthy grandmothering nearly in half, from twenty eight years down to fifteen. Longer lifespans haven't closed that gap, because it's specifically the healthy, energetic years that have been lost, not just years alive. The researchers call it the rotten adult kid problem: when a daughter delays having children to maximise her own life outcomes, the cost of that delay lands on her mother, a classic economic externality hiding inside an ordinary family decision. Via Marginal Revolution.
