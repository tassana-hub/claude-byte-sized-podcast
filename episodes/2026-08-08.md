Good morning, it's Saturday, the eighth of August. Six sections for you today.

Malaysia's governing coalition is reeling after another string of election defeats, and it's worth watching for anyone tracking political stability across the Asia-Pacific. Prime Minister Anwar Ibrahim's Pakatan Harapan coalition lost the state of Negeri Sembilan on the first of August, ousted after eight years in power as the opposition alliance won a supermajority in the state assembly. It's the coalition's third big regional defeat in recent months, following losses in Johor and Sabah, and the pressure is spilling into Anwar's own government: his transport minister lost his seat in the vote, and his coalition partner, the Democratic Action Party, is now holding an internal vote on the sixteenth of August over whether to keep serving in his administration. Anwar isn't required to call a national election until February of twenty twenty-eight, but a coalition partner publicly weighing whether to walk away is not a good sign for how long that timeline holds. Via CSIS Southeast Asia Program.

Staying in the region, CSIS's Korea chair flagged a notable shift this week: China appears to be deepening ties with North Korea, including on the military side, in what the analysis frames as a genuine reset rather than a symbolic gesture. It's a reminder that Beijing and Pyongyang's relationship doesn't move in a straight line, and any warming between the two has knock-on implications for how Seoul, Tokyo and Washington read the security balance in Northeast Asia. Via CSIS Korea Chair.

And in hospitality, a long-time hotel obsessive has given his verdict on the newly reopened Park Hyatt Tokyo, closed for nineteen months for a full restoration. After visiting in May and sitting with the experience for a while, his conclusion is that the team behind the reopening got it almost exactly right: they refreshed genuinely underused spaces, like the main restaurant, which now has an Alain Ducasse collaboration, while preserving the hotel's slow, deliberate arrival sequence from street level up into the light-filled lounge and down into the hushed library. It's a useful case study for anyone renovating a beloved brand or space: the failure mode for classic properties is usually not neglect, it's a new team mistaking quirks for deficiencies. Via Why Is This Interesting.

Now, over to AI and tech.

Google DeepMind had a leadership shake-up this week that rattled investors. Demis Hassabis has moved from chief executive to chair of DeepMind and chief scientist of Alphabet, while Jeff Dean, who'd been at Google for twenty-seven years, departed to launch his own venture called Discovery Loop. Alphabet's shares fell more than five percent on the news, a sign of how tightly investors now tie the company's AI credibility to specific individuals. Via TLDR.

Researchers at Stanford and the Arc Institute have used AI to design working viruses that don't exist in nature, then actually built and tested them in the lab. The team trained AI models called Evo on millions of existing genomes and had them write entirely new versions of a well-studied virus that infects only a common gut bacteria. Of two hundred and eighty five synthetic phages the models generated, sixteen turned out to be viable, some replicating faster than the natural version they were based on, and a few different enough to count as new species, with the results published this week in the journal Science. The viruses only infect bacteria, and the goal is fighting drug-resistant infections, but the obvious worry, flagged by the researchers themselves, is what happens once the same design tools get pointed at something more dangerous. Via The Rundown.

On the security side, a nasty piece of malware called Shai-Hulud is back. A new variant has compromised more than one thousand two hundred and eighty packages in the open-source npm ecosystem, including the widely used Keyv library, by hijacking GitHub Actions pipelines and quietly injecting malicious commits. Once installed, it runs an obfuscated payload that aggressively harvests developer and cloud credentials, for services like Amazon Web Services, Kubernetes and secrets vaults, and uses them to spread further into other codebases. Researchers are telling any organisation that installed an affected package to assume they've been breached and rotate every secret immediately, rather than wait for a routine vulnerability scan. Via TLDR.

And the AI hardware race keeps consolidating. AMD has agreed to buy the Toronto chip startup Taalas, which builds custom silicon that hard-wires AI model weights directly into the chip during manufacturing, folding that ultra-efficient inference technology into its accelerator lineup alongside its existing Instinct chips. Meanwhile, Anthropic has confirmed it's assembling its own in-house chip design team to build custom processors tailored to Claude, aiming to cut inference costs and speed things up rather than relying solely on outside hardware partners. Via TLDR.

Now, over to startups and venture.

The long-running mystery of what Yann LeCun is actually doing next has an answer. The former Meta chief AI scientist, who left to raise more than a billion dollars for his own world-models startup, has now joined a new AI-focused venture fund called two hundred and twenty-four Ventures, where he'll invest alongside Google DeepMind researcher Oriol Vinyals and AIX Ventures founder Shaun Johnson, with the trio investing exclusively through the fund. It has around a hundred million dollars under management. It's a notable landing given LeCun briefly appeared linked to a different, earlier fund just weeks ago, with documents referring to him as a non-managing general partner, before he quietly walked away from that arrangement once exclusivity conflicts with other funds emerged, something he's since chalked up to a miscommunication. Via Sifted.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

A CSIS briefing this week zeroed in on a quiet but consequential vulnerability: the Middle East's heavy reliance on imported pharmaceuticals, a risk that's grown sharper as the war around Iran raises the odds of medicine shortages. Governments across the region, including Saudi Arabia, Qatar and the United Arab Emirates, have all launched national strategies to build up domestic drug manufacturing, but a decade of data shows net pharmaceutical imports have actually kept rising across the board, driven partly by demand for expensive newer drugs like GLP-one treatments. Jordan stands out as the exception, remaining a net exporter thanks to a long-established, export-oriented industry and procurement rules that favour local manufacturers. Lebanon and Egypt illustrate how uneven the picture is elsewhere: Lebanon's imports have fallen sharply, but mostly because of collapsed purchasing power rather than success building local supply, while Egypt's currency depreciation has made even the raw ingredients for domestic manufacturing more expensive. With a wave of major drug patents expiring this year, there's a real window for regional generic manufacturers to close the gap, but the last decade suggests that window won't close itself. Via CSIS Middle East Program.

Now, over to community, events and marketing.

One idea worth stealing: a community-building writer made the case this week that most communities hoping to grow are hiding too much value behind a login wall. The argument is that asking people to create an account before they can even see whether a community is worth their time gets things backwards; people need to see real value and build some trust first, through public content, previews or open spaces, before you ask them to sign up and commit. It's a simple, slightly uncomfortable idea for anyone running a Slack, Discord or forum on lockdown: if your best material is invisible to outsiders, you're not protecting your community, you're starving its growth. Via Community Inc.

Now, one interesting thing.

In June nineteen seventy-three, the racehorse Secretariat ran the Belmont Stakes so fast, pulling away to win by thirty one lengths, nearly three quarters of a football field, that the other horses weren't even in the television frame by the finish. More than fifty years and billions of dollars of breeding investment later, nobody has matched him. Works in Progress calls this the paradox of the thoroughbred: breeders spent generations selecting relentlessly for speed, and in doing so may have narrowed the horse's genetic diversity so much that they've bred away the very variation that would let the breed keep improving, or even stay robust. It's a neat, slightly unsettling illustration of a broader idea: optimising too hard and too narrowly for one trait can quietly destroy your ability to ever get better at it again. Via Works in Progress.
