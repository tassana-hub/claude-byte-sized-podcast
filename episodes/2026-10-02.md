Section one, Japan and Asia Pacific. There's nothing from Japan itself in today's mail, so this section looks at the wider region: four stories, from payments to Laos to a very Singaporean dating app.

First, Ant International has unveiled what it calls a full stack for agentic commerce, meaning AI agents that spend money on your behalf. The centrepiece is a protocol called AMP, built around the wallets and QR codes people actually use across Asia, rather than cards. Ant says fifteen wallets are signed up at launch, including GCash, Toss, TrueMoney and Alipay, along with eight payment processors such as Adyen and Worldpay. Two details stand out. Ant, Mastercard and Visa are making their Know Your Agent identity checks interoperable, under an initiative run by the Monetary Authority of Singapore. And every AMP payment comes with a money back guarantee if an agent hallucinates or drifts from what you approved. Most of the figures are Ant's own, so treat them as claims for now. Via The VC Corner.

Second, China's military has opened a logistics and support base at an airport in Laos, which both sides describe as jointly run and not aimed at any third party. The analysis is that landlocked Laos gives the People's Liberation Army a foothold in mainland Southeast Asia, reducing its reliance on maritime chokepoints such as the Strait of Malacca. It also adds to quiet unease in Vietnam, now flanked by Chinese-linked facilities in Laos, Cambodia and the South China Sea. Via International Intrigue.

Third, Apple Pay has finally launched in India, through a partnership with Axis Bank. For now it works with Axis-issued Visa and Mastercard credit cards, in shops, online and in apps. India's payments market is huge and dominated by local instant payment systems, so the interesting question is how many people will choose a card on a phone instead. Via TLDR Fintech.

And fourth, something lighter. Singapore's government has launched a dating app called FirstDate, initially for public sector workers, as a response to falling birth rates. Users fill in a detailed questionnaire, their identity is verified through the national Singpass system, and a matching algorithm proposes one match at a time, who has seventy two hours to respond. There are even meal vouchers for the first date. A small reminder that governments are now experimenting with demographic policy in very hands-on ways. Via International Intrigue.

Now, over to artificial intelligence and technology.

Section two, AI and tech. Four stories: a new frontier model, a chip race, government adoption, and a debate about coding.

Google has announced Gemini 4 Argon, a frontier model built for sustained reasoning across software engineering, enterprise knowledge work and cybersecurity. Access starts with a limited group of trusted cyber defenders, with wider release to follow after phased safety testing. That staged approach is notable: the most capable models are increasingly being released to defenders first. Via TLDR.

Next, DeepSeek is reportedly building a software layer between AI models and the underlying chips, so that the same workload can run on either Nvidia GPUs or Huawei's Ascend processors. Nvidia's real moat is its software ecosystem, so if this works, the cost and difficulty of moving AI work to Chinese hardware could fall significantly. Via TLDR.

Third, Anthropic has made Claude for Government generally available, with the highest FedRAMP authorisation for coding and agent tools used by federal and state agencies. It's a sign that regulated public sector buyers are now a mainstream market for the labs. Via TLDR.

And fourth, The Pragmatic Engineer reports that David Heinemeier Hansson, creator of Ruby on Rails, used his Rails World keynote to declare an end to writing code by hand for professional work, at least at his company, 37signals. The newsletter asks whether that shift is now unstoppable, and it has reopened the argument about the future of programming as a craft. Via The Pragmatic Engineer.

Now, over to community, events and marketing.

Section five, community, events and marketing. Two ideas worth stealing, both from the team behind the a16z speedrun programme, writing ahead of San Francisco Tech Week, which has more than eighteen hundred events.

First, find the empty slot. Everyone hosts happy hours in the evening. Last June, AWS and the venture firm Flybridge took over a New York bodega at eight in the morning for three hundred people, organised in about a week. Almost nothing else was on at that hour, and the venue did the work of giving the event character.

Second, make the event move. Google Cloud sent ice cream trucks around San Francisco last year, with two thousand spots, the highest capacity brand activation of the week, and no fixed address. People ran into the truck, got ice cream, and left with a story. The rule of thumb from the team: do it well or not at all, and ask whether you'd attend if you weren't the host. Via a16z speedrun.

Finally, one interesting thing.

Section six, one interesting thing. The newsletter Why Is This Interesting covers the Pittsburgh Parallel Parking Championship, a free street party that began as a family argument about who parks best. The rules fit on two images: your score is your time multiplied by your distance from the kerb, and hit anything and you're out. There's no registration, results are immediate, volunteers chat to every driver, and the whole experience takes about ten minutes. The writer's point is that a joke event can still be run with real rigour, and that's exactly why people love it. Via Why Is This Interesting.
