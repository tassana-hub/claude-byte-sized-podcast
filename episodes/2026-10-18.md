Good morning, and welcome to Byte-sized, the week in review, for the week of the twelfth of October. Six sections as usual, and we begin, as ever, in Japan and the Asia Pacific.

Section one, Japan and Asia Pacific.

Where the week landed: a region being pulled between defence, supply chains and shaky markets, with Japan right in the middle of each.

First, the defence supply chain. Thales is set to become the first foreign defence company to open a dedicated military maintenance base in Japan, starting with servicing naval sensors and radars, and transferring technology to build heavy mortar systems locally from twenty twenty seven. Tokyo has traditionally kept this kind of work for domestic champions such as Mitsubishi and Kawasaki. The reading is that Japan's historic defence spending increase is now running into capacity limits, which opens the door to foreign partners. Via International Intrigue.

Second, the chip sell-off. International Intrigue's Friday briefing found Japan's chipmaking tool giant, Tokyo Electron, down seven point two percent in a single day, after Chinese memory maker CXMT said it had made progress on high density memory and had reached ten percent of global market share. Korea's Samsung and Hynix were sweating too, and SoftBank fell around nine percent on its wider investor exposure. In fairness, Tokyo Electron is still up about forty percent this year. But East Asia's hardware giants are now squeezed between nervous Western buyers on one side and rising Chinese suppliers on the other. Via International Intrigue.

Third, a diplomatic test for Tokyo. Russia has arrested Yuki Imai, a reporter for the Hokkaido Shimbun, accusing him of gathering information on Russian military activity in the Far East. The timing is delicate, since President Putin recently angered Japan by visiting the contested Kuril Islands, and Moscow has a record of holding foreign journalists as bargaining chips. Via International Intrigue.

Fourth, the wider region. Indonesia's stock market had its worst day in decades, down eleven percent, after the index provider MSCI said it is considering downgrading the country from emerging to standalone status, citing opaque ownership and weak enforcement. Separately, South Korea's Arctic shipping trial returned to Busan ten to fourteen days faster than the Suez route, a hedge against fragile chokepoints that Japan, equally dependent on sea lanes, will be watching closely. And China and North Korea have finally opened the New Yalu River Bridge, after twelve years of delay. Via International Intrigue.

Finally, two dates for your diary. The CSIS and Nikkei Symposium takes place in Tokyo this Friday, the twenty third of October, with Japan's defence minister, Shinjiro Koizumi, giving a special address. And in Fukuoka, Fukuoka Now is holding Kyushu Roundtable number seven, at CIC Fukuoka in Tenjin on Friday the thirtieth of October, asking what the city should prioritise ahead of the mayoral election. It is in English, free, and places are limited. Via CSIS and Fukuoka Now.

Now, over to artificial intelligence and technology.

Section two, AI and tech.

Where the week landed: models keep getting cheaper and more open, code is no longer the bottleneck, and the money behind it all is starting to wobble.

First, open models had a big week. Meta released Muse Spark Open, a very large open weight model, which it calls the most capable open model from the United States, and tuned it with Apple so that two high end Mac Studios can run it locally without the cloud. It still trails Chinese open models on coding, and it is the third major Western open release this month, after Reflection AI and Mistral, whose new model has its weights arriving on the twenty seventh. Earlier in the week, Europe had its own moment, with Mistral and Germany's Aleph Alpha both pitching sovereign alternatives. Via The Rundown and Sifted.

Second, engineering at scale. Anthropic built its own version control system, called Scion, because AI generated code was pushing roughly thirty thousand merged changes a day through its repositories. Checkouts for testing dropped from minutes to seconds. The wider point stands: when writing code gets cheap, the tooling around it becomes the bottleneck. Anthropic co founder Jack Clark also moved his estimate for fully automated AI research forward a year, to twenty twenty seven. Via TLDR and The Rundown.

Third, the agents are going shopping, and the gatekeepers disagree on the rules. Shopify is opening its catalogue to personal shopping agents while keeping a human sign off on purchases, whereas JPMorgan is blocking outside agents from moving customers' money directly. Booking Holdings launched its own travel agent, Vayo, with a planned hundred million dollar investment. The big platforms want to own the assistant layer, not just the inventory. Via TLDR and PhocusWire.

Fourth, and this is the one to hold in your head going into next week: the financing. International Intrigue reports that bonds issued by the AI data centre firm CoreWeave are now trading at yields around fifteen percent, and Oracle fell nine percent in a single session. The IMF notes that the big hyperscalers now account for ten percent of new investment grade corporate bonds, up from one percent two years ago. Yet TSMC posted record quarterly profit, up sixty nine percent. Chip makers are still the people selling the pickaxes. Via International Intrigue.

Now, over to startups and venture.

Section three, startups and venture.

Where the week landed: a hunt for what is actually defensible, and a little less patience with creative accounting.

First, moats. Investors at the Unlock VC summit told Sifted that a product alone is no longer defensible when building is cheap, so they look instead for go to market strength, brand and talent. Giant rounds are clustering around researchers who have just left DeepMind. Via Sifted.

Second, the revenue question. Sifted found European defence tech investors worried about blurry reporting, with some start-ups blurring bookings and recognised revenue, or counting grant funding as defence revenue. The investor advice was simple: accounting is accounting, so ask the question. Via Sifted.

Third, Europe's money. The British Business Bank has put more than a hundred million pounds into AI and deep tech venture funds, and Nvidia announced stakes in five UK companies as part of a planned two billion dollars of European investment. Sifted also published its list of Europe's hundred standout AI start-ups, topped by Stockholm's Tandem Health, with thirteen of last year's list now unicorns. Via Sifted.

Fourth, a cautionary tale. Character dot AI filed for Chapter Eleven bankruptcy, with Disney making a twenty million dollar opening bid, and Bumble is reportedly in talks to go private at about one billion dollars. Attention is not a business model. Via The Rundown and TLDR.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

Section four, policy and geopolitics.

Where the week landed: sanctions, chokepoints and sovereignty, with little sign of calm.

First, energy. President Trump eased sanctions to let Russian diesel reach world markets, announcing an energy ceasefire that Ukraine says it was not told about, with Brent crude at its highest since nineteen ninety one. Houthi strikes on Riyadh's airport killed thirteen, and several oil chief executives quietly dropped out of a congress in the city. Via International Intrigue.

Second, trade. Beijing has agreed to cap its hybrid car exports to the European Union under a four year quota, but the sharp observation is that production may simply move inside Europe's walls, through Chinese brand factories now being built there. Via International Intrigue.

Third, China and its diplomatic partners. Beijing froze assets and banned visas for cabinet ministers from Paraguay and Belize over their ties with Taiwan. Mostly symbolic, but it raises the personal cost of backing Taipei. In the Pacific, New Zealand is sending a team to help Fiji overhaul its security agencies after China linked hackers reached a Suva based regional organisation. Via International Intrigue.

Fourth, a number to treat carefully. A study from a United States industry group claims European cloud sovereignty rules could cost the continent a hundred and fifteen billion euros over a decade. It is a modelled scenario from an interested party, but expect it in Brussels debates. Via TLDR.

Now, over to community, events and marketing.

Section five, one idea worth stealing.

Where the week landed: connection is being designed, and sold, deliberately.

First, don't send the application, build the proof. Sophie Rogers wanted a community manager job at Lovable. Her first attempt, waiting outside the Stockholm office with a box of cakes, did not work. So she started a video series, walking around Stockholm drinking tea with founders, grew an audience, and applied again. A small recurring series that features the people you want to work with is a better calling card than a cover letter. Via Sifted.

Second, event design. Fukuoka Now's roundtable is timed to a live local issue, opens with one easy question, what is one thing you would change, and keeps the openers short. And Timeleft charges nineteen ninety nine a month to seat strangers at dinner, keeping names private until the night. Design the introduction, not just the venue. Via Fukuoka Now and Skift.

Now, one last thing.

Section six, one interesting thing.

Adam Mastroianni of Experimental History takes on the fear that AI will take all the jobs, pointing to the lump of labour fallacy, the mistaken idea that there is a fixed pile of work to be done. If that were true, unemployment should have climbed ever since the Industrial Revolution, yet it sits roughly where it did eighty years ago. His explanation is that human wants are endless: give a mouse a cookie and it asks for milk. We simply cannot imagine the desires we will have once the current ones are met. Via Experimental History.
