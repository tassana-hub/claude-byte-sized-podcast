Starting with Japan and Asia-Pacific. New Delhi's Great Nicobar Project is being read by regional watchers as India's formal arrival as an Indian Ocean power, a signal that the archipelago at the mouth of the Malacca Strait is becoming a genuine strategic asset rather than a remote outpost, as India builds out infrastructure that lets it project influence along one of the world's busiest shipping corridors. The move sits alongside a steady run of Indian Ocean activity from rival and allied navies alike, and it is the kind of quiet infrastructure story that tends to matter far more in five years than it does the week it is announced.

Over in Seoul, CSIS convened its Capital Cable series this week to take stock of one year of the Lee Jae Myung administration, weighing how the government has handled its early foreign policy and economic choices against the expectations it set on taking office. A year in is normally when a government's actual priorities, as opposed to its campaign promises, become visible, and this is exactly the kind of stocktaking that is worth watching for anyone tracking how Seoul balances its relationships with Washington and Beijing.

And closer to home for Japanese founders, the research firm SPEEDA, part of Uzabase, is bringing together three corporate innovation leads this month, from JGC Holdings, Keio Corporation and Mitsubishi Materials, to dig into a problem familiar to anyone who has tried to launch a new business inside a large company: the original plan almost never survives contact with customers, markets and internal politics unchanged. Their shared argument is that the real skill large firms need is not the ability to execute a fixed vision, but the organisational muscle to keep exploring and revising judgment as assumptions shift, whether that means building a corporate venture arm, as Keio has done, or systematically connecting new technology to market need, as Mitsubishi Materials has tried to do. It is a useful reminder that Japan's biggest companies are still actively wrestling with how to make internal entrepreneurship work, rather than treating it as solved.

Now, over to A.I. and tech, where the week's biggest story is a lawsuit rather than a launch. Apple has sued OpenAI, accusing it of stealing confidential trade secrets and unreleased hardware information through former Apple employees, one of whom allegedly kept access to Apple's internal network after leaving, using a previously unknown authentication flaw and a colleague's still-active credentials to get back in. Apple is seeking damages, the destruction of any misused material, and changes to OpenAI's future hardware if Apple's technology shows up in it. The commentary site The Diff points out that when a company wants to prove theft without revealing exactly what was taken, the trick is to make the circumstantial picture of bad behaviour as damning as possible, and Apple's filing does precisely that. It is an awkward moment for a relationship that still includes OpenAI powering parts of Apple Intelligence, even as the two increasingly compete on hardware.

Elsewhere in AI products this week, Figma acquired the team behind Bud, a vibe-coding and AI agent platform, folding it into its own push toward app-building tools, while Meta pulled its Muse image AI feature from Instagram and WhatsApp just three days after launch, after the tool let users generate images from any public account by default and drew a swift backlash from Hollywood talent agencies and performers.

And on the more reflective side, the newsletter Every argues that as AI agents take over the actual writing of code, the valuable human work left is polish: opening the finished feature yourself, using it as a real user would, and telling the agent what feels off until it doesn't. A model doesn't know what "good" means for your product and your users sitting in front of it, so that final layer of taste and judgment is becoming the part of software work that agents can't yet do for you. Via Every.

Now, over to startups and venture, where the week's best story is also its briefest. Sifted reported that the AI scientist Yann LeCun was launching a new fund, Extelligence Invest, backed by sources close to the deal and confirmed by LeCun's own spokesperson before publication. Within eight hours of that story going live, the fund's website had been taken down, LeCun's team said he had withdrawn over what they called existing exclusive relationships with other funds, and Extelligence itself announced it was shutting down the entire launch. By Saturday the website was back up, scrubbed of any names at all. It's a reminder of just how fast reputational plumbing can unwind once one thread gets pulled in public.

Underneath the drama, TLDR's founders newsletter carried a sharper structural point this week: foundation models, for all the capital and brilliance behind them, don't have a durable business model of their own, because every new release makes the last one obsolete, and enterprises are increasingly buying intelligence the way they buy electricity, as an interchangeable commodity. The flip side, the newsletter argues, is that the biggest financial winners from AI won't be AI companies at all, but low-margin businesses that finally have a cheap way to attack the coordination costs that have kept them structurally unprofitable for decades.

And in a smaller but telling consolidation move, Booking Holdings is forming a new business-to-business unit that brings Booking.com, Agoda and Priceline together to power travel offerings for other companies, putting Agoda's chief executive in charge of an effort to catch up with Expedia's already sizeable B2B arm, which grew three times faster last quarter than its consumer business. Via Skift.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics, where CSIS hosted a panel this week on the two Section 301 investigations the US Trade Representative is running into forced labour and industrial overcapacity, with China a central target in both. Panellists compared the current probe to the 2017 to 2018 investigation into Chinese intellectual property theft, and the framing question running through it was whether Washington's legal, economic and diplomatic toolkit is any better suited this time to managing a trade relationship that keeps generating fresh points of friction.

And CSIS's Futures Lab published a striking piece of geographic analysis on Russia's missile and drone campaign in Ukraine, mapping oblast-level damage reports from 2023 through early this year to show how Moscow is concentrating strikes on frontline and southern regions, industrial and energy infrastructure, ports and major cities. The pattern suggests a deliberate coercive logic: using long-range firepower not just for battlefield effect but to strain Ukraine's ability to repair, govern and negotiate, with cheap Shahed-type drones letting Russia sustain a far higher tempo of attacks than precision missiles alone would allow.

Now, over to community, events and marketing, where two ideas are worth stealing this week rather than just noting. The facilitator Priya Parker argues that a single well-run gathering, whether a family meeting, a celebration or an offsite, works because it gives people connection instead of performance: focus on a small group of the right people rather than a large group of the wrong ones, and let meaning and fun sit alongside each other rather than treating them as competing goals.

And the CMX community newsletter closed out its webinar series with a sharper version of the same point aimed at workplaces. One guest speaker's take was that engagement metrics aren't the outcome of community work, they're just the fuel, and that reporting pipeline influence and retention instead of registrations and badges is what actually gets leadership to invest. Another argued that community managers aren't generalists who failed to specialise, they're the only people in a company who sit in the middle of marketing's story, product's story and everyone else's, and that vantage point is a genuine strength rather than a lack of focus. Via CMX.

Finally, one interesting thing to take you into the day. The economist Tyler Cowen gave a talk at DeepMind this month reflecting on what he calls an exhaustion of meaning rather than a shortage of it. His argument is that as models improve at a relentless pace, the problem for many people won't be finding purpose, it will be coping with how much purpose, stimulation and pressure to keep up a fast-moving world now generates, especially for anyone young enough to fear falling behind if they don't jump on each new wave immediately. He points to his own working life as evidence: he says he is busier now because of AI, not despite it, driven by a wage gradient that rewards engaging with each new model the moment it appears. The eventual leisure dividend from all this technology may well arrive, he suggests, but probably on a much longer horizon than the exhaustion that comes first.
