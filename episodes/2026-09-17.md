Japan and Asia-Pacific.

First up, a proper deep dive from the newsletter Uncharted Territories on why Japan managed to become one of the most densely populated countries on Earth despite having almost no farmland. Only twelve percent of Japan's land is farmable, yet the country holds the twelfth largest population on the planet. The answer turns out to be geology: the collision of the Pacific and Asian tectonic plates created deep, fertile river valleys in the south, while two ocean currents meeting off Honshu brought both the nutrients that built Japan's fishing culture and the warmth that let rice farming push further north than the latitude should really allow. Japan's relative isolation as an island chain also meant fewer wars and disease waves than its neighbours faced, letting population and irrigation infrastructure grow together for well over a thousand years. It's also, apparently, why sushi is the most Japanese food there is: rice moving north from the south, fish moving south from the north, meeting in the middle. Via Uncharted Territories.

Next, a sharper-edged piece from the security think tank C S I S on Asia's simmering maritime tensions. With President Xi due in Washington next week, analyst Harrison Pretat argues the Trump administration risks repeating the mistake it made with Iran: staying quiet on maritime coercion until a crisis forces its hand. Over the past year China has restarted island building in the Paracel Islands, quadrupled coast guard patrols around Scarborough Shoal, and established a persistent coast guard presence east of Taiwan that has already interfered with hundreds of commercial ships. The warning is blunt: if maritime security doesn't make the Trump-Xi summit agenda, expect Beijing to keep pushing, much as it did in the Strait of Hormuz. Via CSIS.

And a smaller but symbolically large shift, flagged by International Intrigue: Japan's defence minister has said Tokyo won't rule out nuclear-powered submarines. It's only a modest crack in a taboo that has held since 1945, but it lands just as China, the United States, and soon South Korea all operate such submarines in the very same waters. Elsewhere in Japan, the health ministry reported that the number of centenarians has topped one hundred thousand for the first time, a milestone officials are calling both a triumph and a warning sign for an ageing economy. Via International Intrigue.

Now, over to A I and tech.

A new kind of A I model launched this week that isn't trying to chat with you at all. TypeSafe, founded by an ex-OpenAI researcher who helped build the technology behind ChatGPT, unveiled Jev, what it calls a System One model: built purely for fast, cheap, structured decisions inside software, like sorting requests or screening outputs, rather than for conversation. It responds in under a second, is priced far below existing large language models, and its maker claims it can't hallucinate because it only ever picks from a fixed set of preset options. The bet is on Jevons paradox: make judgment calls this cheap, and software will simply start using vastly more of them. Via TLDR and The Rundown.

Inside OpenAI itself, a deep dive from The Pragmatic Engineer finds that its coding agent Codex has, in the space of about six months, gone from a nice-to-have to being how almost everyone at the company works, including finance, legal and recruiting staff who have never written a line of code. OpenAI has built what it calls a software factory: agents gather context, write code, run tests, review each other's pull requests as simulated domain specialists, and then babysit deployment into production. Engineers there increasingly describe their job as closer to product management than programming, and the volume of pull requests per engineer is climbing at a pace that would normally take a company years, not months, to absorb. Via Pragmatic Engineer.

And at Salesforce's Dreamforce conference, Slack unveiled a major push to turn itself into what it calls a true A I teammate: bots working inside channels, full screen A I workspaces, live data surfaces pulled from Salesforce, and a new Slack Code feature where developers and agents write and review code together. It's a sign of where enterprise software is heading more broadly, away from seat-based licensing and toward tools accessed through agents and conversation, which is already forcing chief information officers to rethink governance, permissions, and how they even budget for software. Via TLDR.

Now, over to startups and venture.

A new database from the newsletter The VC Corner mapped one thousand five hundred and three American early stage investors, and the numbers explain why founders describe the Series A crunch as brutal. For every fund that leads a Series A round, there are roughly five point three earlier stage investors writing smaller checks and feeding companies toward that narrow gate. California and New York alone account for over half of all mapped investors, though Arizona, driven almost entirely by pre-seed programmes, has quietly overtaken Massachusetts in total investor count. Via The VC Corner.

The same newsletter also flagged a fast growing niche: fundraising advisors who work purely on success fees, no retainers, paid only if a round actually closes. The list runs to one hundred and fifty advisors, evidence that with roughly half the market having raised under a million dollars last year, a whole cottage industry of vetted, contingency based fundraising help has sprung up around the smallest deals. Via The VC Corner.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

The biggest geopolitical story this week is unfolding in Yemen. International Intrigue reports that the Houthis have launched a lightning offensive along Yemen's Red Sea coast, seizing the port of Mokha, the town of Dhubab, and the island of Perim, giving them a direct line of fire over ships transiting the Bab el Mandeb strait, one of the world's busiest oil corridors. Combined with the ongoing disruption in the Strait of Hormuz, that puts roughly a quarter of global oil trade within reach of Iran and its allies, and oil prices have already jumped back above one hundred and eight dollars a barrel. Saudi Arabia's options all look bad: wade back into a Yemeni civil war it spent a decade trying to exit, or watch its own economic ambitions take the hit. Via International Intrigue.

Closer to home for policy watchers, CSIS's Trade Guys podcast dug into a trio of trade stories: the White House's executive orders on beef imports, a Russia sanctions bill still stalled in Congress, and a growing push for closer trade ties between Canada and Europe, using Italian pasta tariffs as a case study in how unevenly the administration enforces trade rules depending on the political relationship involved. Via CSIS.

And economist Alex Tabarrok used Marginal Revolution to push back on a popular theory, that when Anthropic's Dario Amodei calls for A I safety regulation, he's really just angling for regulatory capture. Tabarrok argues that classic regulatory capture takes years of quiet lobbying after public attention has already moved on, which is the opposite of what's happening now, with A I regulation still in its earliest, most politically charged phase, and with researchers who have nothing to sell, including Turing Award winner Geoffrey Hinton, voicing the same worries. It lands alongside a related warning from Sifted, that as A I agents move from research demos to mainstream use in barely two years, Europe's landmark A I rulebook is already struggling to keep pace with systems that can act with limited human oversight. Via Marginal Revolution and Sifted.

Now, over to community, events and marketing.

From Marketing Brew, a nice reminder that a good origin story beats a good advert: pickle brand Grillo's has built much of its online following by leaning into the specific, slightly odd detail of its own brine recipe rather than generic product shots. One idea worth stealing: find the one weirdly specific detail about how your product is actually made, and let that carry the whole story. Via Marketing Brew.

And community building writer David Spinks published a piece worth sitting with if you lean on A I daily. He describes a creeping brain fog and disconnection after months of constant A I use, arguing that talking to an assistant all day, however useful, is empty social calories that can quietly erode the muscle of doing your own thinking and talking to actual people. One idea worth stealing: audit which of your daily A I habits are genuinely saving you time, and which ones have just quietly taken over decisions you used to make yourself. Via David Spinks.

Now, over to one interesting thing.

To close, from the newsletter Experimental History: we may not actually be living through a loneliness epidemic, the data behind that claim is far shakier than the headlines suggest, but we are living through something the writer calls an epidemic of social scurvy. Just like vitamin C, humans evolved without much of a built in craving for company, because for most of history you simply couldn't survive without other people anyway. Strip that necessity away, as modern life has done, and you can quietly wither without ever consciously feeling lonely enough to do anything about it, the same way scurvy sufferers waste away without craving citrus. The fix, apparently, is the same as with thirst: don't wait to feel the need, just show up anyway. Via Experimental History.
