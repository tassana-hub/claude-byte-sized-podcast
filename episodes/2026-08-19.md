Hello, and welcome to Byte-sized for Wednesday, the nineteenth of August. Six sections today, so let's get straight into it.

First up, Japan and Asia-Pacific.

Japan has taken its first real step toward rebuilding its intelligence capabilities. On the thirty first of July, the government formally launched a new National Intelligence Bureau, one of several planned reforms championed by Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi, who has treated intelligence as a strategic priority since taking office last autumn. CSIS analysis notes that supporters see this as essential groundwork for Japan to contribute more to the alliance with Washington, while critics worry it does not fix the underlying weaknesses in Japanese intelligence and could weaken oversight at home. Whether the reform succeeds will depend on whether Takaichi can also pass a promised anti espionage law and stand up a proper foreign intelligence service, but for now it signals a government serious about adapting to a changing security picture. Via CSIS.

Energy security is the other lens on the region this week. CSIS's energy programme is warning that the ongoing standoff over the Strait of Hormuz is reshaping the global market for liquefied natural gas, and a dedicated briefing this week looked specifically at what that means for Japan and South Korea, two of the countries most exposed to any disruption of Middle Eastern gas supplies. Both governments are now having to think hard about energy policy in a world where that chokepoint can no longer be taken for granted. Via CSIS.

And tension on the Korean peninsula is far from resolved. CSIS's Korea Chair called an emergency episode of its Capital Cable series this week to unpack a fast moving set of signals: continuing military exercises, a defiant Kim Jong-un, and the knock-on effects of the Iran conflict on how Washington is managing the peninsula. Separately, CSIS's nuclear policy briefing flagged that Washington has ordered a substantial scaling back of joint exercises with Seoul, even as analysts warn that both South Korea and Japan are increasingly weighing whether they need nuclear weapons of their own, in a world where old arms control agreements, like the expired START treaty, no longer hold much sway over the region. Via CSIS.

Now, over to AI and tech.

Cursor has begun rolling out a new code hosting platform called Origin to paying users, letting developers keep GitHub as their system of record while trialling an alternative underneath it. The timing could not have been more pointed: the launch landed in the middle of a GitHub outage that knocked out repositories, application programming interfaces and Copilot for more than six hours. Alongside that, word emerged that Anthropic's annualised revenue is now on track to exceed sixty five billion dollars, more than seven times where it stood at the end of last year, one of the clearest signs yet of how fast enterprise AI spending is compounding. Via TLDR.

Payments giant Stripe has finalised a deal worth more than seven billion dollars to acquire OpenRouter, the startup that lets companies switch easily between different AI models, pulling Stripe deeper into AI infrastructure and underlining just how much scrutiny AI costs are now under industry wide. In a separate story about caution rather than speed, Anthropic said it has no plans to release an internally discussed, more powerful model, arguing the risks from its current systems remain low even as its models show clear signs of accelerating automated research ability, while OpenAI said it is deliberately slowing the rollout of an upcoming model, called Astra, because it cannot yet rule out dangerous cyber capabilities. Via TLDR and This Week in Startups.

Meanwhile, one small AI native company is offering a candid look at what that spending spree actually feels like from the inside. The head of operations at the newsletter Every wrote that the company's AI credit usage jumped two hundred and thirty percent in the days after a new flagship model rolled out, and rather than imposing hard spending caps, the team settled on a looser approach: give everyone visibility into what they are spending, ask what it bought and what was learned, and only tighten the rules once trust breaks down. It is a useful real world data point for any organisation currently guessing at what an AI budget should even look like. Via Every.

And a lighter note on what one person with the right prompts can now build alone. On Lenny's Newsletter, solo founder Yana Welinder described running an entire AI native fashion brand without a single engineer, using ChatGPT and the coding tool Codex to turn hand sketches into product images, operate professional three dimensional design software she had never learned, and manage manufacturer outreach and a full e-commerce build. Her core lesson: the more precisely you can describe what good looks like before you start, the better both the AI and any human collaborator will deliver. Via Lenny's Newsletter.

Now, over to startups and venture.

Vibe coding platform Lovable has raised another four hundred million dollars in a Series C round led by Menlo Ventures and the Scaleup Europe Fund, confirming a fresh valuation of thirteen point three billion dollars just months after its previous round. The company says it now hosts sixty million projects and draws nine hundred million monthly visitors, backed by its own in house model and a multi year Google Cloud deal, another sign of how quickly AI coding tools are scaling from novelty to infrastructure. Via TLDR.

European fintech is having its own moment of financial one-upmanship. Revolut founder Nik Storonsky's venture firm, QuantumLight, has closed its second fund at five hundred million dollars, part of a wider wave of European fintechs publicising annual recurring revenue milestones the way AI companies have been doing all year, from banking infrastructure firm Thought Machine to spend management platform Payhawk. Via Sifted.

And on The Generalist's podcast, Rain chief executive Farooq Malik talked through building financial infrastructure powered by stablecoins, an idea he backed years before stablecoins became mainstream. Rain now issues cards and payments on stablecoin rails, and Malik argues the company is well positioned for a future where AI agents themselves need to move money, framing the payments market as large enough for many winners rather than a single one, Stripe included. Via The Generalist.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

Geopolitical Monitor's weekly briefing frames the Iran conflict as increasingly a war of economic attrition, with the standoff over the Strait of Hormuz determining whether Qatar ends up the war's biggest winner or its biggest loser, depending on how long the disruption to shipping and gas exports drags on. The same briefing flags Ebola's spread to a sixth province in the Democratic Republic of Congo and Washington's effort to turn its Pax Silica AI initiative into something closer to an exclusive club of partners, alongside a new warning from the Food and Agriculture Organisation about rising global food prices. Via Geopolitical Monitor.

Brazil's presidential race has formally kicked off, and International Intrigue's briefing frames it as a genuine coin toss. Incumbent Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva is seeking a fourth term on a pro worker platform, casting himself as a defence against both Washington's renewed interventionism and the illiberal populism of his rival, Flavio Bolsonaro, son of the imprisoned former president. Bolsonaro is pledging tough on crime policies and pardons for his father's allies, and runoff polls have narrowed to a technical tie. The stakes reach well beyond Brazil: the country is a major source of critical minerals like niobium and the world's top exporter of soybeans, coffee and sugar, making the outcome a genuine swing factor in the broader contest between Washington and Beijing for influence in Latin America. Via International Intrigue.

And CSIS's nuclear policy briefing offers a sobering wide shot on where global arms control now stands. New modelling reported there suggests a full scale intercontinental missile exchange between Russia and the United States could now kill more than half the world's population, even as commentary elsewhere in the same briefing argues the old global nuclear order is effectively dead, with the START treaty expired and China unwilling to negotiate on arms control. The same briefing also described a new US-Saudi nuclear cooperation agreement as a carefully limited legal pathway for American exports rather than a step toward Saudi enrichment capability, while a separate analysis argued Iran's most underappreciated weapons route may not be uranium at all, but the plutonium sitting at its Bushehr power reactor. Via CSIS.

Now, over to community, events and marketing, one idea worth stealing today.

With AI generated content everywhere, brands are reaching for the opposite aesthetic to stand out. TLDR Marketing reports that companies across food, cleaning products, luxury hospitality and even AI firms themselves, Claude included, are leaning into deliberately handmade looking design: hand drawn typography, imperfect textures, retro illustration. The catch is that it only works when it actually fits the brand behind it; used without any real meaning, handmade cues just read as another generic trend. Via TLDR Marketing.

A second idea worth stealing, this one about landing pages. A financial services company swapped a calculator focused headline for a simple human question, "Is equity release right for me?", and introduced a real member of its information team on the page. The change lifted completed leads by seventy nine percent, a reminder that when customers need reassurance before acting, putting a real person's face and expertise in front of them can outperform any amount of clever copy. Via TLDR Marketing.

Now, one interesting thing to leave you with.

The newsletter Experimental History has a lovely piece this week on what makes art or writing feel thick rather than thin, meaning it rewards attention rather than exhausting itself after a single glance. Its favourite illustration is a five hundred year old Hieronymus Bosch painting with an actual, if deliberately unplayable, piece of music painted onto a demon's backside, a joke a modern internet sleuth stumbled onto centuries later and transcribed. The larger argument is that thickness comes from roads not taken, reasons hidden beneath the surface, and something genuinely paid to make it, which is exactly why AI generated slop, however polished on the surface, tends to evaporate the moment you actually look closely. Via Experimental History.
