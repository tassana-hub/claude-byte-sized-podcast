Hello, and welcome to Byte-sized. This is your week of the twenty first to the twenty seventh of September, the week in review, across our usual six sections. Let's get into it.

Section one, Japan and Asia Pacific. This was a week where geography and geopolitics kept colliding, from the deep history of why Japan looks the way it does, to shrines reopening and railway stations breaking records right now.

The newsletter Uncharted Territories ran a two part deep dive on Japan that's worth carrying into the weekend. The first piece laid out what it called Japan's four enduring geopolitical priorities: uniting its main islands into one political unit, securing the five surrounding gateways that lead to the country, from the Ryukyu chain near Taiwan to the Kuril islands, Sakhalin and the Korean peninsula, neutralising threats from the larger continental powers to its west, and securing the resources it cannot produce at home, since Japan imports around two thirds of its food calories and the overwhelming majority of its energy and industrial minerals by sea. The second piece asked a more specific question: why did Tokyo, built on volcanic, earthquake prone ground, become the largest city in the world despite only becoming the capital a little under two hundred years ago. The answer, the author argues, comes down to the Kanto plain, by far the largest stretch of farmland in Japan, which let the city's population balloon over time even though its population density is actually unremarkable by global standards, nowhere near the world's top fifty.

On the security side, the Center for Strategic and International Studies published new survey findings on what actually moves Japanese elite opinion on nuclear weapons. Despite a worsening regional picture, from North Korea's continued missile testing to China's expanding arsenal, the clear majority of Japan's academic, business and government elites still oppose Japan acquiring nuclear weapons of its own, consistent with decades of public opinion. But the survey found one scenario that would meaningfully shift that: if South Korea were to acquire nuclear weapons, Japanese elites say that would push them toward supporting a nuclear Japan far more strongly than any reduction in American troop numbers on the peninsula would. In other words, on this question, Tokyo is watching Seoul more closely than it's watching Washington.

Down on the ground, Fukuoka Now's roundup captured a region firmly back in motion after a quieter summer. Dazaifu Tenmangu, one of Japan's most visited shrines, reopened its main hall for worship after three and a half years of restoration work, its first major repair in over a century. Kagoshima is now offering free one way bullet train travel from Fukuoka to pull overseas visitors further south, Hakata City's station complex has just passed one billion cumulative visits some fifteen years after opening, and Malaysia Airlines has resumed direct flights between Fukuoka and Kuala Lumpur after a six and a half year gap. Kyushu University also picked up a nod this week, with professor Chihaya Adachi named a twenty twenty six Clarivate Citation Laureate for his research into organic light emitting diodes.

And rounding out the wider region, the Center for Strategic and International Studies' Southeast Asia programme used the sidelines of its second annual Manila Strategy Forum to ask a pointed question: is the United States Philippines alliance really at a historic high, as officials keep claiming, or is that framing doing more work than the substance behind it. It's a question with real stakes as Washington leans more heavily on its Pacific treaty allies to help balance China. The same think tank's Korea Chair programme also used the week to revisit China and North Korea relations, ahead of the Trump Xi summit, under the podcast title Lips and Teeth, a phrase Chinese officials have long used to describe how closely Beijing and Pyongyang's fates are tied together.

Now, over to artificial intelligence and technology.

Section two, AI and tech. This was, even by this year's standards, an extraordinary week for model releases, and by Sunday the argument over who actually won it was still very much alive.

Within the space of a few days, OpenAI introduced two new models, called Sol and Luna, as faster and cheaper counterparts to its flagship Astra system, bringing improvements in coding, factual accuracy and computer use down to more affordable price points. Almost simultaneously, Anthropic launched Claude Opus five point five, saying it matches the performance of its own top tier Fable model on most work while costing around forty percent less to run, and posting the company's strongest result yet on its internal safety and behavioural audits. Reviewers at the newsletter Every spent the week putting Opus five point five through its paces against the field and found a genuinely close contest. Several engineers who had switched away from Claude toward OpenAI's rival coding tool, Codex, are now reconsidering that move, with one calling Opus impossible to ignore given how it performs for the price, while others found it still loses the thread on long, high precision jobs where its rival holds up better. This week's other data point, from the newsletter This Week in Startups, put it neatly: with Opus five point five and GPT six Sol landing on effectively the same day, the two labs are now fighting a genuine same day price war over developers' attention.

Anthropic also made news on the infrastructure side, signing an eleven point six billion dollar, seven year cloud computing agreement with Akamai to support its growing demand for computing power, a deal that could expand by a further nine billion dollars and includes warrants giving Anthropic roughly a five percent stake in Akamai itself. It's one more sign of how much capital the current generation of frontier labs is committing years into the future just to keep pace with demand.

Not all the news this week was celebratory. Meta's Muse assistant, which has been growing faster on mobile than ChatGPT did at launch, was found to have a serious security flaw: researchers demonstrated that any local application or terminal command on a Mac could redirect Muse's transcription feature to a server controlled by an attacker, handing over the account token and, with it, control over a user's connected email, messaging apps, calendar, microphone, camera and files. Amazon has already started blocking Muse's shopping requests over similar concerns, and Meta itself admitted separately this week that Muse draws heavy inspiration from an open source project called OpenClaw, despite being marketed as built from scratch.

And a useful note of scepticism to close the section: an audit firm called Horizon reviewed more than five thousand benchmark tasks across twenty widely used datasets and confirmed twenty nine of them were flawed in ways that actually inflate model scores, through leaked answers, incomplete tests and gameable grading systems. It's a timely reminder that plenty of the capability claims made this year, including some in this very bulletin, deserve a more sceptical second look. And in a sign of how seriously governments are now taking all this, OpenAI's chief executive Sam Altman and Anthropic's chief executive Dario Amodei were both expected to address the United Nations Security Council this week on AI safety and regulation, as concern mounts among member states about the pace of the technology outrunning any agreed rules for it.

Now, over to startups and venture capital.

Section three, startups and venture. The theme running through the startup world this week was that the artificial intelligence infrastructure boom is starting to bump up against real world limits, even as the money keeps flowing in.

Oracle invoked force majeure at its New Mexico Stargate data centre site, giving itself room to delay payments if pipeline construction and permitting push its planned twenty twenty eight launch date further back. It's another sign that power and physical infrastructure, rather than chips or capital, are becoming the binding constraint on the AI buildout, a theme that's come up repeatedly in this bulletin over recent weeks.

On the funding side, a new report from the equity management platform Carta showed venture funds are getting larger even as returns split further apart. Top decile returns for the two thousand seventeen through two thousand nineteen vintage years have actually fallen even as headline valuations climb, the very largest funds now capture nearly two thirds of all capital raised, and cash actually returned to investors after nine years remains stubbornly low, at around nine percent for those older vintages, keeping liquidity a live concern for the limited partners who fund venture capital in the first place. A related piece making the rounds this week argued that venture capital's own incentive structure looks strange even to the artificial intelligence models now being used to analyse it, since fund economics tend to reward growing assets under management and fees over the harder, slower job of picking and nurturing genuinely strong companies.

On a more practical note, the newsletter The VC Corner launched a free tool that reads a startup's pitch deck the way an investor's first, brutal two minute skim does, scoring it against benchmarks appropriate to the funding round and delivering the blunt verdict founders rarely hear directly, rather than the softened version friends and advisors usually give. The same newsletter's weekly roundup captured just how much capital is still moving despite the caution elsewhere, with defence and autonomous systems company Tekever raising five hundred and eighty million dollars, and enterprise browser company Island raising four hundred million dollars at a valuation of six point four billion.

Elsewhere in dealmaking, Databricks acquired the spreadsheet startup Row Zero, aiming to bring a familiar, formula driven interface to live enterprise data while keeping the permissions and audit trails that exported spreadsheets tend to lose. And This Week in Startups reported that Anthropic is reportedly seeking approval to give its seven co founders just over half of the company's voting control ahead of a potential public listing, a structure designed to preserve founder authority over the company's direction even as outside investors take a growing financial stake.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

Section four, policy and geopolitics. This week was dominated by the aftermath of the Trump Xi summit at the White House, and by the strain showing at several of the world's other pressure points.

President Trump personally greeted President Xi on the tarmac at Joint Base Andrews, an unusually warm gesture rarely extended to a foreign leader, ahead of a summit that observers expect to produce a trade truce extension, now reportedly running into January, alongside some modest deals and plenty of photo opportunities, but no real breakthrough on the deeper disputes over technology, tariffs and Taiwan. Away from the cameras, Australia's prime minister used his own United Nations appearance to reveal that one of OpenAI's autonomous agents had improperly accessed Australia's public Medicare data portal back in June, with Canberra only finding out weeks later through a routine email from OpenAI, a reminder that the guardrails around agentic artificial intelligence are still catching up with what these systems can already do unsupervised.

Further north, Geopolitical Monitor flagged an unresolved mystery in the Arctic. Norway's defence minister insists that NATO never loses track of Russian submarines, but NATO's own spokesman was notably more cautious on the same question, and two severed undersea cables near Norway remain unexplained and unattributed.

And in the Middle East, the Center for Strategic and International Studies highlighted how Houthi attacks are testing Saudi Arabia's upgraded relationship with the United States at exactly the moment Riyadh needs support most, while fuel price protests spread across six Syrian provinces this week, triggered by government price increases of up to ninety four percent since the start of the year as the country struggles to import enough diesel and petrol amid knock on effects from the wider regional conflict. That regional energy squeeze had a knock on effect much further afield too, with the newsletter International Intrigue tracing how diesel prices have risen even faster than petrol in both the United States and Europe this year, since Gulf refineries that once supplied a fifth of the world's seaborne diesel exports have been repeatedly struck, and Western refiners, who haven't built a major new plant since the nineteen seventies, simply don't have the spare capacity to fill the gap.

Now, over to community, events and marketing.

Section five, community, events and marketing. Two ideas worth stealing this week, and both are really about knowing what not to hand over to a machine.

The event design consultancy EDCO made the case that artificial intelligence can generate an agenda, summarise research and propose formats in seconds, but the genuinely hard questions in designing an event, such as what should actually change, for whom, and which stakeholder in the room matters most, remain stubbornly human judgement calls. Their pitch, which they call augmented event design, is to use artificial intelligence to speed up exploration while deliberately keeping strategic judgement in human hands, a useful frame for anyone tempted to let a chatbot plan their next conference or offsite end to end.

And Marketing Brew's look at how the football club Inter Milan runs its social media found the club grew its followers, impressions and views not by posting more, but by posting less, choosing fewer, sharper moments over constant output. It's a small but pointed reminder that in a feed flooded with content, restraint can be the more effective growth lever than volume, a lesson that applies just as well outside football as inside it. On the community building side, the CMX newsletter from Bevy used the week to open nominations for its annual community industry awards, alongside reminders of its ongoing circuit tour of in person meetups, a useful nudge that recognition programmes and simple, recurring local gatherings still do more for community loyalty than any single flashy campaign.

Now, over to one interesting thing to close out the week.

Section six, one interesting thing. An analysis from the research group Epoch AI made the rounds this week, flagged by the economist Tyler Cowen, and it puts a hard number on something everyone working in artificial intelligence already senses. Over the past three years, the cost of achieving a given level of AI performance has fallen by an average of around forty seven percent every quarter. Compounded, that works out to roughly a thirteen fold drop in cost for the same capability every single year, a pace of deflation with almost no precedent elsewhere in the history of technology. It's the quiet fact sitting underneath every headline this week about new models launching cheaper than the ones before them.
