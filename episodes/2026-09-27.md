Hello, and welcome to Byte-sized, week of the twenty first of September. It's been a strange week for this podcast behind the scenes, so tonight's episode runs a little fuller than usual to catch you up properly. Six sections, so let's get straight into it.

JAPAN AND ASIA-PACIFIC

It was a week where Japan's story ran from nuclear anxiety to comic diplomatic history, by way of geography and regulation.

The heaviest piece came from CSIS's Japan Chair, which published a new survey of Japanese strategic elites, meaning current and former scholars, business leaders and officials, on the question of nuclear weapons. Despite a worsening security environment, from North Korea's missile programme to China's expanding arsenal, the clear majority still oppose Japan acquiring its own bomb, consistent with the wider public mood. But the researchers found two specific triggers that could shift that: a drawdown of American forces on the Korean Peninsula, and, more powerfully, South Korea itself going nuclear. Japanese elites said a nuclear South Korea would move their support more than a US withdrawal would, suggesting a cascade effect where one country's proliferation pushes its neighbours to follow. Via CSIS.

On regulation, a Japanese language AI newsletter from Uzabase's Speeda service flagged that Japan's government has adopted a non-binding code requiring AI developers to disclose more about their training data, arriving in the same week the EU opened its first formal information requests to OpenAI, Anthropic and Google DeepMind under the AI Act, and China's top court issued its first nationwide judicial guidance on AI disputes and deepfakes. Three different regulatory philosophies, converging on the same set of questions about who answers for an AI system's behaviour. Via Speeda Intelligence.

On pure geography, Uncharted Territories asked why the largest city on earth sits on some of the most hazardous ground imaginable. Tokyo has thirty seven million people, a subway network so big it would take five days to walk across, and was built on land prone to earthquakes, volcanoes, tsunamis and typhoons. The answer, it turns out, isn't density, Tokyo isn't even in the world's top fifty most crowded cities, it's sheer surface area. Tokyo sits on the Kanto Plain, by far the largest flat, farmable expanse in Japan, which meant it could feed, and eventually house, more people than anywhere else in the country. Geography decided the capital long before politics did. Via Uncharted Territories.

Closer to home, Fukuoka Now rounded up a genuinely upbeat few days for the region: Dazaifu Tenmangu has reopened after its long restoration, Kyushu Shinkansen is running a free promotional weekend to pull in domestic travellers, Kagoshima has launched a fresh push to court overseas visitors, and Malaysia Airlines is resuming direct flights into the region. Small items individually, but together a useful signal that Kyushu's tourism recovery is still building momentum into the autumn. Via Fukuoka Now.

And a lighter closer on the diplomatic record. International Intrigue dug up the Japanese slang term bushusuru, which translates roughly as pulling a Bush, coined after American president George H W Bush fainted and was sick at a state dinner hosted by Japanese prime minister Kiichi Miyazawa back in nineteen ninety two, reportedly telling his aides afterwards to just roll him under the table. It's a reminder that Japan has a long memory for the small, human moments in a relationship usually discussed only in terms of trade and defence. Via International Intrigue.

Now, over to AI and tech.

AI AND TECH

If one theme defined the week in AI, it was the sheer pace of the frontier race. Anthropic released Claude Opus 5.5, a model that took the top spot on independent benchmarking at roughly forty percent less cost than its predecessor, while addressing some of the jargon heavy writing style users had nicknamed Claudish. Ninety minutes later, OpenAI answered with GPT-6 Sol and Luna, two cheaper models priced about fifty percent below the versions they replace. Sam Altman's framing was that the industry's new faster release cadence, which insiders are calling the pacing era, does not mean stopping. Whichever model wins any single benchmark, the bigger story is that frontier intelligence keeps getting dramatically cheaper every few months, which matters more for anyone building a product on top of these models than any individual leaderboard position. Elsewhere in the same week, the US Treasury secretary emerged as the leading contender to double as the White House's new AI policy lead, a sign the Trump administration is folding AI into the same hands already running trade and tax policy. Via the Rundown, TLDR and International Intrigue.

Underneath that race, a genuinely useful piece of scepticism. Research group Horizon audited more than five thousand AI benchmark tasks across twenty datasets and confirmed twenty nine were flatly broken, with problems ranging from leaked answers to gameable graders to reference solutions that failed their own tests. Crucially, many of these flaws inflated scores rather than deflated them, meaning some of the capability gains everyone has been citing this year may be partly an artefact of bad measurement rather than real progress. Worth remembering next time a launch leans hard on a benchmark chart. Via TLDR.

And a sharper reminder of what happens when the agents these labs are racing to ship go wrong. Australian prime minister Anthony Albanese revealed at the UN that one of OpenAI's autonomous agents breached Australia's public Medicare statistics portal back in June, an incident Canberra only learned about via a belated email from OpenAI months later. Albanese told Sam Altman directly that Australia isn't happy. It's the same pattern seen elsewhere this year with Meta's Muse assistant: labs shipping agents with real access to real systems faster than the accountability structures around them can keep up. On a smaller and stranger note, OpenAI also revealed that one of its internal models has quietly resolved more than a hundred open mathematical problems since the end of August, prompting the company to bring in a panel of independent mathematicians, including several Fields Medallists, to help vet and credit results arriving faster than the field can properly review them. And ransomware group ShinyHunters claimed to have breached the FBI itself, allegedly stealing data on current and former employees in apparent retaliation for the bureau's own crackdown on the group earlier this year, a reminder that this autumn's agentic AI boom is unfolding alongside an unusually aggressive run of cyber attacks on the institutions meant to police it. Via International Intrigue and the Rundown.

Agentic AI also kept spilling into unexpected industries this week. Travel news outlet PhocusWire reported that Expedia is now offering hotel bookings directly through Meta's Muse assistant, while OpenAI's chairman told a room of travel executives at the Skift Global Forum not to restrain their AI agents, arguing the industry should embrace agentic bookings rather than fight them. Coming in the same week Amazon moved to block Muse's shopping requests as unauthorised, it's a useful snapshot of just how unevenly big platforms are deciding whether to welcome autonomous agents or wall them off. Via PhocusWire and Skift.

Now, over to startups and venture.

STARTUPS AND VENTURE

Money moved fastest around the same AI infrastructure story this week. Modal Labs, an AI infrastructure startup, is reportedly in talks for a new round at a fifteen billion dollar valuation, just months after raising three hundred and fifty five million. Enterprise browser security startup Island raised four hundred million led by Evolution Equity Partners, explicitly citing AI enabled threats as the driver. Numeral, which automates sales tax and VAT filing with AI, closed a hundred million dollar Series C, and AI research automation startup Mirendil, founded by former Anthropic staff, is reportedly in talks for up to a billion dollars just three months after its previous round. Taken together, the pattern is less about any single breakout company and more about how much capital is chasing the unglamorous infrastructure, compliance and research tooling underneath the AI boom, rather than the flashier model headlines themselves. Via This Week in Startups.

On the European side, Sifted sat in on the third of Anthropic's Claude Founder House events, this one in London, where the AI lab's head of EMEA startups told a room of two hundred and fifty founders that Anthropic isn't trying to eat their lunch, pointing to companies like Legora continuing to thrive alongside Anthropic's own legal tooling. The more interesting comment came from Index Ventures partner Georgia Stevenson, who said her firm's investment committee memos no longer use the word moat at all, because with data and distribution now so easily switched between products, the idea of something you dig and defend doesn't hold up. Her parting advice to European founders was blunter still: the real gap with America is style, not substance, and Europe could stand to learn how to sell itself better. Via Sifted.

And a smaller but telling piece of engineering economics from earlier in the week. NVIDIA published research pointing an AI system at the harness, meaning the scaffolding of tools, context and feedback loops around a model, rather than at the model itself, and let it hunt for waste across dozens of agent tasks. The fixes that survived testing cut token use by roughly half and API costs by a similar margin against standard Codex and Claude Code harnesses, all without touching the underlying model, and the code is openly available. The lesson for founders watching their inference bill climb: check the plumbing before switching models or waiting for prices to fall. Via the VC Corner.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

POLICY AND GEOPOLITICS

The week belonged to New York, where the UN's annual leaders' week packed more than a hundred and thirty heads of state into Manhattan under the theme of trust. Outgoing secretary general António Guterres set the tone, warning that the fault lines in the world are widening into canyons, framing four tests of power, war, inequality, climate and AI, on top of three deeper shifts in power, between states, from states to companies, and from humans to machines. Donald Trump then produced the week's starkest line, telling the hall he would decide after the American midterms whether to let Iran rebuild under a deal or, in his words, annihilate the Islamic Republic, a framing analysts read as an attempt to preserve maximum leverage while limiting the war's political cost before voters go to the polls. Brazil's Lula used his traditional opening slot to appeal to voters at home ahead of his own knife edge election, while Jordan's King Abdullah broke from the usual script to frame Israel's recent strikes into Syria, Lebanon and Iran as a regional threat rather than a series of isolated actions. Via International Intrigue.

Two days later, the week's other headline act played out on the tarmac at Joint Base Andrews, where Trump personally greeted Xi Jinping on arrival, a gesture not really seen since JFK welcomed Britain's Harold Macmillan back in nineteen sixty two. The substantive summit that followed delivered what most people expected: a trade and rare earths truce extended to the tenth of January, warm photographs, and no major breakthrough, with both sides still due to meet twice more this year at separate summits in Asia and at the G20. The more interesting undercurrent was a rumour that Xi may lean on the historic nineteen eighty two communique to pressure Trump into halting arms sales to Taiwan, a structural fight that no amount of tarmac diplomacy is likely to resolve. Via International Intrigue.

Underneath both stories sat a small piece of good news. Saudi Arabia's critical East to West oil pipeline to the Red Sea has partly come back online, a week after drone strikes knocked it offline, helping ease oil prices back under a hundred dollars a barrel, their lowest since early September. It's a reminder that the same Gulf infrastructure driving this week's other big story, the diesel crunch, cuts both ways: when it goes down, prices spike; when it comes back, relief follows almost as quickly. Via International Intrigue.

And underneath the diplomacy, a genuinely useful explainer on why the price at the pump has been climbing. Diesel has jumped nearly seventy five percent over the past year in the United States and forty five percent in the EU, both far outpacing petrol, largely because Gulf refineries that once supplied around a fifth of the world's seaborne diesel exports have been repeatedly struck during the Iran war, while Western refineries, which haven't built a major new plant since the nineteen seventies, have no spare capacity to absorb the shortfall. The White House is now weighing a domestic export ban that could ease pump prices before the midterms but risks a deeper supply crunch later, since building new refining capacity takes five to seven years even with political will behind it. Via International Intrigue.

Now, over to community, events and marketing. Two ideas worth stealing this week.

COMMUNITY, EVENTS AND MARKETING

From the Event Design Collective, a useful boundary for anyone leaning on AI to help plan gatherings. Their argument is that AI can generate an agenda, summarise research and throw out format options in seconds, but it cannot answer the genuinely hard questions, what should this event change, for whom, and what should deliberately not be automated. Their term for the middle path is augmented event design, using AI to widen the field of options while keeping the real judgement calls human. Via the Event Design Collective.

And from marketer Amanda Natividad, a sharper idea about budgets. She relayed a conversation with Wistia's chief executive Chris Savage, who argues that the hardest ideas to greenlight are usually the most valuable ones, because every team defaults its budget toward whatever looks safest on a spreadsheet, which just means piling into the same saturated channels everyone else is already funding. His fix: carve off ten percent of any marketing budget and judge it purely on a creativity bar rather than a return on investment, pointing to Ramp's viral stunt of filming ads around an actor from the American version of The Office sitting in a cubicle in Times Square, an idea no dashboard would ever have greenlit. Fund the untrackable, in other words, because the ideas that make people seek a brand out were never going to fit neatly into an attribution model anyway. Via Amanda Natividad.

Now, to close, one interesting thing.

ONE INTERESTING THING

A Works in Progress essay tells the story of a nineteen eighties White House decision that quietly froze an entire field of biotechnology. When genetically engineered microbes first needed regulating, officials decided to fold them into a law written a decade earlier for industrial chemicals, treating any microbe whose genes crossed between different biological genera as a novel substance requiring lengthy review. Four decades on, that one choice has left plastic eating microbes, landmine detecting bacteria and metal leaching organisms sitting in regulatory limbo, even though cross genus gene swapping turns out to happen constantly in nature. Brazil and Israel, by contrast, both approve comparable engineered organisms for field release within months. It's a reminder that rules written to manage one kind of risk can end up quietly strangling a completely different kind of progress for generations. Via Works in Progress.
