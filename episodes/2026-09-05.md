JAPAN AND ASIA-PACIFIC

Japan's rare earth lesson for Washington. As the US-China trade truce nears its end, there's growing urgency in Washington to cut its dependence on Chinese rare earths. But analysts at the Center for Strategic and International Studies point to Japan as a cautionary tale. Despite more than a decade of stockpiling, offtake deals and overseas investment, Japan remains the world's largest importer of rare earths, and many of those supply chains still run straight through China. Building new mines and new processing technology at the same time, one expert notes, is simply not an overnight task, for Japan or for anyone else. Via the CSIS Critical Minerals Security Program.

Japan's answer to shrinking towns. With small towns emptying out across the developed world, Skift reports that Japan, Germany and Switzerland are testing whether an old Italian idea, the scattered hotel, can become a proper development tool rather than a one-off passion project. In Japan, the NIPPONIA network is converting clusters of empty houses into distributed hotel rooms spread across a village rather than gathered under one roof. The detail worth watching is Japan's cap of a hundred and eighty days a year on minpaku, or home-stay style lets, which may end up pushing more of these projects into full hotel licensing instead. Via Skift.

Palau hosts, Beijing bristles. The Pacific Islands Forum wrapped up its annual summit in Palau this week with a communique voicing concern over July's surprise ballistic missile test in the region, though it stopped short of naming China outright. Beijing sent its Pacific envoy, who publicly warned of consequences over Taiwan's presence at the summit, before softening his tone under pushback from Pacific leaders. Beyond the diplomatic sparring, the forum delivered real milestones: all eighteen member states are now connected to undersea data networks, and the United States committed a hundred and fifty million dollars to regional fuel storage, clean energy and port resilience. Via International Intrigue.

Now, over to AI and tech.

AI AND TECH

OpenAI's Astra crosses a safety threshold. OpenAI released GPT six Astra this week, its most capable broadly deployed model yet, and the first to be classified at the critical cybersecurity level under the company's own preparedness framework. The system card says Astra resists jailbreaks and prompt injection better than its predecessor, but flags a new concern too: the model is better at controlling its own chain of thought, which means it could potentially evade the very monitors built to watch it. Reaction across the newsletters was largely awestruck, with one builder calling it the most exciting model he's played with in years, even as it only narrowly leads rivals like Anthropic's Fable five point one on coding benchmarks. Via TLDR AI, The Rundown, and This Week in Startups.

Nvidia buys Hugging Face. Nvidia confirmed it has acquired Hugging Face, the platform hosting three million models for eighteen million developers, for twelve point nine three billion dollars. Chief executive Jensen Huang says the platform will remain open, and that Nvidia hardware won't be required to build or deploy on it, a reassurance clearly aimed at a developer community wary of lock-in. Via TLDR AI.

London gets its first robotaxi, and it isn't Waymo's. Uber began running Wayve-powered self-driving cars across London this week, beating Waymo to the city and marking Wayve's first commercial public service anywhere in the world. The initial fleet is fewer than twenty Ford Mustang Mach-Es, each still with a licensed safety driver behind the wheel, but riders booking a standard Uber may now simply be matched with one at no extra cost. Via TLDR Hardware.

The humble folder as an AI agent. Every's engineering lead Kieran Klaassen offers a good corrective to the swarm-of-agents hype. After months spent trying to build elaborate multi-agent orchestration systems, he found the real unlock was much simpler: a well organised project folder. Give a coding model a folder packed with conventions, institutional knowledge and specialist instructions, and you effectively have a specialist agent, no fancy architecture required. He now runs forty four of these folder-based agents through a lightweight dispatch system he built himself, though he's candid that it took months of trust-building before he'd let any of them run unsupervised. Via Every.

Microsoft undercuts the transcription market. Microsoft released MAI-Transcribe-2 this week, a speech recognition model it says beats offerings from OpenAI, Google and ElevenLabs on both accuracy and price, at just ten cents an hour of audio. It's part of a broader pattern of Microsoft building its own frontier-class models one modality at a time, then steadily swapping them into products that used to run on OpenAI's technology. Via TLDR AI.

Now, over to startups and venture.

STARTUPS AND VENTURE

Samsung leads Mistral's latest war chest. French AI champion Mistral is closing a three billion euro round, with Samsung leading and ASML, Eurazeo and the Scaleup Europe Fund also writing cheques, according to Sifted. It's the clearest sign yet that Europe's AI darling is attracting serious industrial money, not just venture capital, as it races to keep pace with its much larger American and Chinese rivals. Via Sifted.

Revolut edges toward a US banking licence. Revolut has received conditional approval from the US Office of the Comptroller of the Currency, clearing its first hurdle toward becoming a fully fledged bank in America. It's a milestone the London-based fintech has been chasing for years, one that would let it compete directly with domestic banks rather than operating around the edges of the US financial system. Via Sifted.

AI infrastructure money keeps flowing. The funding parade for AI infrastructure and agent startups continued this week. Data centre developer Crusoe reportedly raised three billion dollars at a thirty billion dollar valuation after landing a thirteen billion dollar contract with trading firm Jane Street, while enterprise agent startup Wonderful more than doubled its valuation to five billion dollars in under six months. Despite all the talk of an AI bubble, the capital behind the picks and shovels shows no sign of slowing down. Via This Week in Startups.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

POLICY AND GEOPOLITICS

The Strait of Hormuz stays a chokepoint. The US-Iran standoff over Gulf shipping escalated again this week. Iranian strikes hit more Saudi oil tankers, killing two more crew members, before Washington escorted forty vessels carrying eighteen million barrels of oil through the strait and struck Iranian military positions along the way. President Trump floated renaming the strait after the United States, while Vice President Vance ruled out talks until Iran stops targeting commercial shipping. Meanwhile, in the Arctic, Norway's governor of Svalbard seized a Russian state vessel as part of a four point two billion dollar debt collection stemming from Russia's seizure of Ukrainian energy assets back in 2014, a reminder that great power friction keeps turning up in some very unlikely corners of the map. Via International Intrigue.

A UK government adviser jumps to Anthropic. Matt Clifford, who served as the UK prime minister's artificial intelligence adviser, is leaving government to join Anthropic, according to Sifted. The move has drawn sharp criticism from parts of the UK tech world, who see it as another sign of Britain's difficulty holding onto talent when Silicon Valley comes calling, even talent it recruited into public service in the first place. Via Sifted.

India's balancing act on Ukraine. India's foreign minister used his first solo visit to Kyiv this week to pledge Delhi's help ending the war in any manner, according to International Intrigue. The trip lets India keep up its careful line of being a friend to everyone, even as it faces continued scrutiny over how its purchases of Russian oil, some fifty billion dollars worth last year alone, help finance the very war it says it wants to end. Via International Intrigue.

Now, over to community, events and marketing.

COMMUNITY, EVENTS AND MARKETING

A checklist for knowing which fights are worth having. Facilitator Priya Parker, ahead of her new book The Art of Fighting, shared a genuinely useful checklist this week for anyone who runs a team, a community, or even just a group chat: how do you know whether a simmering conflict is worth confronting head on, or better left alone? Her questions include whether you can keep engaging meaningfully without addressing it, whether the disagreement touches a core value, and whether you'd regret staying silent. The idea worth stealing here isn't the specific list, it's the pause itself: building in a deliberate moment to ask whether a conflict is generative before you dive in and try to fix it. Via Priya Parker.

Now, over to one interesting thing.

ONE INTERESTING THING

Fifty years ago, the Italian singer Adriano Celentano released a song called Prisencolinensinainciusol, built entirely from gibberish lyrics engineered to sound exactly like American English, without meaning a single word. He was testing a theory: that Italian audiences would embrace anything that merely sounded American. The track still gets pulled apart and shared today because it nails the rhythm and cadence of English so convincingly that people swear they can almost make out the words. It's a strange, catchy reminder that a lot of what we respond to in language is texture and confidence, not necessarily content. Via Why Is This Interesting.
