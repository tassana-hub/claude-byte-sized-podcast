Starting with Japan and Asia-Pacific. It was a quiet morning for regional headlines, but one number from CSIS is worth sitting with: South Korea's trade surplus with China has collapsed over the past decade, from around ninety nine point seven billion dollars in twenty eighteen down to just nineteen point six billion dollars last year, as Chinese firms increasingly out-compete Korean exporters across electronics, electric vehicles, batteries and petrochemicals. A CSIS analysis this week frames that slide as a genuine fork in the road for Seoul: keep trying to out-export a rival that is closing the technology gap, or pivot toward becoming what the piece calls an ecosystem power, building the platforms, standards and artificial intelligence infrastructure that other economies plug into, rather than just the goods they sell them. It is a choice with obvious echoes for Japan's own export-led model, which has spent decades leaning on precisely the kind of manufacturing scale that China is now matching or beating on cost, and it is worth watching as a bellwether for how the wider region, Tokyo included, responds to China's continuing manufacturing advance. The underlying question for both economies is essentially the same one facing exporters right across the Asia-Pacific: whether the old formula of out-producing everyone else at a lower price still works once a rival with China's scale decides to compete on the same terms, or whether the more durable advantage now lies further up the value chain, in the standards, software and data that sit around the hardware rather than the hardware itself. Via CSIS.

Now, over to A.I. and tech, where the week's story was less about one blockbuster launch and more about the ground shifting under the existing model war, both for the machines and for the people using them.

Start with the people. The second annual Tech Worker Sentiment Survey, run by longtime research leader Noam Segal alongside Lenny's Newsletter, found the industry splitting almost exactly in half: one half of workers thriving alongside artificial intelligence, the other shaken by it, with four distinct emotional groups emerging from the data, ranging from the energised to the outright resentful. Burnout has jumped sharply over the past year, and, tellingly, the survey's authors found the number one fear among tech workers right now is not losing a job to artificial intelligence outright, it is something closer to the erosion of meaning, status and support at work, with line managers emerging as the single biggest lever for employee wellbeing. Via Lenny's Newsletter.

On the model side, Grok four point five arrived this week as the first product of a formal collaboration between SpaceXAI and the coding tool Cursor, trained in part on real coding-tool interaction data. Early internal testing at the newsletter Every put it roughly level with Anthropic's Opus four point eight on tricky multi-step assignments, while running faster and considerably cheaper, at around two dollars per million input tokens against Opus's five, and with none of Claude's more familiar writing tics, if its own habit of short, blunt sentences instead. It is not necessarily a reason to switch your daily coding assistant, but for long jobs where speed and price matter more than the very top of the quality curve, it has already earned a slot in some workflows.

Meanwhile, Every's own team has quietly settled on OpenAI's GPT five point six, nicknamed Sol, as its default daily driver, prized less for raw brilliance than for being fast, easy to steer and reliable across coding, writing, research and spreadsheets, with the pricier Fable model reserved for the biggest, least-defined assignments. The clearest sign of how seriously that shift is being taken: the team has open-sourced a tool called Tend, a free prompt and workflow for letting a model run an entire loop of knowledge work largely on its own, gathering information, proposing decisions and carrying out the ones a human approves, while the person "tends" the loop rather than doing each step by hand.

And underneath all of it, a broader mood shift: as the big labs pull back the generous subsidies that made frontier models cheap to hammer with tokens, the conversation inside engineering and product teams is moving from what Every calls tokenmaxxing, bragging about how many tokens a workflow burns, to efficiencymaxxing, competing instead on how much useful output you can squeeze from a fixed budget, with "revenue per million tokens" emerging as an early candidate for the metric that actually matters. Via Every.

Now, over to startups and venture, where the sharpest piece of the week was less about a single deal than about the industry's own self-doubt. A long essay from The VC Corner traced venture capital's evolution from a handful of wealthy friends taking handshake bets on nineteen-fifties San Francisco semiconductor engineers, through the built-out, tens-of-billions-under-management industry it is today, to argue that the model itself is now under real strain. Returns have become harder to predict, exit timelines keep stretching, and portfolio companies are staying private for far longer than the traditional ten-year fund cycle was ever designed to absorb, prompting what the piece calls a civil war inside the asset class over how much operational hand-holding, rather than just capital, a fund now needs to offer founders to stay competitive. The essay traces that pressure all the way back to the industry's origins as a cottage business of outsiders willing to back ideas that sounded like science fiction, and argues the funds most likely to come out ahead now are the ones rediscovering that original willingness to sit with genuine uncertainty, rather than the ones simply managing an ever-larger pool of capital more cautiously. It is a useful reminder, in a week without a headline mega-round, that the plumbing behind the funding announcements is quietly being rebuilt under pressure. Via The VC Corner.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics, where CSIS's regular numbers round-up doubled as a snapshot of where hard power and hard resources are both heading. The White House's budget request for the twenty twenty seven fiscal year would push total defence spending to around four point six percent of gross domestic product, a notable increase but still, by CSIS's own reckoning, short of what counts as a genuine wartime footing. That matters because Russia is now firing more than five thousand drones a month into Ukraine, leaning on cheap Shahed-type craft precisely because their low cost lets Moscow launch far bigger, more frequent salvos than missiles alone would allow, a pattern of coercion by volume rather than precision. Underneath both numbers sits a resource story: China still controls roughly sixty percent of the world's primary aluminium production, some forty three million tonnes a year, while the United States imports around eighty two percent of its own primary supply, which is why mining group Alcoa's move to deepen a bet on South African aluminium is being read in Washington as a small but deliberate step toward keeping a strategic metal in allied hands. Via CSIS.

Closer to home, in Washington, the Trump administration is pushing ahead with a rewrite of the federal government's core grantmaking regulation, the kind of rule that sounds mundane but effectively decides how America's decentralised, historically successful system of science funding actually works in practice. The pushback has been unusually blunt and unusually bipartisan: the American Association for the Advancement of Science has called it a brazen power grab by the Office of Management and Budget that would leave future cures and discoveries hostage to the "scientific sensibilities" of a single budget analyst, while commentators from The Dispatch on the right to Noah Smith and Dan Drezner on the centre-left warn the rule would hand political appointees sweeping discretion to cancel or redirect funding for ideological reasons, with little recourse for researchers caught in the middle. Via Marginal Revolution.

Now, over to community, events and marketing, where this week's idea worth stealing is a small structural one. The Do Lectures' weekly newsletter leads every single edition with a segment called This Week's Changemaker, handing the very top slot to one small business or maker doing something quietly hard, before any of the week's other links or recommendations. It is a simple habit any community newsletter or events programme could copy without much extra effort: pick one person or one project each week and give them first billing, rather than burying individual stories inside one long, undifferentiated list. Via The Do Lectures.

Finally, one interesting thing to take you into the week ahead. New research on both sides of the Atlantic suggests something odd is happening to how young people talk about their own mental health. The share of young Britons and Americans who say they have a mental illness has climbed sharply over the past fifteen years, but the share who say a mental health problem actually limits their day-to-day functioning has barely moved at all. When people are asked whether ordinary mood swings, occasional worry, frustration, a wobble in confidence, should count as mental illness, more than half of young Americans now say yes, up from roughly a fifth fifteen years ago, while older people's answers have not shifted in the same period. It looks less like a sudden epidemic of illness than a generational change in vocabulary, in what people now consider worth naming out loud. Via Marginal Revolution.
