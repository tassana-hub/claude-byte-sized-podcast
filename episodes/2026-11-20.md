Good morning, and welcome to Byte-sized for Friday the twentieth of November. Six sections today, starting as always in Japan and the Asia Pacific.

Section one, Japan and Asia Pacific.

First, Japan steps up as Southeast Asia's middle power. The CSIS Southeast Asia Program says the past fortnight showed how far Tokyo's role in the region has grown. Vietnam's leader, To Lam, made the first visit to Tokyo by a Vietnamese Communist Party general secretary in eleven years, and he and Prime Minister Takaichi signed ten agreements on trade, defence and energy, with a goal of one hundred billion dollars in two-way trade by twenty thirty. Japan also promised to help Vietnam bring in small modular nuclear reactors as early as twenty forty. On top of that, Japan gave the Philippines its largest ever security assistance grant, about twenty-seven million dollars for coastal radar and surveillance drones. Via CSIS.

Second, what came out of the ASEAN summit in Manila. President Trump arrived on the final day and joined the leaders of Japan, Australia and India for the first in-person Quad leaders' meeting of his second term. The Quad announced a business leaders' forum for twenty twenty-seven and a new maritime surveillance effort, though CSIS says details are thin. The big disappointment was the South China Sea code of conduct with China, which still is not finished after more than twenty years of talks; Manila put progress at somewhere between seventy-seven and ninety per cent, which tells you how far apart people still are on the basics. The quieter win was ASEAN's new Digital Economy Framework Agreement, the region's first legally binding deal of its kind, which aims to double the bloc's digital economy to two trillion dollars by twenty thirty. For Japanese firms selling into Southeast Asia, that digital agreement is the one to read. Via CSIS.

Third, Thailand makes a pitch for Japan's clean-energy network, from inside China. At the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation meetings in Shenzhen, Thailand's prime minister, Anutin, first charmed Chinese media in fluent Cantonese, then used his speech to back linking the ASEAN power grid to Japan's regional clean-energy initiative, the Asia Zero Emission Community. International Intrigue calls it a serious hedge: Southeast Asian governments want Chinese investment, but they also want options so they do not become dependent on Beijing. It also fits the CSIS picture of Tokyo using energy and climate finance, including a six hundred million dollar credit line for the Malaysian energy company Petronas, as a quiet form of influence. Via International Intrigue and CSIS.

And fourth, video games as Japanese soft power and supply chain story. With Grand Theft Auto Six finally out after a thirteen-year wait, International Intrigue looked at the geopolitics of gaming. Japan still takes about nine per cent of global consumer spending on games, behind only America and China, and Sony and Nintendo remain giants, though China's Tencent is now the largest company by market share. American tariffs pushed Sony and Nintendo into one of the fastest manufacturing moves in consumer tech, cutting China's share of consoles bound for America from eighty-six per cent to five per cent and making Vietnam number one. Japan scrapped its troubled Cool Japan investment fund this year, but it still aims to quadruple its content exports by twenty thirty-three. Via International Intrigue.

Now, over to A I and tech.

Section two, A I and tech.

First, Google's fast new model. Google has released Gemini four Neon, a quicker sibling of its top model, Carbon, and its fourth Gemini four release in under two months. Despite its mid-tier label, Neon scores sixty-one on the independent Artificial Analysis index, ahead of Carbon and behind only Anthropic's Fable five point two and OpenAI's Astra. It costs three dollars per million words in and fifteen out, roughly a quarter cheaper than its closest rivals. The Rundown notes that a Google researcher hinted, the night before launch, that self-improving A I may be behind the sudden pace. A separate test flagged by T L D R found Carbon taking almost an hour on bug fixes that rivals finished in minutes, so speed of shipping is not the same as speed of working. Via The Rundown A I and T L D R A I.

Second, Microsoft's counter-argument: as many models as firms. At a roundtable after his Ignite keynote, Satya Nadella told Every that a world run by one A I company will not happen, because customers will demand more than one supplier. Microsoft now lets large customers license the weights of its own models, run them inside their own cloud accounts, train them on their own data and decide when to upgrade. Microsoft says AT and T's custom model beat a leading OpenAI model on telecoms network tasks at one-fiftieth of the cost. Nadella also says former team leads are becoming individual contributors who manage agents, with their own budgets for A I usage. For enterprise buyers in Japan, owning a tuned model rather than renting a general one is becoming a real option. Via Every.

Third, A I is starting to design the chips it runs on. OpenAI says design changes suggested by its models shrank key parts of its own Jalapeño A I chip by an estimated ten to thirteen per cent, and working with A M D, the same approach improved some of A M D's flagship graphics chip designs by up to fourteen per cent. In a separate update, OpenAI says its agents now complete nearly half of research tasks that would take a person two to four hours. And a T L D R item reports that Anthropic spent nearly ten billion dollars on computing in the third quarter, with its compute spending growing about four times a year. Via T L D R A I.

Now, over to startups and venture.

Section three, startups and venture.

First, the largest stock market listing in history. Anthropic priced its shares at eighty-three dollars overnight, above its target range, raising eighty-one point three billion dollars and valuing the five-year-old company at about one and a half trillion dollars, ahead of its Nasdaq debut on Thursday. That beats SpaceX's raise to become the biggest initial public offering ever, and demand was about seven times the shares on offer. The economist Tyler Cowen, who discloses that he sits on an Anthropic advisory board, offers a contrarian read: if a company truly expected superintelligence very soon, it would borrow rather than share the upside with public investors, so the listing hints at sensible, not radical, expectations. Sifted has been saying European founders hope a strong debut frees up capital for their own A I companies. Via International Intrigue and Marginal Revolution.

Second, The Generalist's Future fifty is back. The annual list of the most promising startups valued at two hundred million dollars or less, nominated by partner-level investors, shows three trends. More than two thirds of this year's picks were founded in twenty twenty-four or later; nearly half have twenty or fewer staff, up from about a quarter two years ago; and America's share keeps growing. Of the hundred earlier picks, six were acquired and two are now worth more than ten billion dollars. One name worth noting for this audience: Tokyo's Sakana A I, an earlier pick, is now valued at about two point seven billion dollars. Via The Generalist.

Third, the bar for raising money keeps rising. Carta's latest American data shows that most early-stage SAFE notes end up converting at their valuation cap, so founders should treat the cap as the real price. SaaStr suggests venture investors now want software firms to grow from one million to one hundred million dollars in annual revenue within five years, and ideally two or three. More venture-backed startups have shut down this year than in any year on record. In Europe, Sifted reports the expense-management company Payhawk raised two hundred and eighty million dollars despite a wider fintech slump. Via T L D R Founders and Sifted.

Section four, policy and geopolitics.

First, a landmark genocide ruling. The International Court of Justice has found that Myanmar committed genocide against its Rohingya minority, the first time the court has ever found a state responsible for genocide. The case was brought by The Gambia and took seven years. There is no appeal, and Myanmar could now face enforcement at the United Nations Security Council, where a veto from China or Russia would be more costly after this verdict. It lands days after ASEAN chose to keep Myanmar's military-backed leaders barred from its summits. For companies with supply chains in Myanmar, the reputational risk has just gone up. Via International Intrigue and CSIS.

Second, the three-way summit begins. Xi Jinping personally greeted President Trump on the tarmac in Shenzhen before their meeting with Vladimir Putin, and Trump declared, "we are making history". International Intrigue still expects little substance: Ukraine is not Trump's to give, Taiwan is not Xi's to take, and Putin is in no position to dictate terms. The CSIS Korea Chair ran a live discussion straight after the meeting, which is worth catching for the North Korea angle we flagged yesterday. Via International Intrigue and CSIS.

And third, a warning for anyone planning international events in America. Overseas visits to the United States have fallen for seven straight months, with October down about seven per cent on a year earlier. Surveys suggest costs and exchange rates are keeping Asian travellers away, while visitors from Canada, Germany and Australia cite the political climate, and the industry body does not expect a full recovery until twenty thirty. Skift suggests organisers stop assuming international attendees will bounce back, and consider regional events or moving meetings outside America every few years. Via Skift.

Now, over to community, events and marketing.

Section five, community, events and marketing.

One idea worth stealing, from a futurist who lives on conference stages. Henry Coutinho-Mason, who has advised many large companies, told Skift Meetings that most events waste their stages. His fix is fewer keynote speeches, and sponsors who are willing to drop their prepared talking points and actually engage. Skift adds a practical note from last week's nationwide flight ground stop in America: most event contracts cover a cancelled event, not one where half the attendees arrive a day late, so check your force majeure clauses for attendance drops. For your next event, try swapping one keynote for an unscripted sponsor conversation and see what the room does with it. Via Skift.

And a second idea, from McDonald's: the chef's table at the counter. McDonald's hired a Top Chef winner to host a one-day, reservation-only tasting called Inside the Golden Arches at a New York restaurant counter, where diners heard how a Quarter Pounder, an Egg McMuffin and the fries are actually made. Scan Club says it works because it pokes fun at fine dining while giving people real insider knowledge, which is exactly the kind of brand lore people want to share. The takeaway for community builders: a small, invitation-only, behind-the-scenes session about how your everyday thing is made can feel more special than a big launch. Via Scan Club.

Section six, one interesting thing.

Colin Nagy at Why Is This Interesting shared a list of Tokyo cocktail bars that one hotel bar hands to curious guests, and one entry stands out. Rondo, in Kagurazaka, has just six seats and is devoted to traditional Japanese bartending; it is named after the musical form in which a theme keeps returning with variations, which suits a place built around repeat visits. It is a neat reminder that in Japan, the smallest venues often have the clearest idea of what they are for. Via Why Is This Interesting.
