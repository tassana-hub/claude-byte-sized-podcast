Good morning, and welcome to Byte-sized for Tuesday the sixth of October. Five sections today, plus a closing thought.

Section one, Japan and Asia Pacific.

First, chips. Tesla, SpaceX and xAI may be getting a fabrication plant of their own, and TSMC may be the one to run it. Culpium reports that TSMC is exploring a fab that would serve those three companies exclusively, and Elon Musk confirmed the talks on X, saying it is just discussions, but something may come of it. The structures floated are either a joint venture in the mould of TSMC's Kumamoto plant in Japan, where partners bring capital and demand while TSMC brings process technology, or a stranger arrangement in which SpaceX owns the majority and TSMC simply operates the plant and licenses its process. If it happens, it could also sideline Intel's next generation node. Via TLDR.

Second, a reminder of how porous export controls are across South East Asia. American prosecutors have charged a California businessman with sending more than three hundred million dollars of servers loaded with restricted Nvidia chips to China. The route ran through Malaysia and Singapore, exploiting the fact that shipping this hardware there needs no licence, while shipping it to China does. Expect Asian logistics and data centre firms to face tougher paperwork and more questions from American partners. Via TLDR.

Third, China. Beijing has quietly shut or merged a record six hundred and seventy lenders across several provinces, mostly credit co-operatives and small regional banks. The official line is tidier supervision. International Intrigue's guess is that many were sitting on bad loans from the property crash, and that folding them into bigger state banks buries the losses rather than erasing them. Via International Intrigue.

And fourth, security in the region. CSIS has a new episode of its podcast on American and Australian defence co-operation, and its Korea chair has been running a live debate on the American and South Korean wartime command question. Together they are a reminder that alliance arrangements across the Pacific are being renegotiated at the same time, which matters in Tokyo as much as in Seoul or Canberra. Via CSIS.

Now, over to artificial intelligence and technology.

Section two, AI and tech.

The Atlantic has published an essay by David Robinson, who spent three and a half years at OpenAI overseeing safety reports for twelve frontier launches. He has now left, calling the company's culture broken, and arguing that labs should be run like nuclear plants and airports, with layers of redundancy. His line was that colleagues were so busy sprinting that they seldom had time to consider big changes. It follows the departure of other safety researchers, and it will colour how enterprise buyers ask about vendor governance. Via The Rundown.

Meta has opened its Muse agent to tinkerers. A new open source kit lets hobbyists build physical front ends for Muse on cheap boards, adding screens, microphones, speakers and sensors, and community skills can control lights, televisions and printers. It is a smart move to seed an ecosystem around an agent before rivals do. Via TLDR.

A sobering benchmark from Mercor: twelve certified accountants were pitted against frontier models on four month-end close tasks. The best model scored full marks, while the humans averaged about thirty seven percent. Mercor itself cautions that the tasks favoured file searching and following instructions, and left out client judgement, so this is not proof that accountants are replaceable. It does show how much structured back office work is now automatable. Via TLDR.

And in Washington, President Trump announced a new Super Intelligence Force, led by the director of national intelligence, who is also the new AI czar, to run federal AI policy. Worth watching for who gets a seat at that table. Via The Rundown.

Now, over to startups and venture.

Section three, startups and venture.

Stripe has agreed to buy Parafin, the embedded lending platform behind credit products at DoorDash, Gusto, Jobber and Mindbody, which has financed more than sixty thousand businesses. It folds Parafin's credit and pay over time products into Stripe Capital, and strengthens Stripe's pitch that its eighteen thousand platform customers can become full financial hubs for small firms. For founders, embedded finance is becoming a feature that every platform is expected to offer. Via TLDR.

On the other side of the cycle, London's fintech scene is entering a consolidation era. Funding is at its lowest since at least twenty sixteen, capital is concentrating in business to business companies and a few scaled winners like Revolut, Monzo and Wise, and many founders are choosing mergers, pivots or acquisitions. Via TLDR.

And a thoughtful piece from The Diff on life with non-zero interest rates. Byrne Hobart argues that high rates are terrible for the present value of long-term research, and do little to the return on a cost-cutting project that pays for itself in months. His conclusion is that this is a time to choose deployment over research: more capital should go into working out what to do with the models we already have. Interest rates, he says, are the price of time. Via The Diff.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

Section four, policy and geopolitics.

A CSIS commentary by Francesca Ghiretti looks at the European Union and China ahead of trade talks in Beijing on the eighth and ninth of October. Beijing recently published a forty page paper rebutting the idea of overcapacity, and her reading is that this signals the Chinese growth model is off the table. Chinese exports grew nearly twenty five percent year on year in August, and household consumption is only about forty percent of output. So the two sides are debating remedies while disagreeing on the diagnosis. Expect managed friction with periodic spikes, rather than either a deal or a trade war. Via CSIS.

And in Brazil, Flavio Bolsonaro won the first round with about forty seven percent against President Lula's forty five, and the two meet in a runoff on the twenty fifth of October. Most eliminated candidates leaned right, so International Intrigue says he holds the advantage, and bond yields fell on hopes of a more business friendly government. Markets have also wobbled at the idea he would pardon his father, who is serving a twenty seven year sentence. Via International Intrigue.

Now, over to community, events and marketing.

Section five, community, events and marketing. One idea worth stealing.

The community professionals' network CMX is running a tour of small local meet-ups, and the format is worth borrowing. Each stop pairs one focused panel with a relaxed meal and open networking afterwards, for example a New York evening on launching and scaling ambassador programmes and showing impact in a way leadership cares about. Their upcoming masterclass has the same lesson: turn community data into stories that executives will actually listen to. If you run a community, steal both ideas. Keep events to one clear question, and translate your numbers into a story for the people who hold the budget. Via CMX.

And finally, one interesting thing.

Works in Progress has a piece titled Mosquitoes are a choice. The author argues we already have the tools to wipe out mosquito-borne disease in the United States, but they have been stuck in regulatory limbo. Genetically engineered mosquitoes from Oxitec bounced between three agencies for fifteen years, while a rival approach using a harmless bacterium called Wolbachia won full registration in twenty twenty four. The same idea cut mosquito numbers by eighty five percent and dengue by roughly seventy percent in a Singapore trial. The lesson is that the bottleneck is often not science but who is allowed to say yes. Via Works in Progress.
