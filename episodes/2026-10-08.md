Good morning, and welcome to Byte-sized for Thursday the eighth of October. Five sections today, plus a closing thought. This one covers everything since yesterday morning's edition.

Section one, Japan and Asia Pacific.

First, a Japanese financing story that is turning awkward. International Intrigue reports that India's flagship fifteen billion dollar bullet train between Mumbai and Ahmedabad is facing another round of delays and cost overruns. The tension is over the terms of Japan's financing and technology, including India's push to use locally built trains instead. It is a useful reminder that friend-shoring and Indo-Pacific alignment sound wonderful right up until the invoice arrives. Via International Intrigue.

Second, India again, but closer to home politically. Youth-led protests are back on the streets over the removal of roughly one hundred and thirty million names from India's voter rolls. Delhi says it is simply clearing out the dead, the duplicates and the discrepancies. Critics say the purge falls hardest on Muslim voters and the poor, who face new paperwork. Worth watching, given how much Japanese business is now betting on India. Via International Intrigue.

Third, China and AI talent. The Chinese start-up DeepSeek is reportedly close to closing a twelve billion dollar raise, ahead of its original target, with Tencent and the battery maker CATL among the backers, and a possible Shanghai listing to follow. Meanwhile, despite a dedicated visa and generous grants, China is struggling to recruit foreign AI researchers, and the number of Chinese scientists working in the United States has actually risen. Capital is flowing in Beijing's direction, but talent is not. Via T L D R.

Fourth, Fukuoka. Fukuoka Now notes that the city's mayoral election is on the fifteenth of November, and asks what the city should prioritise over the next four years, from housing and transport to jobs and the balance between growth and everyday life. If you work with the Fukuoka ecosystem, those priorities will shape the next term. Via Fukuoka Now.

Now, over to artificial intelligence and technology.

Section two, AI and tech.

First, mathematics. OpenAI has published seven hundred and twenty two papers from an unreleased internal model, grouped into three hundred and seventy two families of results that it says solve or advance open problems. The boldest claim is a proof of a weaker cousin of the Riemann hypothesis, and a hundred and sixty two of the papers come with machine checkable proofs in the Lean language. The striking part is the cost: nearly all of the new results took a single prompt and about three hours of compute. Some mathematicians are unhappy about the pace of the releases. The takeaway is that progress in maths is starting to scale with chips rather than people. Via The Rundown AI and T L D R.

Second, Europe's open model. Mistral has launched Large 4, a one trillion parameter open model nicknamed Le Chonk, which it says leads every non-Chinese open system by a wide margin. Chinese models still top its coding chart, but two serious Western open releases in one week makes this feel like a proper race. Security experts and government agencies get a less filtered preview first, with the weights going public on the twenty seventh of October. Sifted also notes it as Mistral's bid to re-establish itself in the AI race. Via The Rundown AI, Sifted and T L D R.

Third, personal agents. Hark, the start-up from Figure AI's founder, has launched Hark Pro, an assistant that remembers your preferences and can research, shop and book on your behalf, running tasks on its own cloud computer. Separately, Meta and Sierra are working with partners on an open Personal Agent Protocol, so that companies can see and set limits on what consumers' agents do on their sites. Both point the same way: agents acting for people, and the rules of engagement being written now. Via The Rundown AI and T L D R.

Fourth, one for everyday users. Anthropic has rolled out a beta Claude sidebar inside Google Docs, Sheets and Slides for paid plans, which can edit open files directly or ask approval before each change. Via The Rundown AI.

Now, over to start-ups and venture.

Section three, start-ups and venture.

First, Europe's defence tech boom gets a reality check. Sifted reports from the Resilience Conference in London, where defence was no longer an awkward subject for investors. The debate has moved on to harder questions: where growth capital will come from, whether start-ups can hire engineers against American giants, how fast government procurement can adapt, and what sovereign technology actually means. Via Sifted.

Second, how to raise a Series A. At Sifted Summit, four investors explained what really moves a decision, including whether they tolerate AI generated pitch decks. Good timing, a good story and a clear sense of what investors are looking for was the shared thread. Via Sifted.

Third, deals and rankings. SAP has agreed to buy the Belgian AI start-up TechWolf. The fintech Spiko raised a ninety million dollar Series B from Index Ventures and Speedinvest. And Sifted's new ranking of Europe's fastest growing start-ups by revenue was topped by the wellness marketplace Healf, followed by Neko Health and the Ghent based security firm Aikido, with Oslo and Prague emerging as hubs. Via Sifted.

Fourth, travel. Skift has a scoop that Airbnb is rebuilding its Luxe programme for high end homes, the latest revival of an initiative dropped during the pandemic. Watch launch timing and how homes rank against hotels. Via Skift.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

Section four, policy and geopolitics.

First, trade. The C S I S Trade Guys podcast covers the Trump and Xi summit, the American trade representative's gathering of G twenty trade ministers in Milwaukee, and why the European Union is pressing China to limit exports of hybrid vehicles. In the same vein, International Intrigue quotes the former World Trade Organisation chief Pascal Lamy warning that China is killing the open global trading system through capital controls and state support for exports. Via C S I S and International Intrigue.

Second, Germany. The former head of the foreign intelligence service, August Hanning, has been arrested on suspicion of treason. Police allege he bought more than two thousand classified reports from a former aide and sold them on, with Azerbaijan reportedly one of the main buyers. It surfaced by accident, during a kidnapping investigation involving a steakhouse heiress. The point for anyone working with governments is the systemic one: insider leaks to third countries are a serious allied trust problem. Via International Intrigue.

Now, over to community, events and marketing.

Section five, one idea worth stealing.

Fukuoka Now's Kyushu Roundtable is a simple format worth copying. A small English language evening, capped at fifty, free, with advance registration. It opens with a short news flash, then two guest commentators, then the floor, and drinks afterwards for anyone who wants to carry on. The next one is on the thirtieth of October, on the city's next chapter. Pick a live local issue, invite two people with different angles, keep the room small, and let the conversation do the work. Via Fukuoka Now.

And finally, one interesting thing.

International Intrigue's mystery of the day: the United States once named a series of secret Cold War satellites after the actress Farrah Fawcett. Farrah the Third, launched in nineteen eighty eight to intercept Soviet signals, went silent decades ago, then suddenly exploded into a million pieces on the thirteenth of September. Theories range from a space rock to old age to an anti-satellite weapon. Nobody is saying. Via International Intrigue.
