And for a smaller but arguably more useful data point on what it actually takes to build something that works, Indie Hackers profiled Filip Panoski, who shipped five side projects over seven years and made a grand total of one hundred dollars from them, before quitting his seven thousand dollar a month job and going all in on a sixth attempt. Twelve months later, his Reddit marketing tool for software founders, called Bazzly, is doing seven and a half thousand dollars a month, built largely by using Bazzly to market Bazzly. The lessons he draws are refreshingly unglamorous: pick a business model with recurring revenue, validate that people will actually pay before writing a line of code, and don't cling to full ownership if giving away a slice of the business to the right partner is what gets you unstuck.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics, where the sharpest piece of writing this week wasn't really about any single country, but about why democracies everywhere seem to be struggling at once. Fareed Zakaria's widely shared opinion piece argues that the discontent showing up almost everywhere, from France to South Korea to the United States, has less to do with any one government's specific failures and more to do with living through a technological revolution on the scale of the one that ran from around eighteen eighty to nineteen twenty, when electricity, the telephone, and the motor car tore up entire ways of life within a single generation. Zakaria's point is that the anxiety these shifts produce always finds a political outlet somewhere, and that history's last comparable wave ended in extremism on both the left and the right. He cites Japan's economic comeback, alongside France's and South Korea's, as proof that strong growth alone doesn't buy any government peace with its own voters.

Further out, the economic bill for the Iran war kept arriving. Oil touched a hundred dollars a barrel this week, and airlines across the region are still working out what that means for the rest of the year. Emirates is the rare bright spot, flying at ninety two percent of planned capacity from the first of August and telling reporters at the Farnborough air show that tight coordination between Dubai's airport, military, and government got it through the worst of the crisis with cash and profitability both running ahead of where it expected to be. India's IndiGo has had no such luck: it swung to a twenty four and a half million dollar loss after jet fuel spiked one hundred and twenty percent year on year, and had only just clawed its way back to something like normal capacity when fighting flared up again days before its earnings call. The read across both stories is the same: some carriers have found a way to operate through this kind of shock, and some haven't, and fuel costs are doing more to separate the two than anything happening in the cabin.

Now, over to community, events and marketing, where two very different builders landed on the same underlying idea this week: rehearse the hard thing before you actually need it. One idea worth stealing: Priya Parker's team ran a live session built around what they call a Hot Takes Party, getting a group to argue passionately over harmless, low stakes opinions, whether a burrito beats a taco, whether cats make better pets than dogs. Nobody's identity is on the line, but Parker's point is that the muscle a group builds arguing about tacos is the same one it needs later, at the family dinner where politics comes up, or the Monday meeting after a launch goes wrong.

On the marketing side, Chenell at Growth In Reverse launched something in a similar spirit for anyone building a landing page or an email from scratch: a searchable library of real marketing examples, called the Swipe Files, each one paired with a template you can adapt rather than copy outright. It grew out of the same instinct that's helped her own newsletter add two thousand eight hundred subscribers in twenty three days: don't reinvent a format that's already been proven, study it and make it your own. The takeaway for anyone doing their own marketing is the same one underneath the hot takes idea: borrow what already works rather than starting from a blank screen.

And now, to close, one interesting thing. Why Is This Interesting's weekend roundup flagged a quirk of memory called the reminiscence bump: adults over forty tend to remember events from their adolescence and early twenties far more vividly than events from any other stretch of their life, including more recent ones. The leading explanation is that those years are when most people's sense of who they are gets locked in for the first time, first loves, first independence, first big decisions, so the brain seems to tag memories from that window as unusually important and hold onto them harder than almost anything that comes after. It's a tidy explanation for why so many people's music taste, favourite films, and even their politics seem to freeze somewhere around twenty two, and never quite move on.
