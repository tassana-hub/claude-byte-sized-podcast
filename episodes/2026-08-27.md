JAPAN AND ASIA PACIFIC

We start in Taiwan, where prosecutors have indicted a senior Nvidia manager and two Super Micro Computer executives for smuggling restricted AI servers into China. Investigators say the black market operation rerouted around seventy machines through Japan and Indonesia to dodge export controls. It's a reminder that even inside a legendary, high paying firm like Nvidia, insider risk is real, and that Taiwan is now more willing than ever to help police America's chip rules. Via International Intrigue.

Next, Vietnam has officially overtaken China, Mexico and Taiwan to record the world's largest goods trade surplus with the United States. The takeaway is that Trump's tariffs haven't so much closed America's trade deficit as relocated it, with firms like Apple and Nike shifting production to de-risk from China. Regulators are now probing how much of that shift is genuine manufacturing versus Chinese goods simply being relabelled on the way through. Via International Intrigue.

And a lighter note from Tokyo. Japan's prime minister, Sanae Takaichi, wrote a surprisingly heartfelt social media post about missing a cockroach that her staff had removed from her office, citing a classic postwar manga about the cycle of life and death as her reasoning for not wanting it squashed. Even the pets, it turns out, come with philosophical backstories in Japanese politics. Via International Intrigue.

Now, over to AI and tech.

Nvidia posted a blowout quarter: ninety six billion dollars in revenue, more than double what it made a year ago, with data centre sales alone hitting eighty nine billion. The company is guiding for a hundred and eight billion next quarter, and is reportedly circling a thirteen billion dollar bid for Hugging Face, the platform where developers share and test open source models. The logic is simple: own the hub, and you help lock in your chips as the industry default. Via TLDR and International Intrigue.

Stripe has agreed to acquire OpenRouter for more than seven billion dollars, turning what was a simple AI model routing service into core payments infrastructure. The deal hands Stripe a gateway connecting eight million users to over four hundred AI models through a single programming interface, with centralised billing baked in. The catch is that a lot of that traffic flows to Chinese developed models, which could bring new regulatory headaches. Via TLDR.

And Salesforce and Anthropic have launched what they're calling Claudeforce, pulling live customer relationship data and Salesforce workflows directly into Claude. The first release ships with thirty seven sales specific skills, covering everything from pipeline analysis to account research, and Salesforce is pitching it as a reason you'll never need to open the separate app again. Via TLDR.

And away from the big labs, community pushback against new AI data centres is gaining real momentum. Local lawmakers across several US states are moving toward outright moratoriums, citing electricity costs, water use and strain on local infrastructure, and that backlash is becoming a genuine constraint on how fast AI computing capacity can grow, regardless of how much money the big players are willing to spend. Via TLDR.

Now, over to startups and venture.

Anthropic is gunning for the biggest initial public offering on record, targeting a hundred billion dollar raise at a two trillion dollar valuation, more than double what Elon Musk's SpaceX is worth. The company is already generating sixty five billion dollars a year in revenue, seven times what it made last year, and Wall Street's biggest banks are reportedly being asked to commit over a billion dollars each toward a credit facility ahead of the listing. Given that AI exposed companies now make up more than forty percent of the S and P five hundred, this listing is shaping up as a serious stress test for the wider market. Via TLDR and International Intrigue.

Meanwhile, China's fast fashion giant Shein has filed for its third attempt at going public, this time in Hong Kong, hoping to raise one point eight billion dollars at a twenty five billion dollar valuation. That's a steep comedown from its hundred billion dollar peak in twenty twenty two, after a US listing collapsed under political pressure and a London listing fell apart when Beijing itself refused to bless it. Via International Intrigue.

And a genuine growth story: the AI meeting notes app Granola has gone from nothing to a one point five billion dollar valuation in three years. A detailed teardown of its playbook this week highlighted a deliberate year in stealth that saw the team cut half their planned features before launch, a refusal to build a generic meeting bot despite investor pressure, and a pivot from simple notepad to what the founders call a context layer for AI, now plugging into other tools rather than trying to do everything itself. Via The VC Corner.

Buy now, pay later firm Klarna offers a cautionary tale for founders eyeing a US listing. Its shares plunged nearly twenty five percent this week after warning that weak consumer spending in Germany would hurt full year revenue. Part of the problem is structural: Klarna's core markets are European, but it listed in the US, where it's relatively small and under followed, missing out on the index inclusion and investor familiarity a European listing might have brought. Sometimes where you list matters as much as how you perform. Via TLDR.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

The biggest mystery of the week: America's CIA director, John Ratcliffe, made a surprise, very public trip to Moscow, flying in on a US military transport rather than a discreet private jet. The leading theory is deterrence, echoing a similar 2021 visit that correctly warned Putin the West knew he was planning to invade Ukraine, this time amid intelligence suggesting Russia may test NATO's resolve directly. Quieter theories point to stalled Ukraine peace talks, pressure over Iran, or a handful of Americans still held in Russian jails. Nobody in Washington or Moscow is confirming which it was. Via International Intrigue.

In the Himalayas, flash floods along the Nepal-Tibet border have killed at least a hundred and seventy seven people, with fifteen hundred more missing, after an ice and rock avalanche dammed a river that then gave way. Warmer temperatures have shrunk Nepal's glacial ice by a third over three decades, and the timing was especially cruel, since the area was packed with pilgrims from several faiths heading to the sacred Mount Kailash. Via International Intrigue.

And in the Gulf, oil prices have dropped to around eighty five dollars a barrel on hopes that talks between Oman and Iran might finally ease tensions around the Strait of Hormuz, with Qatar's prime minister also headed to Tehran to encourage a fresh round of talks with Washington. Shipping executives are more cautious, warning the standoff looks more like a prolonged stalemate than a quick resolution. Via International Intrigue.

Back in Washington, Meta has agreed to pay up to eighteen billion dollars to settle a landmark case brought by forty seven US states over teen social media harm. The deal forces the company to add real guardrails across Facebook and Instagram, including limits on how much time young people can spend scrolling. It's one of the largest consumer protection settlements in tech history, and a signal that state attorneys general, not just federal regulators, are now setting the pace on platform accountability. Via International Intrigue.

Now, over to community, events and marketing.

One idea worth stealing this week comes from the founder of systeme dot io, who built his acquisition strategy entirely around a sixty percent affiliate commission, paid for the lifetime of every customer referred. He treats it as his marketing budget rather than a cost, and it's helped take the bootstrapped company to three million accounts. The lesson: if your product naturally creates advocates, consider paying them like a channel, not a favour. Via Indie Hackers.

A second one: the founder behind EditionGuard, an ebook piracy protection tool, grew from thirty to over a hundred customers by writing detailed guides on how to use his biggest competitor's product, rather than only marketing his own. Publishers searching for help with that rival tool found his tutorials first, then discovered he offered the same protection for a fraction of the price. Teaching people how to use a competitor's product, it turns out, can be a surprisingly effective way to find your own customers. Via Indie Hackers.

Now, over to one interesting thing.

Economist Alex Tabarrok has a sharp essay this week on why the fight over data centres bothers him less because of water or land use, and more because of what it reveals about how permission to build anything is granted in America. He borrows a distinction from the economists North, Wallis and Weingast between a natural state, where access to valuable activity depends on political favour, and an open access order, where the same impersonal rules apply to everyone regardless of what they're building or who approves of it. His worry is that the maze of rezonings, negotiated community benefit payments and case by case approval that now surrounds every data centre is really the natural state creeping back in, dressed up in modern language. Via Marginal Revolution.
