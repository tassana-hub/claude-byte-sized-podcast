Good morning, and welcome to Byte-sized for Wednesday the seventh of October. Five sections today, plus a closing thought. This one covers everything since Monday's edition, so there is a little more to get through.

Section one, Japan and Asia Pacific.

First, Japan's bond market. International Intrigue notes that risk premiums on the debt of heavily indebted countries, including Japan, spiked last week. Japan's thirty year government bond yield hit a new high on worries about Prime Minister Takaichi's stimulus plans, and she has told investors to rest assured. Worth watching alongside Europe's own bond wobble, which we come to later. Via International Intrigue.

Second, India, and a neat idea for anyone thinking about inbound tourism in Asia. The Indian government and the ONDC network have launched a National Digital Tourism Stack, an open digital layer that puts more than twenty three thousand homestays, guides, attractions and transport providers where travellers, and increasingly AI agents, can actually find them. A pilot runs in January twenty twenty seven, with a wider rollout from July. The point is that big hotel chains already know how to be found; family run homestays rely on messaging apps and word of mouth. Japan's regional tourism operators face exactly the same problem. Via Skift.

Third, Taiwan. Taipei has condemned what it calls transnational repression after the FBI arrested a California estate agent at Los Angeles airport, accused of following the son of Taiwan's president in Seattle at Beijing's direction. It is another sign that old red lines about operating on foreign soil are blurring. Via International Intrigue.

And fourth, Australia. Counter terrorism police are investigating the background of the co-pilot behind last week's attempted hijacking of a FlyDubai flight, who reportedly studied in Melbourne. It lands just as Australia's conservative opposition promises its biggest ever cut to migration, so expect the politics to heat up. Via International Intrigue.

Now, over to artificial intelligence and technology.

Section two, AI and tech.

The big story is Reflection AI, a startup valued at twenty five billion dollars that had never released a public model, until now. Its first, called Beam, is an open weight model for coding and agents. It has five hundred and one billion parameters in total, with twenty three billion active at any time. Reflection says it matches a recent Chinese rival at a fraction of the computing cost, though a stronger Chinese model, Moonshot's Kimi, still leads on raw ability. The weights are due this month under a permissive Apache licence, and a test is reportedly under way in South Korea. It is being called the DeepSeek of the West, but the lesson is how far ahead Chinese open models still are. Via The Rundown and TLDR.

Second, money. OpenAI is reportedly in talks on a thirty billion dollar funding round, with funds from the United Arab Emirates considering a syndicate of up to ten billion, and BlackRock, Thrive and Andreessen Horowitz also possible. Separately, OpenAI is expanding advertising inside ChatGPT with a new visual format, and has opened a business marketplace with thirty two partners, which TLDR's analysts compare to a cloud style app store where buyers get one invoice. Via TLDR.

Third, watermarking. OpenAI will add an invisible text watermark, called textGrain, to ChatGPT and Codex output in the European Union within weeks, to satisfy the AI Act. It is off by default elsewhere, and the detector is limited to vetted researchers. Anthropic launched its own watermark in August, and drew backlash by applying it globally. Via The Rundown.

Fourth, who actually pays for AI. Andreessen Horowitz's latest consumer apps list finds only four point five percent of Americans subscribe to a major chatbot, and the top one percent of spenders average nine hundred and three dollars a month. Meta's new Muse agent reportedly passed five million downloads in under a month. Via The Rundown.

Sifted reports that Revolut's valuation has gone from forty five billion dollars last summer to seventy five billion in November and now, reportedly, one hundred and fifteen billion, each time through employee share sales. The question from Sifted's secondaries panel is who else gets that liquidity. A speaker from Isomer Capital said the boom concerns fewer than one percent of European companies. If you run a fintech without Revolut's glamour, liquidity for your team is still a real problem. Via Sifted.

Next, a deal falling through: Monzo's reported talks to be acquired by Brazil's Nubank have been scrapped. Via Sifted.

And a bright spot in hardware. German factory robotics startup RobCo has passed a one billion dollar valuation in a share sale. Sifted also launched its Sifted two fifty ranking of Europe's fastest growing startups, won by wellness marketplace Healf, with Neko Health and security firm Aikido next. Via Sifted.

Section four, policy and geopolitics.

First, EU and China. CSIS's Francesca Ghiretti argues that Beijing's long position paper on so called excess capacity signals that its export driven growth model is not up for negotiation. Chinese exports grew twenty four percent year on year in July and twenty five percent in August. EU Trade Commissioner Maroš Šefčovič visits Beijing on the eighth and ninth of October, and her base case is managed tension, with periodic spikes, rather than a settlement. For anyone doing business with Europe or China, expect friction to become normal. Via CSIS.

Second, Europe's own wobble. International Intrigue says the euro fell about one percent on Monday to an eighteen month low, as Spain's prime minister called a snap election for the twenty ninth of November and French budget talks deadlocked, with French debt at one hundred and nineteen percent of output. Spreads between French and German bonds are at their widest since the eurozone crisis, leaving the European Central Bank in a bind. Germany and France have also written to Brussels urging stronger powers to block distorted imports. Via International Intrigue.

Now, over to community, events and marketing.

Section five, community, events and marketing. Two ideas worth stealing.

First, from Indie Hackers: price the outcome, not your time. Vikash Kumar Prajapati took a hundred dollar freelance job making eighteen hundred mockups, automated it in thirty minutes, charged three hundred dollars, then turned the script into a product now earning about one hundred and fifty thousand dollars a year. He reached customers through YouTube, where people were already searching for the fix. The same issue has a growth tip: pursue partnerships where both sides gain customers, as one logistics platform did by swapping referrals with warehouse and freight firms. Via Indie Hackers.

Second, from Skift. Informa is buying Clarion for three billion dollars, the second largest deal ever among event organisers, giving one company more leverage over venues and suppliers. The practical takeaway comes from their note on Brand USA, with international arrivals down about twelve percent: plan twenty twenty seven as if overseas attendance stays soft, and build in virtual or regional options. Via Skift.

And finally, one interesting thing.

Works in Progress argues that mosquito borne disease is now a choice. Genetically engineered mosquitoes cut wild populations by up to ninety five percent in trials, yet the American approval of the first one has sat in regulatory limbo for sixteen years, bounced between three agencies. A rival bacterial method finally won full registration in twenty twenty four. The lesson is that the technology exists, and the bottleneck is review capacity. Via Works in Progress.
