# Byte-sized — Friday, 11 September

Good morning. It's Friday the eleventh of September, and today's episode has six sections for you.

Let's start in Japan and Asia-Pacific.

First, security in North Korea. The United Nations' atomic energy watchdog now believes Pyongyang has built a new uranium enrichment facility large enough to house up to twenty eight centrifuge cascades, based on a study of satellite imagery. That could add roughly three to four extra nuclear warheads a year to North Korea's arsenal. The country has barred international inspectors since two thousand and nine, so this assessment relies entirely on images from space. Via International Intrigue.

Next, a quieter sign of the same regional tension. Google Maps has started blurring images of South Korean military sites and the demilitarised zone, the price Google reportedly paid for finally getting access to Seoul's high resolution mapping data. With an increasingly autocratic North Korea expanding its own satellite fleet, some analysts think the blurring is close to symbolic at this point. Via International Intrigue.

Then, a study in hedging your bets. Vietnam's president To Lam wrapped up a Moscow meeting with Vladimir Putin, signing deals covering everything from trade to energy, then landed in Paris the very next day for France's new International Space Summit. It's classic Vietnamese balancing diplomacy: Hanoi still needs Russian spare parts for its military hardware, but it has spent years quietly diversifying its friendships, and posing with President Macron a day after Moscow is the receipt. Via International Intrigue.

And finally, CSIS previewed an upcoming report on European Union and Southeast Asia coordination under the current Trump administration. The podcast conversation, between CSIS's Greg Poling and analyst Max Bergmann, frames the question as whether Brussels and regional capitals can build a closer working relationship on trade and security precisely as Washington's own long term commitments to the region are being questioned by allies and rivals alike. Via CSIS.

Now, over to AI and tech.

Anthropic pushed further into the enterprise this week with Claude for Microsoft 365, folding Claude directly into Excel, PowerPoint, Word and Outlook with shared context across all four. Claude for Excel, PowerPoint and Word is now generally available on paid plans, while the Outlook version is still in beta. The pitch is reviewable edits and compatibility with a company's existing compliance and cloud setup, rather than a bolt-on chatbot. Via TLDR.

Separately, a team of Anthropic economists published a framework modelling how artificial intelligence could reshape the American economy between now and twenty thirty. Under a modest scenario, AI adds under half a percentage point to yearly growth. Under an extreme scenario, AI is doing nearly half of today's cognitive work within four years, growth rockets to fifteen percent a year, the share of income going to labour falls from sixty to forty five percent, and one in five cognitive workers is out of a job. When the same economists surveyed ordinary Americans about their own expectations, the median answer landed roughly in the middle of those extremes. Via Marginal Revolution.

Apple, meanwhile, held its autumn hardware event, unveiling its first foldable handset, the nineteen ninety nine dollar iPhone Duo, alongside the iPhone eighteen Pro line and a new A20 Pro chip. Siri's long delayed AI overhaul will also launch, but only in beta, with daily usage caps and a paid tier promised down the line to manage server demand. Via TLDR.

And in the foundation model race, Chinese lab DeepSeek shipped V four point one Flash, a leaner, faster model built around an asymmetric architecture that trims memory costs while scaling toward larger systems. It's now live on DeepSeek's API with native multimodal support, and it quietly retires the previous Flash generation, the latest sign that the gap between Chinese and Western frontier labs keeps narrowing rather than widening. Via TLDR.

Now, over to startups and venture.

Corporate card provider Ramp is reportedly in talks for a new funding round at a sixty billion dollar valuation, up from forty four billion just a few months ago and thirteen billion back in March of last year. The company says it has passed a billion dollars in annualised revenue, with transaction volume up more than a hundred and seventy percent year on year, as it leans on AI driven expense and procurement tools ahead of a possible public listing. Via TLDR.

Elsewhere in fintech, the industry's quiet trend of the year kept building: Chime agreed to buy its longtime banking partner Stride Bank for five hundred and ninety million dollars, bringing a national bank charter in house under a future Chime Bank and promising more than a hundred million dollars a year in savings from lower partner fees and cheaper funding. Rival Block, meanwhile, filed its own application with the banking regulator, the O C C, for a federal trust bank charter it's calling Builders Bank and Trust, aimed at custody for bitcoin and stablecoins. Both moves point the same direction, fintechs increasingly want to own their banking infrastructure rather than rent it. Via TLDR.

And in Europe, Sifted mapped out the continent's most active growth stage investors, with a new twist: the European Union's own five billion euro Scaleup Europe fund has become one of the most prolific cheque writers of the year, backing companies from Finnish space tech to French artificial intelligence. It is a sign that public money is now playing a bigger role in plugging Europe's long standing late stage funding gap. Via Sifted.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

The International Monetary Fund quietly dropped its leading candidate for chief economist at the eleventh hour, after it emerged that the London School of Economics professor Ricardo Reis had previously made critical remarks about the Trump administration's trade tariffs. Reis is a highly regarded academic, and the fund had been preparing to announce him over the summer before it suddenly backtracked. It instead appointed another well regarded L S E economist, former Bank of England policymaker Silvana Tenreyro, who started the role last month. It's a small but telling sign of how far political sensitivities now reach into supposedly technocratic institutions. Via Marginal Revolution.

In Brussels, European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen unveiled a proposed Public Procurement Act designed to nudge national governments toward buying more European made goods and services. The measure could mobilise something like six hundred billion euros a year in public spending, and crucially it would apply directly across the bloc rather than needing to be rewritten by twenty seven separate parliaments, though defence procurement is carved out entirely. Some of the detail, an emphasis on quality and local content, also looks aimed at curbing the lowest price wins contracts that have often favoured Chinese suppliers, though the act grants permission to buy European rather than mandating it outright. Via International Intrigue.

And in Nigeria, CSIS's Into Africa podcast dug into why trust in the country's electoral institutions keeps thinning ahead of the twenty twenty seven vote. Host Oge Onubogu spoke with Samson Itodo, executive director of the civil society group YIAGA Africa, about a governance deficit playing out against a backdrop of declining international funding for democracy programmes across the continent, with young voters increasingly disillusioned even as civil society keeps pushing for more accountable governance. It's a reminder that Nigeria's democratic credibility test in twenty twenty seven will be watched well beyond its own borders. Via CSIS.

Now, over to community, events and marketing.

Marketing Brew flagged a curious trend: consumer brands are now building entire campaigns around sorority recruitment season, treating rush week like a captive, highly engaged audience worth designing products and activations around. One idea worth stealing, if your audience already gathers around a tight seasonal ritual, build the campaign to that ritual's calendar rather than your own. Via Marketing Brew.

And venture firm a sixteen z's speedrun newsletter shared some pointed advice from its general manager Josh Lu, who argues founders too easily outsource growth entirely to whatever algorithm is fashionable. He tells an old story from his own growth hacking days: staring hard at exactly how Facebook throttled friend invites for new apps, then quietly building tools to maximise acceptance rates so a fledgling app could send far more invites than its rivals. The takeaway isn't the specific hack, it's the discipline, study the mechanics of the platform you're distributing through, rather than assuming the algorithm will do the work for you. Via a sixteen z's speedrun newsletter.

And finally, one interesting thing.

Long before social media, a slow, memory starved home computer called the Commodore sixty four quietly gave birth to the first virtual world. In nineteen eighty six, a dial up service called Quantum Link launched a game called Habitat, where players got their own currency, customised avatars, held real estate, and even accidentally quintupled the game's money supply by exploiting a pricing bug between two in game vending machines. Its creators later said the experience taught them that anonymous crowds will do everything possible, from the wonderful to the horrible, given the freedom to do so. Long before Facebook or Fortnite, Habitat had already met every one of the internet's oldest problems. Via Works in Progress.
