Good morning, and welcome to Byte-sized for Thursday, the fifteenth of October. Six sections today, and we begin, as ever, in Japan and the Asia Pacific.

Section one, Japan and Asia Pacific.

First, a Japanese journalist detained in Russia. Russian authorities have arrested Yuki Imai, a reporter for the Hokkaido Shimbun, accusing him of gathering information on Russian military activity in the Far East. The timing is delicate. President Putin recently angered Tokyo by visiting the contested Kuril Islands, and Moscow has a record of holding foreign journalists as bargaining chips. Expect this to become a diplomatic test for the Japanese government. Via International Intrigue.

Second, a French defence firm comes to Japan. Thales is set to become the first foreign defence company to open a dedicated military maintenance base in the country, starting with servicing naval sensors and radars, and transferring technology to build heavy mortar systems locally from twenty twenty seven. Tokyo has traditionally kept such work for domestic champions like Mitsubishi and Kawasaki. The reading is that Japan's historic defence spending increase is now running into supply chain limits, which opens the door to foreign partners. Via International Intrigue.

Third, South Korea's Arctic shortcut. A Korean container ship has just completed a round trip to Europe via the Arctic, returning to Busan ten to fourteen days faster than the usual Suez route, with about thirty five percent less distance. It is a hedge against fragile chokepoints like Hormuz, the Red Sea and Panama. But the route runs through Russian controlled waters, which raises sanctions questions in Western capitals. For Japan, which is just as dependent on sea lanes and just as exposed to those chokepoints, expect the same debate to arrive at home. Via International Intrigue.

Fourth, one for your diary. The CSIS and Nikkei Symposium takes place in Tokyo on Friday the twenty third of October, on the future of the Indo-Pacific order. Japan's defence minister, Shinjiro Koizumi, gives a special address, and sessions cover the United States, China and Japan triangle and regional views on trade and security. In person registration closes today, Japan time, and online viewing is open until midday on the day. Via CSIS.

Now, over to artificial intelligence and technology.

Section two, AI and tech.

First, the story every AI newsletter is talking about. Federal prosecutors have charged OpenAI engineer Abdulrahman Alfozan, a Saudi national, with hiding a listening device behind a television in an executive meeting room at the San Francisco office. OpenAI's security team found it on the thirtieth of September, and the company says its own models helped analyse the device's storage and trace it to him. OpenAI says no model weights, source code or customer data were accessed. Prosecutors haven't linked the case to any foreign government, but the United States Attorney called it a matter of national security. The lesson for anyone building in this space: the insider is as big a risk as the model. Via The Rundown and TLDR.

Second, chips. Microsoft says that from twenty twenty seven it will deploy its own Maia accelerators at multi gigawatt scale, making Maia the main inference system for its own products. The next generation uses TSMC's latest process and targets long, multi step agent workloads. This is the pattern to watch: every big cloud is reducing its dependence on a single chip supplier. Via TLDR.

Third, how close is self improving AI? Anthropic co founder Jack Clark has moved his estimate for fully automated AI research forward by a year, to twenty twenty seven, citing a new test of how good models are at research judgement. In that test, Anthropic's latest model beat a panel of human experts at roughly one thirtieth of the cost per attempt. Anthropic engineers also say coding is no longer their bottleneck, with daily code merges up more than tenfold since May. Whatever you think of the forecast, the direction of travel is clear. Via The Rundown.

Fourth, Google has opened general access to Gemini 4 Argon, in the Gemini app, in search's AI Mode and on Google Cloud, priced at two dollars and ten dollars per million tokens for input and output. Enterprise buyers now have another frontier option to compare. Via The Rundown and TLDR.

Now, over to startups and venture.

Section three, startups and venture.

First, a cautionary tale from the consumer AI boom. Character dot AI has filed for Chapter Eleven bankruptcy to run a court supervised sale. It was once valued at around two and a half billion dollars, and at its peak was answering twenty thousand messages a second. Disney, which once threatened it with legal action, has put in the opening bid of twenty million dollars for a licence to some of the technology. If no buyer appears by early December, the service will probably shut. Safety lawsuits and a loss of direction did the damage, a reminder that attention is not a business model. Via The Rundown and TLDR.

Second, a sales playbook worth studying. The VC Corner breaks down how Rippling, valued at about seventeen billion dollars, grew its pipeline. When it swapped email sequencing software for people working the very same accounts, conversion rose from about one percent to between three and seven percent. Roughly half of the thirteen hundred meetings booked each month are still arranged by phone, and outbound now drives more than half of revenue. The honest caveat is that the maths only works because of the company's many products and high lifetime value, so a one product firm selling a small contract should not copy the headcount. Copy the habits instead: fix conversion before buying more lists, and be specific about who you refuse to call. Via The VC Corner.

Third, travel tech. Booking Holdings has launched Vayo, an AI travel agent that organises bookings from a traveller's inbox and watches trips for savings and disruption, including reservations made outside Booking's own brands. The company plans to invest a hundred million dollars. The signal is that the big platforms now want to own the assistant layer, not just the inventory. Via PhocusWire.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

Section four, policy and geopolitics.

First, China has frozen assets of, and banned visas for, cabinet ministers from Paraguay and Belize over their diplomatic ties with Taiwan. The measures are mostly symbolic, since few of those ministers hold Chinese assets, but they raise the personal cost of supporting Taipei, and Paraguay's agricultural lobby is already pushing its government to switch recognition for better access to the Chinese market. Taiwan has twelve formal partners left. Via International Intrigue.

Second, a global warning on AI safety. Geoffrey Hinton and Yoshua Bengio, with more than four hundred other scientists, have signed an open letter calling for an international body to log what they call containment failures, and asking labs to keep models capable of hacking offline by default. Governments have so far preferred voluntary commitments, so watch whether this nudges anyone towards a formal incident reporting regime. Via The Rundown.

Third, stablecoins as geopolitics. Tomas Pueyo argues that stablecoins, now worth over three hundred billion dollars, behave like silver to Bitcoin's gold: stable enough to be useful as money. If dollar stablecoins spread worldwide, Washington could borrow more cheaply, but the dollar's reach would grow and the leverage of the SWIFT payments network would shrink, and smaller countries would find their own monetary policy weaker. It is a useful frame for any conversation with government or finance people. Via Uncharted Territories.

Now, over to community, events and marketing.

Section five, one idea worth stealing.

Sifted interviews Sophie Rogers, who landed a community manager job at Lovable. Her first attempt was to wait outside the company's Stockholm office with a box of cakes bearing the message that building communities requires sweet ingredients. It didn't work. So she started a video series, Builders Brew, where she walks around Stockholm drinking tea with founders, grew an audience, and applied again. The idea worth stealing: don't send the application, build the proof. A small recurring series that features the people you want to work with is a better calling card than a cover letter, and it works just as well for community builders as for job hunters.

And a second, short one from Skift. Travel brands such as Hostelworld, Timeleft and WeRoad now charge for social access, as more of everyday social life has to be deliberately arranged. Solo travellers often lead group trips. If you run events, the takeaway is that connection itself is the product, and people will pay for someone else to organise it. Via Skift.

Now, one last thing.

Section six, one interesting thing. Adam Mastroianni of Experimental History takes on the fear that AI will take all the jobs. He points to what economists call the lump of labour fallacy, the mistaken idea that there is a fixed list of work to be done. If that were true, unemployment should have climbed ever since the Industrial Revolution, yet it is about where it was eighty years ago. His explanation is that human wants are endless: give a mouse a cookie and it asks for milk, then a straw. We cannot imagine the new desires we will have once the current ones are met. And the bank teller is a nice example: it was not the cash machine that disrupted the job but the smartphone, and there are still over three hundred and twenty nine thousand tellers in the United States. Via Experimental History.
