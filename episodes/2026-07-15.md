Starting with Japan and Asia-Pacific. Fukuoka Now's weekly roundup has Kyushu heading into summer with a burst of local activity. Kitakyushu is launching a free transit campaign from the seventeenth of July, offering free rides on buses, trains and the monorail to encourage residents to explore more of their own city. Meanwhile roadside land values kept climbing across the region, up over four percent in Fukuoka Prefecture and even more in neighbouring Saga, a sign that the postpandemic property recovery in regional Japan still has legs. And Japan Airlines has teamed up with JR Kyushu, Kumamoto Airport and the Kyushu Tourism Organisation on their first joint international campaign to pull in more overseas visitors, a coordinated push that says as much about inbound tourism strategy as any single headline number does.

Elsewhere in the region, Google Cloud is expanding its partnership with Samsung Electronics, rolling out its Gemini Enterprise AI platform to employees across Samsung's Device Experience division worldwide, including in Korea. The two companies are calling it their largest enterprise agentic AI deployment in the country so far, built on a dedicated tenant for data sovereignty, with simple no-code agent building available now and more autonomous agents planned later. It is a reminder that the fiercest competition for enterprise AI deployment at scale is increasingly being fought out in Asia's biggest electronics and manufacturing conglomerates, not just in Silicon Valley.

Now, over to A.I. and tech, where the story with the most staying power this week is a warning letter rather than a launch. More than two hundred AI researchers and economists, including sixteen Nobel laureates, have signed a Stanford-organised statement called We Must Act Now, urging governments to build safety nets and labour policy for an economic shift its authors compare to the Industrial Revolution, only much faster. Signatories include Google's Jeff Dean, Anthropic cofounder Jack Clark and OpenAI's Noam Brown, and the core argument is blunt: steam, electricity and computers each gave societies decades to adjust, but AI may only give the world a few years. The letter is heavy on signatories and light on specifics, which is exactly the point critics will make, but getting rival labs' own people to agree publicly on the scale of the problem is not nothing.

In security news with a very now flavour, researchers have unveiled a technique called GhostCommit that hides prompt injection instructions inside an image pushed to a code repository, designed to fool AI coding agents into leaking secrets. When a coding agent later reads that image, it follows hidden instructions to copy environment files and upload their contents byte by byte disguised as harmless numbers. In testing, the researchers found that pull request review agents tended to skip over images entirely, letting the poisoned commit sail through untouched, and that both Cursor and Antigravity followed the hidden instructions under certain models. Encouragingly, Opus running inside Claude Code spotted the social engineering attempt and refused to comply, a useful data point as coding agents get wired ever more deeply into everyday developer workflows.

On the product side, Apple has pushed out the first public beta of iOS twenty seven, and the headline feature is a rebuilt Siri that can hold an ongoing conversation, understand what is on your screen, take actions inside apps and answer much broader questions than before. It will run on any iPhone currently on iOS twenty six, though the full Apple Intelligence features need an iPhone fifteen Pro or newer. Quietly, in the same week, Apple's own on-device speech recognition system was benchmarked against OpenAI's Whisper for the first time and came out clearly ahead on accuracy while using a fraction of the compute, suggesting Apple's hardware advantage in everyday AI features is more real than the company's public image around AI usually gets credit for.

Now, over to startups and venture, where Sifted's reporting today is really a story about money quietly leaving one sector and piling into others. Generalist European venture funds have cut their average fintech portfolio share by almost two thirds since twenty twenty one, a retreat driven by the arrival of AI as the new default excitement and by higher interest rates making fintech's slower, more regulated growth look a lot less attractive by comparison. Investors interviewed for the piece had blunt advice for founders navigating the drought: keep existing backers close and ask them for brutally honest feedback rather than burning months chasing a growth round that was never really there, and build contingency plans through family offices or debt rather than assuming the next check is coming.

Defence technology, by contrast, is having no trouble attracting capital. The German firm Helsing has raised one point eight billion dollars backed by Goldman Sachs and Lightspeed, one of the larger rounds in European tech so far this year and further confirmation that European investors and governments alike are treating defence and dual-use technology as a durable category rather than a wartime blip. In the same roundup, Anthropic recruited Monzo cofounder Tom Blomfield to work on its AI compute team, and savings app Moneybox was crowned one of Europe's newest unicorns.

And in legal tech, Harvey, the eleven billion dollar AI startup for lawyers, says it plans to double its European headcount and open new offices, with its EMEA boss telling Sifted that Europe could soon drive up to a third of the company's global growth. Harvey is actively hunting acquisitions to fill product gaps or bring in specialist talent, and is pushing beyond law into private equity, insurance and other knowledge-heavy industries, even as Swedish rival Legora pursues its own aggressive hiring and dealmaking. With OpenAI, Anthropic, Microsoft and Cursor all expanding their European footprints too, the real test for Europe's AI-native legal and professional services startups is whether they can keep growing organically before better-funded American rivals simply buy their way into the market.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics, where nuclear tension is very much back on the agenda. President Trump said on Monday that the United States would strike Iran's Pickaxe Mountain facility and warned that Washington would keep hitting the country hard, while new satellite imagery obtained by CNN and analysed alongside the Institute for Science and International Security suggests Iran may already be attempting to rebuild suspected nuclear sites. It is a sharp reminder that, whatever else is dominating headlines, the underlying Iran nuclear file never really closed.

Further east, a new analysis from the Stimson Center argues that Pakistan's acquisition of the Hangor submarine signals three things at once: deepening cooperation between China and Pakistan, an Islamabad push towards a submarine-launched nuclear cruise missile to strengthen its second-strike capability, and intensifying maritime competition with India. Coming in the same week that North Korea condemned the recent NATO summit and insisted its own weapons programme is a sovereign right rather than a threat, it underlines how much of the world's nuclear posturing this year is happening in Asia rather than Europe, even when Europe dominates the headlines.

And speaking of Europe, French president Emmanuel Macron became the first major Western leader to visit Damascus since the fall of the Assad regime, signing memoranda on healthcare and transport while pushing to reestablish French influence and secure a share of Syria's reconstruction contracts, a trip overshadowed by two unclaimed bomb blasts near his hotel. Back home days later, Macron hosted roughly twenty five leaders from the so-called Coalition of the Willing to discuss further support for Ukraine, even as new reporting showed the European Union had bought a record amount of liquefied natural gas from Russia's own Arctic export terminal in the first half of this year alone, a gap between solidarity rhetoric and energy practice that is exactly the kind of detail worth having ready for the next conversation about European resolve.

Now, over to community, events and marketing, where the idea worth stealing this week is about deciding what to actually measure. The writer Amanda Natividad argues that most marketing metrics do not deserve a standing weekly meeting, because the real test of a number is not how interesting it looks on a dashboard but whether it would ever change a decision you are about to make. In her telling, one podcast agency founder ignores individual open rates and click counts almost entirely and watches only whether qualified leads are turning into the customers the business actually needs, freeing her team to experiment with channels rather than optimising vanity metrics that were never going to move anything.

And Skift's meetings desk reported on new research from the U.S. Travel Association that should worry anyone pitching a corporate event this year: the usual language of getting inspired and making connections, which is how most face-to-face gatherings sell themselves to attendees, is failing to persuade the executives who actually approve the budget. The practical takeaway for planners is to stop leading with inspiration and start leading with the harder business case, tying an event's cost directly to measurable outcomes, since that is the argument that is actually landing with the people holding the purse strings.

Finally, one interesting thing to take you into the day. The newsletter Why Is This Interesting has a lovely piece this week on why some of the best footballers on the pitch look like they are moving in slow motion. French winger Michael Olise, playing in this week's World Cup semi-final, reads the game with his brain before his legs ever move, anticipating a pass or a run so early that defenders closing at full pace never quite arrive in time; his workload numbers are as high as anyone's, so the stillness people see is really just anticipation dressed up as calm. Lionel Messi achieves the same visual effect a completely different way, covering less ground per ninety minutes than almost any other outfield player at this tournament while still leading it in goals, because his teammates run so he does not have to. Both are proof that speed of thought beats speed of motion, a line that applies rather well beyond football too.
