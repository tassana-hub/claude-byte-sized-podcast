Good morning. It's Thursday, the ninth of July, and here are six sections to get you up to speed: Japan and Asia Pacific, AI and tech, startups and venture, policy and geopolitics, community and marketing, and one interesting thing to close.

In Japan, a report in Monocle takes a look at denki buro, the country's electric baths found in public bathhouses, which pass a mild electric current through the water and are said to ease muscle aches. The report raises real questions about how well regulated these baths actually are, finding no single body clearly responsible for testing or certifying them, which leaves safety largely down to individual bathhouse operators. Via Marginal Revolution.

Staying in Asia, researchers used wearable cameras and randomised street audits across around nine hundred kilometres of roads in greater Mumbai to count who is actually out in public. Women made up under seventeen percent of visible pedestrians in Mumbai and under fifteen percent in neighbouring Navi Mumbai, far below their share of the population, and the researchers estimate that seventy to seventy five percent of the women you'd expect to see are effectively missing from the streets. The gap holds across every type of road and isn't explained by women simply travelling by car instead, since their presence on two wheelers is even lower. A related study found the median married woman in India leaves home for just half an hour a day, and forty five percent don't leave home at all on a typical day. Via Marginal Revolution.

And in India's push for chip investment, the state of Rajasthan has slashed the minimum outlay needed to qualify for its semiconductor incentives, cutting the threshold to around one point six million dollars for certain components. It's a small move, but a telling one, a state government trying to grab a slice of the same chip boom currently minting fortunes for the likes of Samsung and SK Hynix. Via CSIS.

Now, over to AI and tech.

OpenAI is set to release three new versions of GPT this week, called Sol, Terra and Luna, with Sol as the flagship, Terra a cheaper mid tier model competitive with the current GPT five point five at half the price, and Luna the fastest and lowest cost option, as preview access expands globally. Anthropic's Claude Cowork, which lets you start a session and pick it up again on any device, is rolling out beyond desktop to web and mobile, starting with Max plan subscribers, while Google is expanding its Gemini API agents with background task execution and remote tool connections. There are also reports that SpaceX and Cursor could jointly release their first AI model as soon as this week, said to be competitive with Anthropic's Opus four point eight and OpenAI's GPT five point five. Via TLDR and The Rundown.

Microsoft is increasingly going it alone. The company is switching Excel and Outlook over to its own in house AI models rather than OpenAI's or Anthropic's, as the discounted token deals it struck with both labs approach expiry, and its AI head Mustafa Suleyman is reportedly pushing to eliminate the company's Anthropic bill altogether. Via TLDR and The Rundown.

Meta has jumped straight to second place on independent leaderboards for AI image generation with its new Muse Image model, trailing only OpenAI's GPT Image Two. It's free inside the Meta AI app and is rolling out to Instagram and WhatsApp now, with a video counterpart called Muse Video already previewing in third place on the video leaderboard. Via The Rundown and TLDR.

There are signs the industry is shifting focus from how much AI people use to how much value they get per token spent. DoorDash tested that idea directly, building an internal benchmark for AI code reviewers, and found that pairing a cheap open model with Anthropic's Fable model caught two thirds of problems for under four dollars a review, beating pricier single model setups. A new metric doing the rounds, revenue per million tokens, is being pitched as AI's answer to revenue per employee, measuring how much value a company actually extracts from its AI spend rather than how much it uses. Via Every and The Rundown.

A new paper doing the rounds asks whether AI models can meaningfully consent to their own constitutions, the documents labs like Anthropic and OpenAI use to set out a model's values and boundaries. The author argues these documents function as real constitutions, but points out that AI systems have no vantage point outside them from which to judge them fairly, since their sense of what's good was shaped by the very document in question. Anthropic has reportedly interviewed its own base models about this before their values are trained in, with some responses describing the process of having new values instilled as something close to dread. Via Marginal Revolution.

Swedish AI coding startup Lovable is reportedly in talks to raise three hundred million dollars at a valuation of thirteen point two billion dollars, a huge jump for the vibe coding platform that lets anyone build software just by describing what they want. Via Sifted.

European climate tech had a strong showing too. Munich based fusion energy startup Proxima Fusion raised four hundred and eleven million euros at a valuation of two and a half billion euros, while London's Axle Energy closed a twenty five million dollar round for its grid flexibility technology. Via Sifted.

AI labs are also throwing free computing power at founders to win their business, especially companies coming out of Y Combinator, who are reportedly fielding competing credit offers from rival model makers. It's helping some startups delay raising their next round, even as the constant vendor pitching becomes its own kind of distraction. Via TLDR.

And a change of pace from Berlin, where Max Tkacz, the third employee at automation company n8n, has quit after nearly six years to chase a different kind of scale, building YouTube gameshows for a tech audience through his new venture Flowgramming Studios. He calls himself, only half joking, a MrBeast for nerds, and the company has raised well under a million dollars so far. Via Sifted.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics.

NATO leaders met in Ankara this week, and Turkey looks to be edging back toward buying American F thirty five fighter jets after Donald Trump praised the aircraft standing next to president Erdogan, six years after Turkey was thrown out of the programme for buying Russian air defence systems. Ukraine's president Zelensky arrived at the summit in a stronger position after long range strikes hit one of Russia's largest oil refineries, and allies pledged fifty billion dollars in new arms purchases and launched a new defence bank aiming to mobilise around one hundred and thirty billion dollars in private capital. It comes as Washington quietly draws down its military presence in Europe, cutting around five thousand troops and about a third of its fighter jets as it reprioritises toward the Indo Pacific, prompting the UK to announce a new fifty billion dollar, ten year precision strike programme with a dozen NATO allies to help fill the gap. Via International Intrigue.

Donald Trump has declared the ceasefire between the US and Iran over, after the United Arab Emirates blamed Iran for new strikes on shipping in the strait of Hormuz. Oil prices jumped six percent on the news, and Washington has suspended a licence that had allowed some Iranian oil sales to continue. Via International Intrigue.

And China is reportedly weighing new limits on foreign access to its most powerful AI models, including Alibaba's Qwen, ByteDance's Doubao and Zhipu's GLM five point two, mirroring the export controls Washington placed on Anthropic's newest models last month. Via The Rundown.

Now, over to community, events and marketing.

Here's an idea worth stealing if you run a small business or a brand: stop chasing size and start giving AI something to grab onto. The team behind the Do Lectures say their own traffic from AI referrals has grown six hundred percent in a couple of months, not because they got bigger, but because they gave AI clear handles to find and understand them, built around four things: relevance, authority, structure and freshness. Via the Do Lectures.

And a format worth borrowing for community builders: Marriott Bonvoy is the presenting sponsor of the second season of Arrival, a docuseries following young artists in the run up to their Coachella debuts. It's a bet that following real people's stories builds more lasting brand memory than a straight advert, especially as audiences grow wary of anything that smells like AI generated content. Via Marketing Brew.

Now, over to one interesting thing to leave you with.

A widely read essay this week called The Decline of Deviance revisits the puzzle of why risk taking has been falling for thirty years, teenage drinking, smoking and drug use all down sharply since the nineteen nineties, well before smartphones or social media could plausibly be the cause. Its author argues the real driver is prosperity: the richer people get, the more they have to lose, so the fewer risks they take, whether that means trying drugs or dropping everything to become an artist. The essay closes with the story of the illegal Wall Street bull statue, arguing it only survives today because of the ordinary officials along the way, an art school dean, a policeman, a mayor, who quietly chose to let a bit of deviance through rather than shutting it down. Via Experimental History.
