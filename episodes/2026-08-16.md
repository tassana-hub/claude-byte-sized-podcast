Japan and Asia-Pacific this week was a story of quiet domestic resilience running alongside mounting pressure from China across the wider region.

First up, Fukuoka's preparedness moment. Fukuoka is formally pushing to become one of Japan's official backup capital cities, a status created by new national legislation designed to keep government functions running if a major disaster strikes Tokyo. The push landed just days after a University of Tokyo seismologist warned locals that an unruptured section of the nearby Kego fault zone should be treated as a serious, live risk rather than a distant one, which gives the backup capital bid a rather pointed local backdrop. In the same week, the wider Kyushu and Okinawa region's population slipped below fourteen million for the first time on record, even as its foreign resident numbers kept climbing, a demographic split that's becoming familiar across regional Japan. And Sony and TSMC pressed ahead with a roughly seven hundred and forty seven billion yen joint sensor plant in Kumamoto, a reminder that the chip investment boom in Kyushu hasn't slowed even as the local population does. Via Fukuoka Now.

Next, a case for deeper US-Japan collaboration on culture, not just chips or defence. A new paper from the CSIS Japan Chair argues that Japan and the United States should combine their complementary strengths, Japan's globally competitive intellectual property and America's dominant digital platforms, to expand their reach across the international creative and entertainment industries. The paper points out that creative industries are already a meaningful source of American economic growth and export earnings, and that Japan is now treating its own creative sector as a strategic pillar of long-term competitiveness, which makes the two countries unusually well matched. The authors frame this as more than a commercial opportunity: as competition for global audiences intensifies, closer collaboration on content could help both countries shape the wider information environment in a positive direction, while deepening people to people ties across the Indo-Pacific over the long term. Via CSIS.

Then, a small diplomatic needle. Tokyo lodged a formal complaint after Vladimir Putin made his first ever visit to the Kuril Islands, a chain the Soviets seized from Japan at the end of the Second World War and which Tokyo still partly claims, a dispute that is one reason the two countries never signed a formal postwar peace treaty. The timing looks pointed: the trip came just after Japan's government published a new defence white paper warning about growing cooperation between Russia, China and North Korea, making the visit read as a deliberate jab timed to Tokyo's own security messaging. Via International Intrigue.

And finally, a warning that the region's biggest security story isn't really about the sea at all. A detailed CSIS analysis lays out how China is squeezing the Philippines through eight interlocking levers, from disinformation and elite capture to cyber operations and coast guard coercion, arguing that the headline-grabbing standoffs in the South China Sea are really just the tip of a much broader campaign. The report notes that Filipino public opinion still favours Washington over Beijing by a wide margin, but warns that this advantage could prove more fragile than it looks from Washington if recent policy missteps and energy shocks keep chipping away at it. And the author says this is a playbook China is already extending elsewhere in the region, pointing to a recent Chinese ballistic missile test fired into the Pacific and intensified pressure on both Japan and New Zealand in just the past few weeks, which makes the Philippines less an isolated dispute and more an early warning for everyone else nearby. Via CSIS.

Now, over to AI and tech, where the headlines this week were about how fast frontier models are moving, and who is quietly paying for the compute behind them.

xAI released Grok four point six, a model tuned for long running agents, coding and knowledge work, and it immediately jumped into the top tier of the field. The new model ties GPT five point six Sol Max for third place on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index, overtaking Kimi K3 in the process, while undercutting GPT five point six sharply on price, at two dollars per million input tokens and six dollars per million output tokens, less than half of what OpenAI charges in standard mode. It's another sign that the gap at the very top of the leaderboard keeps narrowing, even as the price war underneath it keeps intensifying. Via TLDR.

Anthropic announced it will start quietly embedding an invisible watermark into text generated by Claude, though it hasn't disclosed exactly how the method works. Researchers who have looked into it believe the marker lives in a subtle statistical bias in word choice, one robust enough to survive ordinary copying and pasting, though simple countermeasures like paraphrasing or a full rewrite could likely strip it back out again. Via TLDR.

And Nvidia made a bold attempt to turn its chips into a new kind of financial asset. The company unveiled financing platforms built with Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs and KKR, aiming to raise more than five hundred billion dollars from outside investors to fund AI data centres, with chief executive Jensen Huang pitching compute itself as a durable, resaleable, investable asset class. Sceptics were quick to point out that these are non-binding agreements rather than signed deals, and that the entire pitch rests on unproven resale value, since Nvidia's own evidence for durability, rising rental rates on older chips, really only proves current earnings, not what the hardware would actually fetch if a customer walked away. Via TLDR.

And away from the frontier labs, a quieter story about who is actually training the robots of the future. Tens of thousands of workers in India are being recruited to film themselves doing ordinary, mundane tasks in first person, footage that is then fed into AI systems designed to teach robots how to physically interact with the world. It's a reminder that even as the headlines focus on chatbots and coding agents, the harder unsolved problem in robotics is simply a shortage of real footage of humans doing everyday things, because until very recently nobody thought that footage was worth collecting at all. Via TLDR.

Now, over to startups and venture, where the story of the week was scale: more new unicorns in six months than in the whole of last year, and mega rounds chasing whoever looks like the next frontier lab.

The world minted one hundred and ninety five new unicorns in the first half of twenty twenty six alone, according to Crunchbase data, already surpassing the entire total for twenty twenty five, with robotics and AI leading the surge. China's DeepSeek and Seychelles based crypto exchange OKX both crossed major valuation milestones during the period, and the United States and China dominated overall, with fast follow funding rounds pushing valuations up rapidly across both AI and semiconductor startups. Via TLDR.

In Europe, Index Ventures is reportedly in talks to lead a five hundred million dollar round for a brand new AI lab founded by a former DeepMind researcher, one of several outsized raises reported this week. In the same stretch, Spanish AI model compression startup Multiverse Computing was said to be pushing for a two billion euro valuation on the back of technology that claims to shrink large AI models by up to ninety five percent with little loss in accuracy, a pitch that's already landed it more than a hundred customers including Bosch and the Bank of Canada. Drone maker Uforce, meanwhile, was reportedly targeting a valuation upwards of four billion dollars in its next round, and coding assistant Lovable closed a four hundred million dollar round backed partly by the European Union's own Scaleup fund, a deal that raised eyebrows given the fund was originally pitched as being for defence, space and quantum technology rather than a consumer coding tool. Via Sifted.

And in China, Moonshot, the maker of the Kimi K3 model, announced a three and a half billion dollar raise at a thirty five billion dollar valuation, with a Hong Kong listing reportedly under consideration. The timing raised eyebrows well beyond its own investors, since the round landed squarely in the middle of a US Treasury investigation into intellectual property theft allegations against Chinese open model makers, making it an unusually large vote of confidence delivered mid-scandal. It capped a week in which AI kept pushing into new corners of everyday life across Asia too, with reports of AI reaching into drug discovery, hospital records and even autonomous payments, a reminder that the contest between open and closed models is playing out well beyond the leaderboards. Via Speeda Uzabase.

Now, over to policy and geopolitics, where the week's conversation circled oil markets, an escalating trade fight, and Europe's newest AI rulebook starting to bite.

BlackRock's latest geopolitical risk dashboard, compiled with a former US national security advisor and two former central bankers, flagged four themes worth watching for the rest of the year: energy markets staying vulnerable to renewed price spikes even without outright shortages, an escalating trade fight between Europe and China over cheap Chinese exports, continued US economic and diplomatic pressure on Latin American governments it considers adversarial, and no meaningful US-China cooperation on frontier AI beyond a narrow new dialogue focused purely on safety practices. The single most striking number in the report: China's trade surplus with the European Union has now overtaken its surplus with the United States for the first time in thirty years, and Germany, once China's second largest buyer, has slipped all the way down to ninth, a shift that helps explain why Brussels and Beijing keep trading tit for tat probes, from electric vehicles to cognac to pork. Via International Intrigue.

Closer to the ground, the European Union's AI Act is starting to reshape how an entire consumer facing industry talks to its customers. The Act's requirements for clear labelling of AI generated content and transparency whenever a customer is interacting with an AI system are now filtering into travel, forcing airlines, hotels and booking platforms to rethink how they disclose AI use at the point of booking, one of the clearest early examples yet of the Act's transparency rules landing on an everyday consumer sector rather than staying confined to enterprise software. It lands the same week separate industry research found travellers broadly want AI to personalise their trips, but draw a hard line at unchecked autonomy, wanting to stay firmly in control of the final decision. Via PhocusWire.

And a quieter but arguably bigger story: economists are now arguing humanity has likely dropped below replacement level fertility for the first time in recorded history, a milestone that survived even wars and pandemics. A new paper highlighted on Marginal Revolution examined two hundred and thirty six countries going back to 1950 and found the decline is concentrated among poorer, less educated women in low and middle income countries rather than wealthy ones, with no single global driver behind it, and not one of two hundred and nineteen country-specific trends the researchers identified is currently levelling off. That leads the authors to a blunt conjecture: modernity itself makes a third child expensive and childlessness cheap, and because children come in whole numbers, it takes very little economic change to knock a country's fertility rate down and nothing much in a modern economy to push it back up again, which is why the paper closes on slow growth as the main long run economic consequence. Via Marginal Revolution.

Now, over to community, events and marketing, two ideas worth stealing this week, both about the harder, less glamorous side of bringing people together.

One idea worth stealing: treat group conflict as a skill, not a crisis to be avoided. Facilitator Priya Parker announced her second book, The Art of Fighting, arguing that most existing conflict advice is built for pairs, spouses, business partners, and leaves groups, teams, communities, families, with almost no real tools beyond pretending everything is fine one day and reaching for a lawsuit the next. She traces the idea back to 2020, when the same skills she'd built for designing joyful gatherings turned out to be exactly what clients needed most once the pandemic and a wave of institutional reckonings pushed group tension out into the open. Her practical suggestion for anyone running a team or a community: build small, deliberate rituals for surfacing tension early, the same kind of intentional design she brought to gatherings in her first book, rather than waiting for a full blow-up before anyone addresses it. Via Priya Parker.

The second idea worth stealing: you can build real community even in spaces you don't own. Community strategist Shyaamal Tripathi shared hard-won lessons from building community inside Discord servers, forums and events he did not control himself, drawing on experience from a student hackathon, a Web3 project and an enterprise software company. His core takeaway for anyone trying to grow a following on someone else's platform: you earn a seat at the table by consistently adding value on other people's turf first, long before you ever ask for anything back, since nobody owes a newcomer trust just because they showed up with something to promote. Via Community Inc.

And finally, one interesting thing to close out the week.

Does animal welfare follow the same curve as air pollution once did? A Works in Progress essay asks whether farm animal suffering will eventually shrink the way pollution and deforestation did as countries grew richer, the pattern economists call a Kuznets curve, named for the economist who first noticed inequality rises then falls as nations industrialise. The uncomfortable finding: some wealthy nations, Germany among them, are genuinely eating a fifth less meat than they used to, but the meat they do eat has shifted heavily from beef towards chicken and farmed fish, so per person they now cause roughly twice as many animals to suffer as they did forty years ago, not fewer, since a single serving of chicken implies vastly more days of a suffering animal's life than the same serving of beef. It's a reminder that getting richer doesn't automatically bend every curve the way it eventually bent the smog over London.
